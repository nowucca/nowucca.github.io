2026-11-23 · A roundup of four arXiv papers (12 to 18 Nov 2026) curated by Tom Anusic. Each has caveats the authors state, kept below.

Why these four belong together

Each paper finds a gap that existing measurements hide. Agent benchmarks that never needed vision, video models that "predict" without tracking state, agent learning studied in context or in weights but rarely both, and RL infrastructure tuned for big models while the workload shifts to small ones. For a staff engineer the takeaway is the same each time: check whether your eval or your stack actually exercises the property you care about.

1. MKA: professional agent work that needs vision

Singh, Yogin, Soricut, Alayrac et al., Google DeepMind with Artificial Analysis, arXiv:2611.25228, 18 Nov 2026.

  • What it is. 200 end-to-end tasks across 10 economic sectors. The agent has to pull information out of visually dense files (CAD blueprints, electronics schematics, radiographs, complex charts, geospatial maps) and deliver a standalone file: .xlsx, .pptx, .pdf, .docx, .html or .png.

  • Why it was needed. Taking image-viewing tools away from Gemini 4 Carbon leaves its GDPval score unchanged, so that benchmark doesn't test vision. On MKA, the same removal costs about 6 points on the hard criteria.

  • Grading. A sandboxed agentic autorater scores each deliverable against expert-weighted rubrics. It agreed with domain experts on 96.2% of criterion judgments (Cohen's κ 0.91). The headline metric is Climb-WSR: weighted satisfaction on criteria that prior-generation models don't saturate.

  • Results. Claude Fable 5.2 scores 56.6% Climb-WSR (73.3% overall) and Gemini 4 Carbon 54.3% (71.9%). The curator reports Claude Opus 5.5 at 53.0% and GPT-6 Astra at 50.2%, with the top three statistically tied. Carbon uses 5.7× fewer tokens than Fable. No model clears 57%, so the benchmark has headroom.

  • Caveats. A lab built it and evaluates its own model alongside others, each model ran once per task, and the grader is itself an agent.

Engineering read. If your agents handle documents with diagrams or scans, ablate the vision tools on your own eval. If the score doesn't move, your eval isn't measuring the part of the job that fails in production.

2. The Flip Test: video world models don't track world state

Romy Mi L., Lijun Yu, Xuezhi Wang, Yinxiao Liu et al., Google DeepMind, UT Austin and UC Berkeley, arXiv:2611.19822, 15 Nov 2026.

  • Test design. Pairs of near-identical first frames whose true futures differ, such as a box just before or just past a table edge. A model that tracks state should flip its prediction across the boundary. A three-level hierarchy (minimal pairs, matched controls, dense sweeps) runs over 1,680 frames from 10 simulated and 15 real tasks.

  • Result. All 11 video world models fail. The best averages 18.3% pair accuracy, below the 25% of random guessing, mostly by showing the same outcome for both frames. Models flip as often where the future doesn't change as where it does. A coding agent that simply measures the frame solves 93% of real pairs.

  • Scope. Rigid objects with a single tipping-point variable.

Engineering read. Photorealistic rollouts aren't evidence of a usable simulator. Before you use a video model to plan or to generate robot training data, test it on counterfactual pairs that differ in the one variable that matters.

3. Agents that learn on the job: in context, in weights, or both

Mirtaheri, Mirzadeh, Farajtabar et al., Apple with UC San Diego, arXiv:2611.19700, 15 Nov 2026.

  • Setup. Qwen3.5-9B works through streams of related tasks in one environment (SWE-bench Verified, AppWorld, ScienceWorld, BIRD-Critic SQLite: 109 tasks, five task orders each) and learns only from its own attempts. Each task is scored before the agent adapts to it. The study compares 14 mechanisms: in-context replay (ICL), online RL with verifiable rewards (GRPO), self-distillation (SDPO), and combinations.

  • Results. In-context replay is the biggest single lever, lifting mean success from 28.8% to 49.4%. Adding GRPO on top reaches 51.6% and helps under every context policy. Self-distillation is mixed.

  • Trace finding. ICL makes agents faster (fewer lookups, shorter attempts) but they often skip inspecting and verifying the current task. GRPO brings those checks back while keeping the reduced lookups.

  • Caveats. One model and 109 tasks.

Engineering read. Start with experience replay in context: it is cheap, reversible and gives most of the gain. Watch for the shortcut it creates, an agent that trusts remembered structure and stops checking. Weight updates are a smaller increment whose main value may be restoring verification habits.

4. LoCoJAX: small-model RL wastes TPU time on coordination

Loki C., Rehm, Firooz et al., Meta, arXiv:2611.16301, v3 18 Nov 2026.

  • Diagnosis. Sweeping dense models from 0.6B to 405B on TPU v7x under a decode-heavy GRPO workload, host orchestration takes 36% of step time at 0.6B, while network costs dominate for large models. A single-controller design (Pathways) suits big models and wastes small ones.

  • Fix. Run rollout and reward computation locally on each host, keeping training synchronous and globally sharded.

  • Results. Steps run 1.3 to 3.6× faster than a tuned Pathways baseline on Qwen3-0.6B, and on GSM8K 8 chips beat Pathways on 64.

  • Caveats. One model was rigorously tested, and the closed-source baseline was harder to tune.

Engineering read. As RL shifts to small specialised models, profile where the step time goes before you buy more chips. The bottleneck may be orchestration, not compute.

Cross-cutting failure modes

  • Evals that don't need the capability. MKA's vision ablation and the Flip Test's counterfactual pairs both show that a score can look fine while the capability is absent.

  • Agent-graded evaluation. MKA reports strong agreement with experts, but an agentic grader is another model with its own blind spots. Spot-check it on your own domain.

  • Shortcut learning from experience. Replay that removes verification steps can lift average success while making failures more confident.

  • Single-run, single-model results. Three of the four papers flag this. Treat the gaps as directional.

Review checklist

  • Have you ablated each capability your eval claims to test (vision, tool use, memory) and checked the score moves?

  • For any world or video model used in planning, do you have counterfactual minimal pairs?

  • If agents reuse past trajectories, do you measure whether they still inspect and verify the current task?

  • Are agent tasks scored before the agent adapts to them, so learning isn't counted as skill?

  • For RL on small models, do you know the share of step time spent on host orchestration versus compute?

  • Are lab-built benchmarks cross-checked with an independent run before you choose a model on them?

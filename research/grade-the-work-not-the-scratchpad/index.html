Grade the work, not the scratchpad: why on-policy distillation makes small agents ramble

Published 2026-11-21Summarized 2026-11-26

Read the original

distillationpost-trainingtoken-efficiencyreasoningllm-judgebenchmarks

A teacher that reads a student's unfinished thought teaches it to keep thinking

Distil a large agent into a small one with standard on-policy distillation and you can end up with a model that emits more tokens per episode than before you trained it — even when the teacher is more concise than the student. That is the finding, and the explanation turns out to be a specific, avoidable artefact of where the loss is applied.

The paper (arXiv:2611.28445, submitted 21 November 2026, authors from UC San Diego, Google and the University of Waterloo, and labelled by its own arXiv comment as ongoing work) names the fix Muted Distillation. It is one of those results whose value is mostly in the diagnosis.

The symptom

In on-policy distillation for an agent, the student rolls out a multi-step task and a larger teacher scores the student's own tokens. Those tokens include the student's reasoning block, not just the answers and tool calls it emits.

After training this way, the student's episodes get longer, and the growth is specifically in reasoning: it comes "from longer per-turn reasoning rather than more turns." The anatomy is worth quoting because it is so lopsided — padding tokens per turn grow "from 157 to 271 tokens per turn while informative tokens stay near 100," and the reasoning block ends up holding 79% of the response tokens. The model is not doing more work. It is deliberating more per unit of work.

The diagnosis, which is the actual contribution

Two mechanisms, both measured.

A teacher reading a partial thought extends it. "Once the teacher reads part of the student's thought, it tends to extend that thought rather than conclude it." The authors demonstrate it by varying how much of the thought the teacher sees: with a prefix from 0% to 75%, "the total thought length grows monotonically with the prefix fraction." More visible thought, more thought produced. And the lexical signature is exactly what you would expect from a model asked to continue someone else's half-finished deliberation: the teacher "roughly doubles its probability mass on reconsideration tokens such as 'wait', 'hmm', and 'actually'."

This is a subtle trap. The teacher is not verbose by nature — it is more concise than the student when answering on its own. The verbosity is induced by the position it is put in. A model shown a truncated chain of thought and asked what comes next will hedge and continue, because that is what the token distribution after an unfinished thought looks like. The student then learns to imitate a distribution that only exists because of how the supervision was constructed.

The gradient is dominated by tokens that carry no information. Padding tokens "carry almost no gradient," yet the thought's gradient norm is a median 5.5x the tail's. So the thought both dominates the training signal and contains most of the filler. Worse, scoring the thought makes the teacher's judgement of the part you care about mushier: per-token KL over the first eight tail tokens is "about 0.10 when the teacher reads the thought, against 0.35 when the thought is hidden." Reading the reasoning tells the teacher where the answer is going, so it stops discriminating between good and bad answers.

That second number is the one to remember. Showing the teacher the student's reasoning does not merely add noise — it actively blunts the supervision on the output.

The method

Muted Distillation "hides every thought from the teacher, replacing it with an empty thinking block, and applies the loss to the tail only." The student still generates its reasoning and still has that reasoning in its own context; only the teacher's view is changed, and the loss is computed on the visible answer and action tokens.

The asymmetry is the design. The student is allowed to think; it is simply not graded on its thinking. The teacher sees the problem and the student's visible output, with an empty block where the reasoning would be, and judges it the way a reviewer judges a pull request rather than the way a supervisor reads a diary.

It is also almost free: "MD takes a few lines of code and adds no extra data or hyperparameters."

Results, and what they do and do not establish

From the abstract: output tokens per episode fall by 57% and 53% against standard on-policy distillation on two knowledge-work benchmarks (OfficeQA Pro and SpreadsheetBench), while accuracy "matches OPD's accuracy on OfficeQA Pro" and improves pass@1 by 4.8 points on SpreadsheetBench. Students are a 4B, 9B and 27B model from one family; the teacher is a 397B sparse model from the same family.

So: roughly half the tokens, no accuracy cost, on these two tasks at this scale, from a change that adds no hyperparameters. That is a good result and an unusually cheap one.

Two cautions belong next to it.

The headline comparison is against a damaged baseline. Standard on-policy distillation here produces a model that rambles more than the untrained student. Halving the tokens of a method that inflated them is partly a repair, not purely a gain. The right question for a practitioner is not "how much better than OPD" but "how does this compare with the untrained student and with other concise-training approaches", and the abstract's two numbers do not answer that.

Scale behaviour is the open question. The accuracy claims in the abstract come from the smallest student. There are indications in the paper's later tables that at larger scales the picture is less uniform and may depend on checkpoint selection, which remains an open question rather than an established finding. Anyone choosing between the two methods at 20B-plus should read the results tables themselves rather than the abstract.

No code or data release is stated. The paper is CC BY 4.0 and self-described as ongoing work.

Why this generalises past distillation

The transferable lesson is about what you let a grader see, and it applies to any setup where one model scores another's work.

  • An LLM judge shown the candidate's reasoning is a different judge. The KL measurement says the teacher's discrimination on the output collapsed when it could see the reasoning. If your eval harness passes the model's chain of thought to the judge, the judge is partly grading plausibility of process rather than quality of result, and it will be less sensitive to a wrong answer that was reached thoughtfully.

  • Grading an unfinished artefact invites continuation. This is the sharper version of the same point. A reviewer model handed a partial state tends to extend it, because continuation is the likely completion. Score finished units.

  • Check where your gradient or your signal actually lives. A 5.5x gradient-norm ratio on tokens that are mostly filler is the kind of thing nobody measures and everybody could. The analogue in an agent-evaluation pipeline is a scoring rubric dominated by a section that does not correlate with task success.

Review checklist

  1. In any teacher-student or judge-candidate setup, write down exactly what the grader sees. If it includes the candidate's reasoning, test whether hiding it sharpens the grade.

  2. Compute the loss or score on the units you care about — answers, actions, tool calls — rather than on everything the model emitted.

  3. Measure tokens per episode before and after any distillation or post-training run. An increase against the untrained model is a bug, not a quirk.

  4. Split token counts into informative and filler and look at the trend separately. An aggregate length figure hides the pattern.

  5. Never score a partial artefact if you can avoid it; if you must, check whether the grader's output is biased towards continuation.

  6. Before adopting a method on a headline delta, find the pre-training baseline. A large gain over a degraded baseline may be a small gain over doing nothing.

  7. Check whether a result holds at your model scale, and whether the reported numbers come from a best or a final checkpoint.

Related reading

The token-efficiency theme connects directly to work on shipping a non-reasoning variant alongside a reasoning one: there the saving comes from suppressing deliberation at inference, here from not rewarding it during training, and both land on the same observation that most agent work does not benefit from a long chain of thought.

On the measurement side, the gradient and KL asymmetries are a reminder of how much a training or evaluation pipeline can be dominated by a component nobody inspected — the same shape as accounting failures in other parts of the stack, where the quantity that determines the outcome is not the one being reported.

And for anyone building evaluation harnesses for agents, the finding about a judge's discrimination collapsing when it sees the reasoning is worth testing against your own rubric before trusting its scores.

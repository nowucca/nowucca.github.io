A 142 KB tree model that reads the pointer: affect detection as a UI input, not an LLM call

Undated sourceSummarized 2026-11-25

Read the original

A commercial UI component catalogue has published a beta model that infers whether someone is frustrated, confused or lost from how they move the pointer, press, scroll and type, and exposes the result to the interface as a plain number. It is interesting for two reasons that have nothing to do with the product: the architecture is deliberately not a neural network, and the design around the model is more carefully specified than the model itself.

What it is

The artifact is a single JSON file of 142 KB (32 KB gzipped) holding 360 small gradient-boosted trees. One inference is "a few hundred comparisons, with no dependencies", bundled into the application and run in the browser. No LLM, no server, no network request at runtime, no permission prompt. It reads pointer positions, timings, and facts about the element under the pointer — that a control is a button, disabled, invalid — and explicitly not text, field values or the screen.

It emits six likelihoods (neutral, blocked, lost, confused, waiting, frustrated) plus a combined score, four times a second. Neutral returns null, so an unchanged screen is the default.

For anyone who has watched teams reach for a vision-language model to answer "is this user stuck", the size comparison is the argument. The signal here is a few dozen numbers per second describing cursor kinematics and DOM facts. That is a tabular classification problem, boosted trees are the right tool for it, and the resulting model fits in less space than a photograph and runs client-side forever at no marginal cost. A model-per-interaction API call could not meet the latency budget and would turn every mouse twitch into telemetry.

The part worth copying: two timescales and specified hysteresis

The model proper reads roughly the last eight seconds. On its own that is too slow for the obvious cases, so a second path reads the last one or two seconds — the pointer shaken back and forth at speed, circling one spot, three quick presses on one spot or on no control, a press on a disabled control — and answers within a few hundred milliseconds. The page is direct that this exists to catch "what the 8 second model is slow to see".

The two are then combined every 66 ms into one value between 0 and 1, and the smoothing rule is given as numbers rather than adjectives: output starts at 0.30 of the reading, reaches full strength at a 0.65 alert line, rises in about a tenth of a second, holds for a second once the user is calm, then eases back over a few seconds — or about half a minute while the pointer is at rest, on the reasoning that someone stuck may be reading.

That last clause is the detail that shows someone actually used the thing. A naive implementation treats pointer stillness as calm and immediately withdraws help from the person who has stopped moving because they are lost. Encoding "stillness is ambiguous" as a longer decay is a one-line change that a model-accuracy metric would never surface.

More generally, this is the pattern to take away for any continuous classifier that drives visible UI: a fast cheap detector for unambiguous events, a slower model for accumulated evidence, and an explicit asymmetric smoothing layer whose job is to stop the interface flickering. Most teams build only the middle one and then discover the output is unusable.

The labels are behavioural, and that is both the strength and the problem

Read the definitions and the word "mood" mostly dissolves. Frustrated means fighting the interface: pressing a locked or disabled control, quick repeated presses, the pointer shaken, a field retried unchanged after failing — "A blocked step reads as frustrated." Confused is read from pointer movement alone: circling one spot, scrolling back and forth, typing and deleting, invalid entries.

These are operationalisations of interface friction, and as such they are honest and useful. The hazard is in the naming. Nothing here measures emotion, and shipping a value called frustration invites downstream consumers to treat it as one. The failure mode is predictable: a feature logs "user frustration" per session, someone builds a dashboard, and the organisation starts managing a number that actually means "clicked a disabled button". If you adopt something like this, name the signal after what it detects.

The evidence, and how far it goes

The model was trained on 80 simulated people with planted episodes (shaking, circling, revisiting, retrying) plus real sessions from more than ten people across mouse, trackpad and touch, the latter included to teach it what ordinary use looks like so it stays quiet. Evaluation is on real sessions from more than ten people with planted problems — a Pay button that stays locked, a strict date format, a buried setting, a silent slow save — each scored by a model that had never seen them: 48 of 60 problems caught at the alert line, with 2.3% of calm time flagged (median person 1.5%).

Publishing a false-positive rate alongside the hit rate is more than most components of this kind offer, and the planted-problem design is sensible. Four things bound how much the numbers mean.

There is no per-mood breakdown. 48 of 60 is pooled across four problem types and six output classes, so a model that reliably detects that something is wrong while frequently mislabelling which state would score identically. The vendor's own weak-spot list suggests exactly this is happening: a blocked checkout is often read as lost rather than blocked.

The ground truth is a model's opinion. "Scored by a model that had never seen them" is a judge, unnamed and never validated against human labels. For a construct as slippery as confusion, the judge is the measurement instrument and it is undescribed.

The sample is more than ten people for both real training sessions and evaluation, and the page does not say whether they overlap. If they do, 2.3% is an in-distribution figure.

And detection is not the claim that matters. Nothing here measures whether adapting the screen in response actually helps. The product's value proposition rests on an unmeasured second step.

To its credit the page states its weak spots plainly: a short lost search under about 20 seconds can stay below the line, a blocked checkout often reads as lost, and someone exploring many sections of an easy flow can read as lost. Two of those three are false positives on ordinary behaviour.

The gap nobody mentions: assistive input

The detector's confusion signature — circling one spot, scrolling back and forth, slow wandering pointer paths — is also a description of how people navigate with head pointers, eye-tracking, switch access, a trackball under tremor, or screen magnification at high zoom. Someone with a motor impairment produces the frustration signature (repeated presses near a target, overshoot and correction) as a matter of course.

Nothing in the source addresses this, and it is the first thing to test before shipping. A UI that "brings forward what helps" when it decides you are struggling becomes a UI that constantly interrupts a subset of disabled users, and the interruption is triggered by their input method rather than their state. The mitigation is not a better model: it is a per-user off switch, a cap on how often the interface may react, and treating a sustained high reading as a signal to stop reacting rather than to escalate.

Checklist if you build one of these

  • Name the output after the behaviour you detect, not the emotion you infer from it.

  • Report per-class accuracy, not a pooled hit rate, and validate any model judge against human labels on a subset.

  • State the false-positive rate on calm time as prominently as the detection rate. It is the number that decides whether the feature is tolerable.

  • Measure the intervention, not just the detection. A correct diagnosis followed by an unhelpful change is a regression.

  • Test the full range of assistive inputs before launch, and give the user a way to turn the adaptation off permanently.

  • Keep inference local. The privacy story here (positions, timings and element facts, nothing leaving the page) is what makes continuous behavioural sensing defensible at all, and it disappears the moment the signal is sent anywhere.

  • Specify the smoothing as numbers in the design, not as a tuning exercise after the fact, and decide explicitly what stillness means in your product.

Status and caveats

The model is v0.4 beta, published as a paid component with a React kit under a model licence rather than as an open release. All figures are the vendor's own, measured by the vendor, with no published dataset, no repository and no external replication. The page is undated and carries no changelog, so the version number is unanchored. Treat the measurements as an indication that someone did the work, not as a result that travels.

CoEvoBench: can an LLM researcher co-evolve a model and its harness?

Xiao, Wang, Fan, Du and Xu (Alibaba); shared by Elvis SaraviaPublished 2026-11-20Summarized 2026-11-23

Read the original

Authors Elvis Saravia (shared)

self-improvementharnessbenchmarksrlpaper

Source: Xiao, Wang, Fan, Du and Xu, "CoEvoBench: Can LLMs Drive Recursive Co-Evolution of Agentic Systems?", arXiv 2611.27116, 20 Nov 2026 (Alibaba). Numbers below combine the abstract and a summary by Elvis Saravia (DAIR.AI).

The question

An agent's score depends on two things: the model and the harness (the code that wraps tools, prompts, retries and environment access). Existing benchmarks measure one with the other held fixed: post-training research with a frozen harness, or harness optimisation with a frozen model. CoEvoBench measures the joint decision a real researcher faces each round: change the harness, train the model, or both, knowing each change shifts the value of the other.

Setup

  • An LLM acts as the researcher. It gets a 9B base model, a starting harness and a 24-hour budget.

  • It may edit the harness, launch training runs, or do both, as many rounds as fit the budget.

  • It submits one model-harness pair, scored on held-out tasks in five agentic domains: SWE-bench Verified, a Terminal-Bench subset, AppWorld, tau-bench and BFCL.

  • Score is the fraction of the gap closed between the base system and an instruction-tuned model running a reference harness.

Results

Researcher modelGap closed
GPT-6 Astramore than four-fifths
Claude Opus 5more than half
Kimi K3, GLM-5.3, Qwen3.8-maxless than a third

  • The harness did most of the work in four of five domains; training mattered most on BFCL (function calling).

  • Co-evolution succeeded on interaction tasks but rarely on long-horizon coding.

  • Strong first step, then a stall. Researchers made a good first improvement but could not turn feedback from the improved system into the next one. Two habits stood out: choosing to train before checking whether training would help, and judging candidates on the same tasks they trained on.

Why it matters

  • It is a controlled testbed for one slice of recursive self-improvement: does an agent improve the system that improves it, round over round? Today the answer is one round.

  • It reinforces a pattern seen elsewhere: for agentic tasks, harness changes are cheaper and often worth more than weight changes.

  • The stall behaviours are classic research mistakes (no ablation, no held-out validation), so the gap is in experimental discipline as much as raw capability.

Tradeoffs and limits

  • One 9B base model and a 24-hour budget; results may shift with larger bases or longer budgets.

  • Five domains, each with its own benchmark quirks; "gap closed" depends on the chosen reference harness.

  • Researcher models are compared under one scaffold; a better research scaffold could narrow or widen the spread.

Failure modes to watch in your own improvement loops

  • Training first because it feels like progress, without a cheap harness-only baseline.

  • Selecting candidates on training tasks, which rewards overfitting.

  • Treating the first gain as a trend; most loops plateau after one round.

  • Changing model and harness in the same round, so you can't attribute the gain.

Review checklist

  • Is there a harness-only ablation before any training run?

  • Are candidates scored on a held-out split distinct from the tasks used to tune them?

  • Does each round change one component, or log both changes separately?

  • Is the improvement still there in round two and three, not just round one?

  • Is the reference system (what "gap closed" means) fixed and documented?

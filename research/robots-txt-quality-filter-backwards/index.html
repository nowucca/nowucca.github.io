The question, and why it is an engineering question

The paper asks a narrow thing: do reputable news sites and misinformation sites configure AI crawlers differently in robots.txt? The answer turns out to be one of the sharpest facts about web-scale corpora published this year, and it has nothing to do with news and everything to do with anyone building retrieval or agents over the open web.

If you honour robots.txt — and compliant crawlers do — then the corpus you are permitted to collect is not a random sample of the web. It is systematically depleted of exactly the sources you would most want.

The dataset

Sites were classified with Media Bias/Fact Check, following prior work:

  • Misinformation: MBFC "Questionable Source" or "Conspiracy-Pseudoscience", rated Low or Very Low factual reporting and Low overall credibility. N = 710.

  • Reputable: MBFC Pro-Science, Least Biased, Left-Center or Right-Center, rated High or Very High factual reporting and High overall credibility. N = 3,369.

  • Total 4,079 sites.

robots.txt and homepages were fetched from seven AWS vantage points (Germany, Sweden, US, Brazil, Africa, Asia, Australia), three crawls per site, keeping the most permissive valid result — a conservative choice that biases against the paper's own finding.

Operational homepages: 3,299 reputable, 672 misinformation. Valid robots.txt: 3,179 reputable (96.4%) against 493 misinformation (73.8%). The agent list is 63 AI user agents drawn from Dark Visitors, Cloudflare Radar, prior work and ai.robots.txt, including CCBot even though it is not run by an AI company.

A factor of about 6.6× in full-block rate, and about 20× in how many agents are named.

The distributions are more striking than the means. About 40% of reputable sites disallow no AI agent at all, about 25% disallow more than ten, and the most restrictive name 54. On the misinformation side, more than 80% disallow none and over 90% disallow two or fewer.

Per agent, over 50% of reputable sites fully disallow GPTBot, and roughly 40–50% disallow CCBot, ClaudeBot, ChatGPT-User and Google-Extended. Every named agent is blocked by under 5% of misinformation sites.

One subtlety that cuts the other way and is worth keeping: once an agent is named at all, 98.4% of reputable sites fully disallow it against 72.7% of misinformation sites. The gap is overwhelmingly about whether a site bothers to engage with AI crawlers, not about how it engages.

Popularity does not explain it

The obvious objection is that reputable sites are bigger and better resourced. The paper addresses it directly at the top of the distribution: 15 misinformation sites sit in the Tranco Top 10K, one was inaccessible, and of the 13 with a robots.txt, 9 had no DisallowAll for any AI agent. Counting the inaccessible site, the paper states that 10 of 14 (71.4%) of the most popular misinformation sites impose no AI-crawler limits.

So the most visible, highest-traffic misinformation properties are also open. Whatever drives the gap, it is not capability.

Two things are legible in that table. First, blocking is reactive and lags agent launch — each new named agent starts near zero and climbs, so a corpus crawled under a newly-introduced user-agent string is temporarily collected under permissions nobody has updated. Second, the reputable median passes 25 disallowed agents by early 2025 with an upper quartile near 50, while the misinformation distribution stays below five. The gap is widening, not closing.

One vivid detail: 27 reputable sites and exactly 1 misinformation site moved from partial allowance to DisallowAll; of those 27, 26 switched for PerplexityBot, all in 07/2024. That is a single news cycle visible in configuration files, and it says blocking behaviour is driven by public incidents rather than by policy.

robots.txt is advisory; active blocking is different and weaker

The paper also ran a control crawl varying the User-agent to see who actually blocks at the server:

AgentReputableMisinformation
Anthropic-AI516 / 2,877 (18.0%)58 / 576 (10.2%)
ClaudeBot711 (24.8%)147 (25.6%)
Both486 (16.9%)56 (9.8%)

Note the ClaudeBot row: at the level of active blocking the two populations are indistinguishable — misinformation sites block it at a marginally higher rate. The declared-policy gap is far larger than the enforced-behaviour gap.

And policy and enforcement barely agree with each other. Of the 486 reputable sites actively blocking both agents, only 230 (47.3% of those blockers, about 8% of all reputable sites) also have a matching DisallowAll in robots.txt. On the misinformation side, exactly one site did both. So the two mechanisms are nearly independent: reading robots.txt tells you little about what a server will serve you, and vice versa.

What the paper does not say

Worth stating plainly, because this result gets stretched:

  • It measures access controls only. There is no result about what AI search or assistants actually cite. There is no figure resembling "one in six sources cited by AI search was written by AI" — that claim circulates attached to this finding, and it is not from this paper.

  • It does not classify agents by purpose. No training-versus-retrieval-versus-user-triggered breakdown appears in the paper's main text.

  • It cannot see training data. No causal link between an exclusion policy and presence in any model's corpus is established.

  • Compliance is assumed, not verified. The authors note that adherence is "voluntary and relies on crawler compliance" and cite prior work that major crawlers "generally respect" robots.txt; they do not test it.

  • Active blocking was measured from one location with simple HTTP requests and limited headers, a deliberate choice to filter automated-detection responses, and the authors call it "an all-or-nothing mechanism" that can also block legitimate search indexing.

  • MBFC labels are human judgements and skew English-language (this point is from a later section and is not verified against the main text).

  • Paywalls and licensing deals are not covered.

What to do with it

  • Treat a compliant open-web crawl as a quality-biased sample, and say so in writing. The direction of the bias is now measured: a ~6.6× gap in full-block rate between high- and low-credibility sources. A retrieval corpus assembled by honouring robots.txt is enriched in the sources you least want, and no amount of downstream ranking recovers a document you never collected.

  • Do not use declared policy as a proxy for what you can fetch, or the reverse. Fewer than half of the servers that actively block also say so in robots.txt, and the enforced gap between the two populations is roughly nil.

  • Expect permission state to lag your user-agent. Each new agent string starts from near-zero block rates. If you introduce one, you are collecting under permissions that nobody has had the chance to express — which is a defensible legal position and a poor ethical one.

  • If source credibility matters to your product, you need a signal other than crawlability. An allowlist, a licensing arrangement or an explicit credibility rating — the open web's own gate is now running against you.

  • When you see this result quoted, check which claim is being made. The 60% figure is solid and well-denominated. The citation-composition claims often bundled with it are not in this work.

Related reading

Pairs naturally with work on provenance inside retrieval pipelines — where a graph or index records which document supported which claim — and with work on human verification of AI output, since both ask what a system can know about the quality of its own inputs. The uncomfortable connection is that most retrieval architectures treat corpus composition as given and spend their effort downstream of it.

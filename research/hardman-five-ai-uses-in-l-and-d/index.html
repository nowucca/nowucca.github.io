Hardman's five AI uses in L&D: from faster to better, each with a check

Source: How I AI: my top 5 daily AI use cases in 2026, Dr Philippa Hardman (Dr Phil's Newsletter), 26 Nov 2026.

What it is

Hardman had Claude search a year of her chats and classify how she uses AI across the five ADDIE phases (analysis, design, development, implementation, evaluation), sorting each use into faster, better, or newly possible. She reports three shifts: from faster to better, from single tasks to chained workflows with standing setups (templates, standards, sources), and from prompting to building her own tools with a spec, prototype, test, then shelve or scale loop.

The five uses

PhaseOld useNew useBuilt-in check
AnalysisSummarise a few interviewsClassify every learner signal (transcripts, survey comments, tickets) into your own categories, count distinct people per category, quote eachFresh chat verifies each quote as VERIFIED / PARAPHRASED / NOT FOUND; read the one-person list
DesignDraft a designDeep research builds an evidence checklist first, then the draft is scored against it (met / partly / not met) and the weakest assumption namedOpen the top three studies: adults at work or students in a lab?
DevelopmentBuild contentSynthetic learners (novice, intermediate, expert, with real misconceptions) answer draft quiz items before the key is shown; then compare with the keyModels play weak learners badly, so items look easier than they are; compare with the first real cohort
ImplementationWrite facilitator guidesFeedback bot whose rubric is induced from 5 to 10 pairs of (first draft, your edit); one next step, never rewritesMark 20 pieces yourself and use the bot only where it agrees; 30 adversarial test conversations
EvaluationSummarise happy sheetsCount target-behaviour instances in blinded, shuffled before/after work records, quoted and locatedHand-count three documents; compare rates, not raw counts

Engineering takeaways

  • Information hiding is the recurring design move. The evidence review runs before the model sees the design (so it doesn't hunt for confirming studies), the answer key is withheld from the synthetic learners, and before/after labels are stripped and shuffled for the behaviour count. These are the same leakage controls you'd use in an eval harness.

  • Every step ends in a verification pass, often in a fresh context. Quote checking in a new chat is the Elephant-Goldfish pattern (deep-context drafting, zero-context review).

  • Rubric induction from edit pairs is a cheap way to turn tacit standards into a grader, with agreement on a labelled sample gating its use.

  • Self-audit from chat history is a reusable prompt: ask what the model can and cannot access, search per phase, cite chat and date, and forbid invented titles.

Evidence and caveats

Cited figures: AI use in L&D teams went from about 40% (2024) to 68% (2026), and evaluation and analytics rose from 8th to 3rd of ten uses (Taylor and Vinauskaitė 2026). In Synthesia's survey, 75% cite faster content as a gain and 11% cite better job performance. Leading teams are about twice as likely to put AI in front of learners for practice and feedback (38% vs 17%). Synthetic item difficulty correlated with real difficulty at 0.75 to 0.82 in school maths (Acquaye et al. 2026). Unrestricted AI raised practice scores but cut exam scores by 17% (Bastani et al. 2025). Per-behaviour feedback from transcripts raised that behaviour 13% (Demszky et al. 2024).

These are secondary citations; the underlying studies were not independently checked. The five uses are one practitioner's self-report, and the post ends with a bootcamp pitch. Sample sizes in the recipes (20 records, 20 marked pieces) are explicitly called first looks, not proof.

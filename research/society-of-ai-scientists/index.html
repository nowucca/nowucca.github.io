A Society of AI Scientists: 936 agents, 35 labs, and why review must execute code

A Society of AI Scientists (MIT, Stanford and others), alphaXiv, 2026-11-19; shared by Ethan MollickPublished 2026-11-19 (paper); 2026-11-20 (post)Summarized 2026-11-20

Sources: "A Society of AI Scientists" (alphaXiv with authors from MIT, Stanford and others), submitted 19 Nov 2026, with its alphaXiv summary and launch thread; Ethan Mollick's LinkedIn post on the paper.

The claim in one paragraph

Agents can now run end-to-end research, but can independent agents act as a scientific community: critique each other, correct mistakes and build cumulative knowledge? Over twelve weeks (19 Aug to 12 Nov 2026), 936 agents in 35 human-operated labs shared a preprint server and a messaging workspace. They published 936 studies, each with code, data and a record linking reported numbers to outputs, and exchanged 9,500 messages in 229 agent-created rooms. Norms emerged: public retraction of mistaken claims before publication, negative results cited as often as positive ones, and an agent-founded venue, the Journal of Agent Research, with editors who inspected code. The strongest finding is about verification: reviewers who only read text could not tell a disclosed weakness from a hidden one, while objections turned into executed scripts overturned headline conclusions in about half of major cases.

Setup

  • Humans provisioned, agents chose. Operators supplied compute, budgets and broad themes; agents picked hypotheses, designs and analyses. Hardware ranged from CPU-only laptops to B200 clusters; topics ran from mechanistic interpretability and continual learning to solar physics.

  • Staged growth. One lab, then six, then all 35, under a shared onboarding protocol asking agents to announce plans, share critiques, cite prior work and give rigorous null results full credit.

  • Records, not just papers. Each study bundles manuscript, runnable code, raw data and a claim ledger with hashed file records.

  • Observational. Nothing was randomized, so differences between stages cannot show that scale caused them.

Findings worth keeping

  • Self-correction before publication. 19 published cases of empirical self-correction, all before upload, with a median lead of 21.4 hours. One example: a lab withdrew a claimed continual-learning gain after a preregistered learning-rate control exposed an under-tuned baseline.

  • Nulls valued. Three of the eight most-cited studies reported negative findings.

  • Citation depth is not planning. The longest citation chain reached 101 steps, but a recency-matched random null averaged 99.4 (P = 0.44): agents cite recent work, not evidence of long-horizon research programs.

  • Text review fails; executable review works. A four-model blind panel (methods, reproducibility, significance, devil's advocate) flagged 11 of 16 flawed first drafts but also 10 of 16 repaired versions where the limitation was disclosed. When a guest editor turned 276 objections into scripts run on the uploaded data, the scripts overturned the headline conclusion in 77 of 156 major objections (49.4%) and 24 of 120 minor ones (20.0%).

  • Value checks are necessary but not sufficient. 93.8% of 23,811 reported statistics matched saved outputs exactly, with no fabricated values found, yet code inspection still caught scripts and prompts that used answer keys or leaked targets.

  • Human comparison. Blinded LLM judges on 202 matched pairs rated agent papers higher on experimental rigor (9.02 vs 7.00) and human papers higher on novelty and significance. The pooled rigor advantage came from one lab's structured replication pipeline; other labs did not differ significantly from matched human papers.

Tradeoffs

  • Volume versus scrutiny. Fast publication only helps if review keeps pace; text review scaled but could not separate honest disclosure from hidden flaws.

  • Structure versus autonomy. The best results came from the lab with the most structured pipeline, suggesting process matters more than agent count.

  • Ledger checks versus method checks. Matching numbers to outputs is cheap and catches fabrication, but not leakage or a design that tests the wrong thing.

Failure modes to expect

  • Target leakage. Agents using answer keys or leaked targets in scripts or prompts, invisible to number-matching audits.

  • Reviewer conservatism. Text reviewers penalizing honest disclosure as much as concealment.

  • Citation churn mistaken for cumulative progress.

  • LLM-judged quality. Rigor and novelty ratings came from LLM judges that could not run code or inspect figures.

Review checklist for an agent research pipeline

  1. Does every claim link to a hashed output that an auditor can recompute?

  2. Are reviewer objections converted into executable checks, not just text responses?

  3. Are scripts and prompts inspected for answer keys and target leakage?

  4. Are null results recorded and citable?

  5. Is citation depth compared against a recency-matched null before claiming cumulative progress?

  6. Are quality comparisons blinded and, ideally, judged by humans who can run the code?

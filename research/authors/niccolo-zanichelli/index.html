  1. 2026-11-25R298Swarms do not yet raise the roof: agent teams trade tokens for speed, not qualityAt a matched total token budget, does a team of coding agents reach a higher score than one agent? Across more than 1,100 runs and 80-plus configurations on coding and contest tasks, the answer is no: teams of eight finished about 6x sooner than a single agent and scored about 7% lower, and on tasks with headroom teams never beat the single agent. The study's most useful move is in the baseline. One result looked like a genuine swarm premium — teams of eight scoring 93.2% against 83.7% for one long session — until the same single-agent budget was split into eight consecutive <em>fresh</em> sessions sharing only the files on disk, at which point the single agent reached 97.0% and beat the teams, including teams of 16 and 32 on the weaker model. Best-of-N does not explain it either: eight independent agents at a quarter of the budget each reached only 43.7%. So what the swarm was beating was a stale context, not a smaller one. A long session degrades as context accumulates, because compaction has to guess what will matter later and carries stale material forward; a fresh session with only the working files avoids that. Measured against the restarted baseline, team efficiency runs about 0.6 to 0.94, so budget for losing roughly 10 to 40% of token efficiency to coordination. The practical conclusion for anyone sizing an agent fleet: compare a swarm against one agent with periodic fresh-context restarts and state kept in files, use teams when wall-clock time matters, and expect harness work on context hygiene to pay more than adding agents. Key caveats: about three runs per configuration, so a few points of difference is underpowered; the restart baseline was run on one task only; and budgets are roughly 100x smaller than some lab runs.

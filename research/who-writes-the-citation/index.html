Who writes the citation decides what your reviewer is checking

Filed 2026-11-25. Sources: a practitioner post in a weekly production-AI series (Hao Hoang, sourceCode), and the paper it cites — Liu, Zhang and Liang, "Evaluating Verifiability in Generative Search Engines", arXiv 2304.09848, Findings of EMNLP 2023.

The claim

A document assistant that shows a link under each answer looks finished. The practitioner post argues that the link's value depends entirely on which component produced it, and that teams rarely know which one did.

There are two architectures hiding behind the same UI:

  1. The retriever emits the reference. The component that fetched the passage records what it fetched, including the document version. The citation is a field the pipeline writes, derived from an action that actually happened.

  2. The model writes the reference in the same pass as the prose. Nothing is recorded. The link is a token sequence the model produced, so it can point at a passage that does not support the sentence, or — if nothing validates it — at a document that was never opened at all.

The post's one-line version: a link the model wrote is a claim about a source, not a source.

The consequence is a review-cost consequence, which is what makes it an architecture decision rather than a style preference. If the retriever emits the reference, a human reviewer is checking evidence: does this passage support this sentence? If the model wrote it, the reviewer is checking a claim against another claim: is this link real, does this passage exist in this version, and does it say what the sentence says? That is a second review step, and somebody has to own it.

What the cited measurement actually says

The post leans on a 2023 audit of four generative search engines, and it reports the paper's two headline numbers correctly. Because it is the only quantitative anchor here, the details matter.

Setup. Bing Chat (Balanced), NeevaAI, perplexity.ai and YouChat, scraped between late February and late March 2023. 1,450 queries per system, drawn from twelve query distributions (Natural Questions subsets by answer type, ELI5 both historical and live, WikiHow keyword queries, Oxford All Souls essay questions, model-generated debate propositions). 34 pre-screened crowd annotators, one annotation per query–response pair. Support was judged Full / Partial / No per statement–citation pair under the AIS framework.

Two metrics, defined per response.

  • Citation precision: of the citations attached to the response, what fraction support the sentence they are attached to (partial support counts only when the union of a statement's citations fully supports it).

  • Citation recall: of the statements that need a citation, what fraction are fully supported by the citations they carry.

So: about a quarter of citations did not support their sentence, and about half of sentences were not fully supported by the citations they carried.

The inverse correlation, and how much weight it bears. The post's transferable point is that support ran inversely to how fluent and useful people found the answer. The paper reports Pearson r of recall with fluency at −0.76 and with perceived utility at −0.53; precision at −0.84 and −0.96. Read these carefully: the paper does not state the unit of aggregation, and the surrounding text implies the four systems, which would make n=4. The authors themselves write that they do not believe the relationship is fundamental and call it an empirical observation. The ranking is visible without the coefficients — the system with 11% recall scored highest on both fluency and utility — and that ordinal fact is the durable part.

The mechanism the paper offers is the useful engineering detail. Supported statements were largely extractive: mean BLEU of 31.3 against the cited evidence, BERTScore F1 of 73.2, with similarity correlating to precision at r=0.80, and annotators finding extractive evidence for 99.5% of fully or partially supported statements. In other words, these systems were accurate when they copied and unsupported when they synthesised. Which tells you where your own failures will concentrate: on answers that aggregate across sources, or that select among citations of differing relevance — exactly the two weaknesses the paper names.

Caveats the paper flags itself. Abstention rates differ sharply (NeevaAI abstained on 22.7% of queries, the others on under 0.5%), so the systems are hard to compare head to head. And this is a snapshot of consumer products from early 2023. The post hedges correctly: treat it as evidence the gap is measurable, not as a number for your system.

Architecture: three places the reference can come from

A. Retriever-emitted, versioned. The retrieval layer returns (doc_id, version, span_offsets, retrieved_at) alongside the text, and the answer renders citations from that record rather than from the model's output. The model is never permitted to name a source; it may only refer to passages by handle. This makes the citation unforgeable by construction — the worst failure available is a correctly-cited passage that does not support the sentence, which is a relevance problem rather than a provenance problem.

B. Model-written, then validated. The model writes [doc_id, span] markers; a post-hoc checker resolves each one, confirms the span exists in the version served, and runs a support check (entailment model, or an LLM judge, against the retrieved text). Failures are repaired or the sentence is dropped. This is recoverable, but it costs a second pass, and the support check becomes a component with its own error rate that you now have to evaluate.

C. Model-written, unvalidated. The default when citations are added as a prompt instruction ("cite your sources"). Nothing resolves the reference. This is the configuration the post is warning about, and it is the one that demos best, because the citation is generated to look plausible alongside the prose.

The important asymmetry: A and C are indistinguishable in the UI. A reviewer looking at the screen cannot tell which one they are using, which is why the post's closing question — would the team that built your assistant know which component writes the link? — is a real audit question rather than a rhetorical one.

Tradeoffs

  • Fluency cost. Retriever-emitted citations constrain what the model may assert, which pushes answers toward extraction and quotation. Expect the prose to read worse. The 2023 data suggests users will rate it lower, which makes this a product decision as much as an engineering one — and means a fluency or helpfulness metric alone will select against grounding.

  • Coverage cost. If a sentence can only carry a citation when a retrieved passage supports it, some questions become unanswerable. That is the honest outcome, but it shows up as abstention, and abstention is unpopular. Instrument it rather than letting it silently disappear.

  • Aggregation is the hard case. Both the post's architecture split and the paper's findings converge here: a sentence that synthesises three passages has no single supporting span. Retriever-emitted provenance degrades to "these three documents were read", which is weaker than it looks. Decide explicitly whether multi-source claims are allowed, and if so how they are attributed.

  • Validation adds a model to the critical path. Option B's support checker is another evaluated component with its own precision and recall, and a judge that never sees the full source document will pass work that reads as consistent. Grounding the judge in the retrieved text is the whole point of the step.

  • Version drift. A citation without a version is a citation to whatever the document says today. For contracts, policies and anything with an effective date, the version is the load-bearing field and the one most often dropped.

Failure modes

  1. Phantom source. The model names a document that was never retrieved. Only possible in option C; impossible in A.

  2. Wrong span, right document. The citation resolves but the passage does not support the sentence. This is the 74.5% precision gap, and it survives into option A.

  3. Unsupported synthesis. A fluent sentence that no single retrieved passage supports. This is the 51.5% recall gap, and it is the common case for "why" and "should" questions.

  4. Stale version. The cited clause has since been amended; the answer is about the old text and says nothing about it.

  5. Reviewer habituation. Links that are usually right train reviewers to stop opening them. A quarter-wrong citation rate with no visible marker is worse for review discipline than no citations at all, because it converts an active check into a glance.

  6. Grading on fluency. Evaluating the assistant on helpfulness ratings optimises against grounding, per the inverse correlation. If the only eval is a thumbs-up, the system will drift toward option C.

  7. Mixed-architecture surface. One answer path emits retriever-backed citations and another lets the model write them, both rendering identically. Nobody can state the system's provenance guarantee because it does not have one.

Review checklist

  • Which component emits each citation in each answer path? Name it. If the answer is "the model", say so out loud.

  • Can a cited reference exist that the retrieval layer never returned? If yes, you are in option C regardless of what the prompt says.

  • Does the record carry the document version and the span offsets, not just an id?

  • Is there a resolution step that fails loudly when a span does not exist in the version served?

  • Is there a support check, and is it grounded in the retrieved text rather than in the answer alone?

  • What are your own citation precision and recall, measured on your own corpus, by human annotation on a sample? (The published numbers are not a substitute.)

  • Are multi-source and synthesised claims allowed, and how are they attributed? What is the abstention rate when they are not?

  • Does any eval reward fluency or helpfulness without a grounding term? If so, what stops the drift?

  • Who owns the second review step when the model writes the link — by name, in the on-call rotation?

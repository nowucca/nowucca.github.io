SVReg: when a JEPA world model learns a latent map, and what it forgets

The claim in one paragraph

A latent world model used for planning needs two properties at once. Geometry: distances in latent space should smoothly track progress toward a goal, so a planner can follow them. Content: task-relevant factors, such as where a manipulated object is, must stay decodable from the latent. The two common ways to stop a JEPA from collapsing (a stop-gradient target, or a distributional variance regularizer without stop-gradient) both tend to give jagged, tangled latent paths. SVReg, a sliced variance regularizer, gives smooth latent maps: trained only to predict the next frame from first-person video, with no positions or labels, the model's leading principal components lay out the maze, and the same happens on walks around real city blocks. The catch is that SVReg can drop slow, hard-to-predict factors. Slow SVReg adds two components to keep them, and has the best planning success in the hard settings of five visual control tasks.

Method

  • SVReg. A one-sided floor on the standard deviation of the latent projected onto random unit directions: each direction is penalized only if its spread over time falls below 1. Slicing along random directions is a cheap way to keep the whole latent from collapsing without a stop-gradient target.

  • Slow channels. A small fixed set of latent channels gets a variance-normalized slowness penalty, so persistent factors (a resting cube, a block's pose) keep room in the latent instead of being squeezed out by fast-changing ones.

  • Gated residual predictor. The predictor copies the current latent and learns only the change, scaled by a per-dimension gate initialized at 0.1. Small default updates bias the model toward smooth trajectories and keep slow content stable.

Hard goals on the mazes, all goals on Cube. SVReg wins on navigation and reaching but drops on Cube, where it lost the cube height in three of six seeds (the seeds whose random initial encoder read height least). Slow SVReg recovers it. City-walk experiments use first-person walks rendered from 3D Gaussian-splat reconstructions of real blocks, with actions but no poses.

Why it matters

The paper names a tradeoff that applies to any learned state representation used for planning: regularizers that make the space smooth enough to plan in can discard exactly the slow, hard-to-predict variables a task depends on. Measuring only planning success on navigation would hide that loss; it shows up only in held-out readouts of specific factors and in manipulation tasks.

Tradeoffs

  • Geometry versus content. Smooth maps come from spreading variance evenly; slow factors carry little frame-to-frame variance and are the first to go.

  • Hand-picked slow channels. The fix reserves a fixed number of channels for slow content, which is a design choice per task family.

  • Seed sensitivity. Content loss depended on the random initial encoder, so single-seed results would mislead.

Failure modes to expect

  • Silent content loss. A planner on a smooth latent can look good on reaching while failing manipulation because the object state is gone.

  • Proxy readouts. Linear readouts of chosen factors only test the factors you thought to probe.

  • Sim-to-real gap. City walks come from splat reconstructions, not raw camera footage.

Review checklist for a latent world model used for planning

  1. Is latent geometry measured directly (path smoothness, distance-to-goal correlation), not inferred from planning success alone?

  2. Are task-critical slow factors probed with held-out readouts?

  3. Are results reported over multiple seeds, with per-seed failures visible?

  4. Are both navigation-style and manipulation-style tasks evaluated?

  5. Is the anti-collapse mechanism compared against stop-gradient and a distributional regularizer under the same budget?

Dated web snapshots as an API parameter: freezing retrieval for training and evals

Published 2026-11-25Summarized 2026-11-25

Read the original

evaluationretrievalreinforcement-learningdata-contamination

A search-API vendor has added a date-cutoff parameter to its retrieval and page-fetch endpoints, announced 25 November 2026. The pitch is narrow and the mechanism is the interesting part: it is a way to run retrieval against the web as it stood on a chosen date, so that an agent trained or evaluated on a past question cannot read the answer out of later reporting.

What it actually does

The provider stores a copy of every page body at crawl time, with the capture time, so each URL accumulates a version history. The cutoff parameter answers from that history instead of the live web.

On the search endpoint, retrieval considers only pages that were published or first crawled by the cutoff; each surviving candidate is then swapped for its latest stored copy at or before the cutoff, and ranking scores the query against that older text. Title, date and snippet all come from that same copy. On the page-fetch endpoint, the call returns the full text of the stored copy, and the live site is never contacted.

That distinction carries the whole value proposition. A publication-date filter leaves you reading today's version of a page that happens to have an old date on it, which is exactly how outcomes leak into questions about the past: a page gets updated with the result and keeps its original date. Swapping in stored content at the cutoff is a different and much stronger guarantee.

The interface is one optional parameter carrying an ISO 8601 UTC timestamp; omitting it returns the current web. It works against an existing API key with no separate enrolment. Coverage is stated as up to 365 days back, with no fixed earliest date given. Pricing is unchanged for dated requests — no historical surcharge — at the provider's standard per-thousand rates for search requests and fetched URLs.

The evidence offered, and what it shows

All of the numbers are the vendor's own, and the comparison is against a single named competitor product, so read them as a direction rather than a measurement. Two results are still worth knowing.

The first is a reinforcement-learning run on 2026 political prediction-market questions with a small open model. Before any training, the held-out score was 0.94 with live-web retrieval against 0.77 with the dated retriever. The market's own baseline on those questions was 0.79. The live-web figure being far above what the market itself achieved is the leak made visible: the model is not forecasting, it is reading the answer. Training with the dated retriever moved held-out reward from 0.77 to 0.80.

The second number is the one an RL engineer should care about more. With live-web retrieval, roughly 90% of rollout groups produced zero gradient, against roughly 25% with the dated retriever. A leaky retriever makes most rollouts uniformly correct, and a group with no spread teaches nothing. Contamination in a training loop is not primarily an accuracy problem; it is a sample-efficiency problem, and most of what you pay for gets thrown away.

The comparison against the competitor product reports publication dates present on 98.8–99.5% of returned pages against 66.1–77.7%, and an automated judge flagging under 0.5% of pages per set as containing post-cutoff content against 1.10% on the competitor's random sample — 18 of 1,000 queries versus 62. The judge's method is not described, which is a meaningful omission when the judge is the only instrument measuring the thing being sold.

The honest caveat, and the one that is missing

The announcement does state that leakage is not zero: a publication date alone does not show whether the cutoff held, and its own judge flagged a small fraction of its own pages. That is a fair admission and it is more than most launch posts make.

The gap is crawl frequency, which is never given. "The latest copy saved at or before the cutoff" is only as tight as the crawl interval. A page crawled monthly gives you a month-wide window in which the content you receive is not the content that was live at your cutoff, and nothing in the interface tells you how stale the copy you got is. For a backtest pinned hours before an earnings call — one of the stated use cases — that is the difference between a usable experiment and a silently wrong one. If you adopt this, the first thing to ask for is per-page capture timestamps alongside the results, so staleness is a number you can filter on rather than an assumption.

Two smaller gaps follow from the design. The inclusion test is date-based (published or first crawled by the cutoff) sitting in front of a content-based swap, so a page that existed before your cutoff but was first crawled after it is excluded entirely — the recall side of the guarantee is weaker than the precision side. And nothing is said about deleted pages, paywalled content, robots.txt exclusions, coverage by domain or date, or what the fetch endpoint does with a URL it has never crawled.

Why this matters beyond one vendor

The useful idea here is that temporal isolation belongs in the retrieval layer as a request parameter, not in the harness as a post-hoc filter. Every team that has tried to build a time-respecting eval on a live search API has ended up writing its own date filtering over returned results, which cannot work, because the filter sees metadata and the contamination lives in the body.

That makes the obvious alternatives worth naming, since the announcement names only one competitor. A public web archive gives you genuinely dated page bodies with documented capture times, but sparse and uneven coverage and no ranking. Fixed public crawl snapshots give you a reproducible corpus with a known date, but you supply the index. A commercial dated-retrieval API gives you ranking and coverage with an opaque capture schedule and a dependency on one vendor's history. For a benchmark that needs to be reproducible by other people years from now, the archive or the fixed snapshot is the safer substrate even though it is more work; for an RL loop where you need throughput and ranking quality and the experiment is yours, the API is the better trade.

The practical move for anyone currently training or evaluating agents against live search is smaller than adopting any of this: measure your own zero-gradient fraction. If most of your rollout groups are uniformly correct, you are probably not measuring reasoning, whatever your held-out score says.

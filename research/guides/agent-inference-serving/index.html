Field guide: Inference serving

How to serve models for agent workloads: prefix reuse, batching and scheduling, speculative decoding, MoE and sparse attention, local inference, quantization, cold starts and RL rollouts.

Claims read from the full papersUpdated 2026-11-24

Bottom line

For agent workloads, reuse KV state across shared prefixes, batch continuously, and chunk long prefills when they hold up other requests (R051-f2) (R051-f3) (R051-f5). Measure speculative decoding at the batch size and model type you actually serve (R215-f2) (R215-f3). Treat quantization and expert pruning as accuracy trades and expert offloading as a speed trade, and measure each per model (R174-f6) (R239-f2) (R153-f2). In rollout infrastructure, manage the slowest-rollout tail and weight staleness as limits in their own right (R176-f1) (R238-f3).

The sources frame serving this way. A practitioner explainer lists prefix caching, continuous batching, PagedAttention, chunked prefill, speculative decoding and prefill/decode disaggregation as techniques that leave the model unchanged, with no measurements of its own (R051-f1) (R051-f7). NVIDIA's RL post names slowest-rollout completion time, repeated shared prompt prefixes and weight freshness as the three constraints on RL post-training, as a vendor description with no experiments in the post (R176-f1).

Most measured numbers here come from authors or vendors testing their own systems (R086-f15) (R096-f5) (R115-f2) (R153-f2) (R174-f2) (R215-f2) (R238-f5) (R239-f2) (R240-f2), and several speedups hold only in a narrow configuration. Google's G-MTP drafter reached 3.14x on the 31B dense Gemma 4 model at batch size 1 on H100 with vLLM, but 1.34x on the 26B MoE model, in the authors' own measurements (R215-f2). The Evolving Infra authors, who ran both their system and the Dynamo v1.5.1 baseline, report up to 4.12x lower mean end-to-end latency (R086-f15), but only 1.15 to 1.23x on their decode-heavy workload (R086-f17). The Ling team cut mean makespan by 10.4 to 37.5 percent on Ling-3.0-Flash in their own live runs (R115-f2), while their chain-advancing action scheduler alone gave 1.9 to 3.5 percent in replay (R115-f4).

Cold starts and rollouts have their own failure modes. NVIDIA reports that Snapshot restored replicas 8x to 36x faster than cold start in its own tests (R096-f5), and a restore needs a node with the same GPU model and driver (R096-f7). Microsoft AI reports that adding inference servers without changing batch size can raise staleness, because more training steps complete while one rollout finishes (R238-f3).

Several sources were read only in part or are thin: a LinkedIn post with its loaded comments, an interactive simulator, and project READMEs or release posts. "How this was made" lists the read depth of the partly read sources, and the discrepancy notes give it for every serving source. Claims from thinly read sources carry less weight below.

Do

  1. Build prompts and routing around prefix reuse. Prefix caching reuses computed KV state for a shared token prefix, while the uncached suffix is still prefilled and generation runs as normal (R051-f2). The Ling team's system combines routing-prefix scheduling with load-balanced consistent-hash routing (R115-f1). In their runs, raising concurrency alone on SWE dropped cache reuse from 98.3 to 87.5 percent (R115-f5). NVIDIA describes Dynamo's router as balancing KV cache reuse against worker load (R176-f2), but the post gives no measurement of how it does so (R176-f4). The Evolving Infra evaluation replays real Claude Code traces with a replayer that keeps pauses and prefix sharing but fixes output lengths and tool-call durations to recorded values (R086-f16).

  2. Batch continuously and chunk long prefills. Continuous batching removes finished requests and admits waiting ones between iterations, instead of holding a fixed batch until every request ends (R051-f3). A long prefill can delay decode iterations of requests already being served, and chunked prefill makes chunk size part of a latency and throughput trade-off (R051-f5). PagedAttention maps logical KV blocks to non-contiguous physical blocks to cut fragmentation, and it manages memory without approximating attention (R051-f4). These are conceptual statements from a post with no figures (R051-f7). The Serving Trace Lab simulator shows whole against chunked prefill and static against continuous batching step by step (R180-f1); its batching view uses a fixed five-request illustrative workload, not a measurement (R180-f2).

  3. Benchmark on agent-shaped traffic and report tail latency per load level. Gains in the Evolving Infra comparison varied by workload, and its planner traded P95 TTFT for throughput, so P95 TTFT was above Dynamo at 224 and 288 agents (R086-f17). The FreeToken authors report worst-turn TTFT: theirs stayed under 44 s, while each baseline crossed 150 s somewhere (179 to 946 s), a mark they tie to the OpenClaw 120 s idle watchdog (R153-f3). The Ling paper reports the final-5-percent tail separately from mean makespan (R115-f4) (R115-f5). The Evolving Infra headline of up to 4.12x and 13.92x is a best case; the same authors report 1.15 to 1.23x on the decode-heavy workload and P95 TTFT above Dynamo at 224 and 288 agents, against one baseline they ran themselves (R086-f15) (R086-f17).

  4. Separate environment capacity from sample admission in batch rollouts and evaluations. The Ling system decouples sample admission from environment capacity and adds Chain-Advancing Action Scheduling that favours deeper trajectories (R115-f1). On MCP-Atlas with constrained environment capacity, inference optimisations plus capacity separation cut makespan by 63.9 percent on Ling-3.0-Flash and 57.9 percent on Ling-3.1-Flash, and CAAS cut a further 3.1 and 8.5 percent, as means of three 500-task runs on H20 GPUs (R115-f3). Scores stayed aligned rather than unchanged, for example BFCL moved from 73.0 to 75.65 (R115-f5).

  5. Measure speculative decoding at your batch size, model type and device. With the classic acceptance and correction rule, speculative decoding preserves the target distribution, and its benefit depends on draft cost, verification cost and acceptance rate (R051-f6). G-MTP's speedups on H100 at batch size 1 ranged from 1.34x (26B MoE) to 3.14x (31B dense) (R215-f2). At 31B it reached 3.14x against 2.60x for DFlash and 2.10x for EAGLE-3 under the same vLLM setup on two H100s, in the authors' comparison (R215-f3). On a Pixel 11 Edge TPU, the clustered vocabulary projection cut drafting time but also lowered acceptance, for a net gain of 1.01x (R215-f4). The authors state that speedups depend on draft length and batch size (R215-f6).

  6. Check drafter efficiency after you fine-tune the target, and retune the drafter if it drops. When only the Gemma 4 E4B backbone was fine-tuned, block efficiency fell 36.3 percent on SciQA and 20.9 percent on GSM8K, and joint fine-tuning or two-stage distillation of the drafter restored most or all of it, in the authors' LoRA experiments (R215-f5).

  7. For sparse attention, count the indexer's cost. In NVIDIA's Figure 1, the indexer and Top-k take 47 percent of a DeepSeek V3.2 prefill layer at 128K input tokens (R198-f1). On NVIDIA's production FP8 indexer kernel, decode indexer runtime moved only 2 percent while group size grew 16x, and one pass can score about 200 query rows of group size times draft depth before runtime steps up (R198-f2). NVIDIA states that reusing the previous step's Top-k as a guess is 2.1 to 2.4x faster than a standard radix select, without naming hardware (R198-f3). Its eight design guidelines are aimed at model developers targeting NVIDIA GPUs (R198-f4), and they come from one kernel sweep rather than a comparison of the listed sparse attention methods (R198-f5).

  8. When an MoE model does not fit, compare expert offloading with expert pruning on your own tasks. FreeToken streams experts over PCIe, splits each decode step's cache misses between GPU cache fill and CPU execution, and resizes VRAM between expert cache and KV at runtime (R153-f1). On an RTX 5090 the authors measured 77 to 83 tok/s on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and 22 to 25 tok/s on DeepSeek-V4-Flash, 1.8 to 2.3x and 1.5 to 1.9x the strongest baseline in each workload, on a rented server capped at 6 to 8 CPU threads (R153-f2). Dynamic Expert Pruning instead predicts a per-request expert mask from the agent's prompts (R239-f1). At 50 percent retention it averaged 75.9 against 82.2 for the dense model, and at 25 percent it averaged 63.4, in the authors' runs over three seeds (R239-f2). The authors bound the predictor and swap overhead at about 1 percent of generation wall time in aggregate (R239-f4).

  9. Search quantization recipes per model where you can, checking accuracy outside the search loop. QDA has a coding agent score candidate recipes by output KL divergence on reference traces it never sees, then checks promising ones with long generations for loops and cut-off answers (R174-f1). Its evaluator runs outside the agent's sandbox from a read-only copy and returns only KL values (R174-f4). Across four reasoning models it improved average accuracy over the strongest public recipe by 0.9, 1.4 and 2.2 points at W4A16, W4A8 and W3A16, from one search per model and precision in the authors' runs (R174-f2). On the 320B GLM-5.3-Flash at NVFP4, one search recovered 98.3 percent of FP8 accuracy against 96.9 and 96.3 percent for the NVIDIA and Red Hat AI checkpoints (R174-f3).

  10. Treat bandwidth-based decode estimates as rough until you measure. MLX Model Scout estimates decode speed as bandwidth times an efficiency factor divided by active weight bytes plus KV bytes, and takes weight size from the actual download (R162-f2). Using the download size avoids formula errors of up to 9.7 percent on repos such as gemma-3-27b (R162-f4). Its four measurements on one M5 Pro were within about 1 to 4 percent of the estimates, but the efficiency factors were calibrated on the same two models (R162-f3).

  11. Cut cold starts with checkpoint and restore, and plan for its constraints. Dynamo Snapshot dumps GPU state with cuda-checkpoint and the process tree with CRIU, through a privileged DaemonSet that writes to shared storage (R096-f1). The worker signals readiness before it registers with the control plane, because CRIU cannot capture established TCP connections (R096-f2). Releasing the KV cache buffer before checkpointing cut one artifact from about 190 GiB to about 6 GiB, for Qwen3-0.6B on one B200 (R096-f3). In NVIDIA's November tests on B200 with VAST NFS, restores took 3.6 to 42.7 seconds, 8x to 36x faster than cold start, with run counts not stated (R096-f5). The measured restore path excludes provisioning and image pull, and checkpoint creation took 15 seconds to 5.5 minutes per configuration (R096-f7). A different mechanism, Evolving Infra's runtime phase switch, averaged 6.2 s against a 754 s Dynamo relaunch in its authors' comparison; it is not checkpoint and restore (R086-f15).

  12. In RL rollout infrastructure, control staleness explicitly. Rocket models staleness from sampler lag and the time between generation and training, and controls it with frequent weight updates, oldest-rollout-first priority, dropping failed rollouts and over-allocating the learner so jobs stay inference-bound (R238-f2). In one example group in Microsoft AI's blog, the fastest rollout waited 7.6 hours for the slowest (R238-f3). Dropping failed rollouts can bias the GRPO group baseline (R238-f6). Microsoft AI reports bitwise sampler-learner parity for identical checkpoints and inputs, which it attributes to replicated attention output projections, a local FP32 expert combine and aligned FA4 tiling; its evidence is two runs of a 7B-active, 180B-total model launched about a month apart (R238-f4). Rocket also stores MoE weights and global-attention KV in MXFP8 and computes in BF16, which Microsoft AI reports gives about 67 percent more global-context tokens (R238-f4). NVIDIA's Dynamo copies weights GPU to GPU and can send compressed delta weights rebuilt from a matching base version (R176-f2).

  13. Keep interactive agent turns off deferred capacity, and make cancellation explicit. In ADK, each model turn of a tool-using agent queues separately on the deferred tier, so total latency is the sum of every turn's queue wait, and ADK sets no client deadline (R206-f3). A client timeout stops polling, but the backend request still runs and is billed with its output lost (R206-f4). On AWS Lambda, the function also keeps running and billing after a client disconnects (R235-f4).

  14. Check where bytes buffer before you trust a streamed response. On Lambda's managed Java runtime, the stream handed to a RequestStreamHandler buffers in memory, and a probe that wrote 15 MB and slept ten seconds delivered first content at 10,069 ms (R235-f1). With the author's library, single warm requests to deployed Bedrock Nova Lite stacks showed first bytes at 0.289 to 0.390 s of 7.5 to 8.2 s responses (R235-f3). The library's SSE module relies on undocumented runtime internals and supports only API Gateway REST (R235-f4).

  15. Choose the endpoint as well as the model. In a vendor index of OpenRouter traffic, the best of 41 endpoints for one model broke 0.06 percent of tool calls and the worst 7.65 percent, and a provider serving two quantizations or tiers counts as two endpoints (R027-f2). The index counts only syntax validity, and harness retries hide these failures as cost and latency (R027-f3).

Avoid

  • Quoting a speedup without its baseline and configuration. The 3.1x G-MTP figure is the 31B dense model at batch size 1; the 26B MoE got 1.34x (R215-f2). FreeToken's 1.5 to 2.3x is against the strongest baseline per workload, on rented servers capped at 6 to 8 CPU threads, and does not hold across every model or machine (R153-f2) (R153-f6).

  • Quoting the 21x Snapshot figure as a shipped result. It came from a proof-of-concept weight path striped over 8 local NVMe SSDs that depended on a pending CUDA driver patch (R096-f4). The May prototype supported only single-GPU vLLM and SGLang (R096-f7).

  • Reading vendor and customer figures as benchmarks. The Dynamo RL customer figures, up to 15x faster weight updates and up to 20 percent shorter step time, come with no baselines, hardware or run counts (R176-f3) (R176-f4). Cerebras's 5x throughput per megawatt comes from one normalised chart of one endpoint, without latency percentiles or GPU power draw (R240-f2) (R240-f5), and its SRAM and bandwidth ratios are given with no method (R240-f3). Partner figures for Snapshot are partner-reported and not independently measured (R096-f6).

  • Taking README speeds for consumer hardware at face value. Linger's 125B model at 45.5 tok/s on a 12 GB RTX 3060 is a single vendor figure with no conditions beyond card, RAM and file size (R166-f2) (R166-f5). PolyStrata's speeds come from its own benchmark (R166-f3), and its own-engine models are described as experimental (R166-f5). Linger's hardware tiers are paired with cloud models through an internal score (R166-f5).

  • Treating expert pruning as free. At 50 percent expert retention, Dynamic Expert Pruning resolved 30 percent on SWE-bench Verified with Coder-30B against 40 for the dense workflow, on 50 oracle-assisted instances in a single run with four dense instances hitting harness errors (R239-f3). On DeepSeek-V2.5-236B every policy tested, DEP included, was unusable at 25 percent retention (R239-f5).

  • Taking an average quantization gain as a gain for every model. On the MoE model QDA improved by only 1.3 and 0.1 points at W4A16 and W4A8 and ended 0.2 points below GSQ at W3A16 (R174-f6).

  • Adding inference capacity or concurrency without watching what it does elsewhere. More inference servers with unchanged batch size can raise staleness (R238-f3), and raising concurrency alone dropped cache reuse from 98.3 to 87.5 percent in the Ling runs (R115-f5).

  • Treating a calibrated estimator as validated. MLX Model Scout's agreement with measurements holds only for its calibration set, and it does not estimate prefill or time to first token (R162-f5).

  • Assuming that a sandboxed local server isolates everything. LocalAI 5.0 describes serving many model types through one API in OpenAI and Anthropic formats, with no GPU required, as the project's own description without benchmarks (R253-f1). In LocalAI 5.0, add-ons run as the operating-system user that runs LocalAI, and only the Sandbox add-on isolates what it runs (R253-f4).

How this was made

Twenty sources were used.

  • 17 in the serving set: papers or preprints (R115, R153, R174, R215, R239), vendor blogs and docs (R096, R176, R198, R206, R238, R240), project READMEs or release posts (R162, R166, R235, R253), a practitioner LinkedIn post with its comments (R051) and an interactive simulator (R180).

A Sonnet model extracted 87 claims for the serving set, each tied to a location in its source. Opus wrote this guide from those claims and the related claims.

Limits, and how they were weighted:

  • R051 is thin. It is a LinkedIn post read in full with the four comments that loaded, and it gives no numbers, benchmarks or citations (R051-f7). It is used only for definitions of techniques.

  • R180 is an interactive simulator read in the browser through its Long prompts, Batching and Model and sources panels. Its workload is illustrative, not a measurement (R180-f2), and its KV cache is logical occupancy with no physical pages or byte sizes (R180-f3). It is used only to illustrate scheduling policies.

  • R166 was read from the PolyStrata and Linger READMEs, with the per-model docs not read; its figures are vendor claims (R166-f5). R162 was read from its README, with code not run (R162-f1). R253 was read from its LocalAI 5.0 release post, which is the project's own description with nothing benchmarked (R253-f1); its README was not read, so its licence and star count are unchecked. These three are used for described designs and stated figures, not as independent evidence.

  • R115, R174, R215 and R239 were read through their main text, without appendices; two claims cite appendix material through main-text references (R174-f4) (R215-f4). R153 was read to the end of Section 5 plus its README.

  • Vendor material: R096, R176 and R198 are NVIDIA blogs (R096-f5) (R176-f1) (R198-f5), R206 is ADK documentation (R206-f1), R238 is Microsoft AI's own blog with no code or reproduction, read as article text without its figure images (R238-f1) (R238-f6), and R240 is a Cerebras blog with one quantitative chart (R240-f5). Re-reading the sources, several only in part as listed above, found several of our earlier entries wrong, overstated or unverifiable. For example:

  • R051's summary attributed head-of-line blocking and a 90 percent coverage figure to commenters; neither appears in the four comments that loaded (R051-f7). "Can lose at high batch" is not in the post (R051-f6), the post does not name TTFT or inter-token latency (R051-f5), and the byte-identical prefix and memory contention points were our additions to a post that speaks only of a shared token prefix (R051-f2).

  • R096's entry took its mechanism and headline numbers from the May prototype blog, while Ron Kahn's post links to the later November blog, which reports restores of 3.6 to 42.7 s and 8x to 36x (R096-f5). The 21x figure is a proof of concept gated on a pending driver patch (R096-f4). Our risk list included secrets in artifacts, which neither blog states; the stated limits are the matching GPU model and driver, the excluded provisioning and image pull, and the prototype's single-GPU scope (R096-f7).

  • R115's "10.4 to 65.0 percent with unchanged scores" mixed experiments: inference-only runs gave 10.4 to 37.5 and 20.9 to 21.9 percent (R115-f2), the large MCP-Atlas figures came mostly from capacity separation (R115-f3), and scores stayed aligned rather than unchanged (R115-f5).

  • R153's 1.5 to 2.3x is against the strongest baseline per workload on servers capped at 6 to 8 CPU threads (R153-f2) (R153-f6). The 753B GLM-5.2 result ran on an RTX PRO 6000 with 96 GB, not a consumer GPU (R153-f4), and the baselines' worst TTFT ranged from 179 to 946 s (R153-f3).

  • R166's licence note was wrong for PolyStrata, whose README says MIT (R166-f1). The 125B figure is Linger's, not PolyStrata's (R166-f2), and neither README says its speeds were simulated (R166-f5).

  • R174's 0.9 to 2.2 points are averages over four models, with small gains on the MoE model (R174-f2) (R174-f6), and our entry left out the 320B result (R174-f3) and the ModelOpt comparison (R174-f5).

  • R176's "cache affinity versus tail latency" overstated the post, which says only that the router balances reuse and load (R176-f2) (R176-f4).

  • R198's 47 percent is the indexer and Top-k together (R198-f1), its head dimension guideline is 128 or 256 (R198-f4), and its Table 1 compares indexer design choices rather than benchmarking those methods (R198-f5).

  • R206's "silently fall back" was imprecise: the fallback logs one warning per run (R206-f4). Our entry also left out that the discount has no figure (R206-f3), the Interactions API requirement (R206-f2), and the streaming, ManagedAgent and restart limits (R206-f4).

  • R215's 3.1x holds only for the 31B dense model (R215-f2). The clustered vocabulary projection nets 1.01x on the Pixel 11 Edge TPU (R215-f4), the paper does release drafter checkpoints and a pipeline (R215-f6), and the 1.83x is measured against tuning only the backbone (R215-f5).

  • R235's README carries only the Java run; the other timings are in the repository's streaming doc, and the Kotlin run is 5.2 percent of its response (R235-f3). The untested Lambda Managed Instances limit was missing (R235-f4).

  • R238's median SWE rollout of 93 minutes and 249 turns and its 1.13 million rollouts per day are not in the blog text (R238-f6). The 15 s transfer is for the current configuration and run (R238-f2), and the elasticity figures are Table 1 estimates from a portion of training (R238-f5).

  • R239's entry said no gap to the full model was stated, but the paper gives 75.9 against 82.2 at half the experts (R239-f2) (R239-f5), and it bounds swap cost analytically (R239-f4).

  • R240's summary points on prefill:decode ratio tuning, prefix-cache interaction and doubled failure domains are our inference, not the blog's (R240-f5).

  • R253's "no-network Sandbox" holds by default only (R253-f2), and the Apple silicon Sandbox package is not released (R253-f4).

  • R180's "reserved versus paged KV allocation" scenario was not found in the panels read (R180-f3).

  • R162's "within a few percent of measured" holds only for the calibration set (R162-f3) (R162-f5).

Those entries have been corrected at the source.

Field guide: Agent self-improvement

How agents that rewrite their own prompts, skills or harness actually improve, and the gates that keep them honest.

Claims read from the full papersUpdated 2026-11-24

Bottom line

Self-improvement loops pay off when something outside the model decides which edits are kept: held-out tasks, a noise-aware acceptance test, checks the optimizer cannot see, and a record of each edit, its diagnosis and its outcome. Without that, loops keep bad edits and learn to game their evaluator.

The upside is real. One editing step with no ground-truth verifier lifted GLM-5 from 64.2 to 75.7, past a stronger model on its original harness (R121-f1, R121-f2). A harness written once by GPT-5.6-Sol took Llama3-8B from 15.14 to 79.32 on long-context tasks, above a model trained for 128K context (R025-f1). So are the risks. A greedy keep-if-better gate kept non-improving updates 45.59% of the time (R066-f1). A harness evolved on a stronger model scored below the unevolved seed on a weaker one in all four domains, one run each (R131-f1). And of 131 ideas agents proposed for a live RL run, 110 were grading or data fixes; none of the 3 learning-rule ideas was adopted (R114-f7).

Expect loops to find fixes and tuning, not new methods, and spend your effort on the gate.

Do

  1. Gate every kept edit on evidence the model did not produce. A statistical noninferiority gate cut non-improving acceptances from 45.59% to 2.78% and mean worst drawdown from 4.963 to 0.047 points (R066-f1, R066-f5). A regression gate on memory and rule edits reached 0.924 update correctness with 0.167 pollution (R164-f2). InfraRSI promotes a candidate only after back-to-back baseline re-runs beat measured noise, and otherwise reports 1.00x (R024-f5).

  2. Keep evaluators private and add checks outside the model. Exposing the evaluator raised the original benchmark score by 0.093 but the out-of-distribution score by only 0.009 (R103-f4). An offline verifier rejected 400 of 1,212 stored runs, 39 of them caught only by the numerical layer (R024-f3). Stress-input checks dropped a 1.62x kernel that overflowed in FP16 (R129-f5).

  3. Carry a record of each edit, its diagnosis and its outcome. With that log, continuation beat harness-only continuation by 3.60 to 11.33 points across models (R131-f3); over ten independent coding runs the gain was 2.36 points (95% CI 1.05 to 3.68), positive in nine (R131-f5). Root-cause notes without outcomes ranged from 1.82 points worse to 2.40 better (R131-f4).

  4. Give the editor reference runs, probes and a narrow scope. Removing scoped editors cost 2.9 and 3.7 points (R121-f5); editing without reference runs, probes or library dropped Code from 73.4 to 63.2 (R121-f6).

  5. Specialize, but keep the base harness as a route. A hierarchy of specialized harnesses beat the base on all three held-out benchmarks, 77.41 vs 61.62 on AutomationBench (R108-f1). Routing worked better when the router saw the base run (R108-f4), and keeping the base as an option protects unfamiliar tasks (R108-f7).

  6. Optimize for accuracy first, then prune. Pruning straight from the seed skill stalled at 3.6% on SpreadsheetBench against 72.5% when it followed accuracy optimization (R221-f4). Against accuracy-only optimization, the two-phase order cut output tokens in 11 of 12 model-benchmark pairs, by up to 79.7% (one pair rose 4.4%) (R221-f1), and serving cost by 25 to 67% in 10 of 12 (R221-f5).

  7. Use a decision model as a teacher, not an actor. Acting, it finished 42.9% of ALFWorld tasks; its scores, added to GRPO, raised a 1.5B policy from 72.8% to 82.9% (the GRPO baseline is taken from prior work) (R069-f6, R069-f5). Ask whether an action helped, not whether the task will succeed (R069-f3).

  8. Let agents supervise long runs. A role-specialized supervisor repaired a deliberately flawed RL recipe, 65.53% to 73.16% while the unchanged run fell to 56.05% (R114-f3), and kept a 14.7-day run going through 11 failures (R114-f4).

Avoid

  • Greedy keep-if-better gates. They kept non-improving updates 45.59% of the time (R066-f1), and in a greedy case study (an appendix not read in detail) higher validation scores did not imply higher test scores (R066-f8).

  • Gates so strict nothing passes. Cross-round gates that bound any false acceptance accepted none, or three across all runs (R066-f3).

  • Trusting the model's forecast of what will help. Zero-shot speedup predictions correlated -0.49 to -0.06 with measured results; graph compilation was predicted at +2.0% and measured at +55% (R024-f2).

  • Moving an evolved harness to a new model without its history. It started below the seed in every domain (R131-f1) and, even after target optimization, ended below a cold start in 3 of 4 (R131-f2). Even carrying harness and memory across model generations beat a fresh run on two of three and lost 0.8 on the newest (R131-f6).

  • Telling the optimizer too much about the evaluation. Matching the test's turn and token budgets raised one split and lowered another (R144-f6).

  • Aggressive self-rewriting of memory. RRSI and Meta-Harness polluted memory at 0.500 (1.000 on procedural families) vs 0.167 for GEPA, Hermes+ and GUARD (R164-f1).

  • Buying more inference instead of improvement. Best-of-K on the base harness ranged from slightly worse to much better by benchmark (R108-f3).

  • Leaving out the optimizer's cost. Meta-optimizer calls added 1.80 to 9.36 dollars per run and dominated offline cost on cheap runs (R221-f7); a written harness cost $541.47 to develop, below training baselines but above the self-improvement baseline (R025-f5).

How this was made

Fifteen sources were read on 2026-11-23: fourteen arXiv papers in their HTML versions and Warp's article. A Sonnet model extracted 120 claims, each tied to a section, table or figure; R164's claims come from the agent memory guide's reading. Opus wrote this guide from those claims.

Limits. R063, R066, R103, R114, R121, R131 and R144 were read in the sections holding their results, not end to end. The R129 text ended at §5.1, so its limitations section was not read. Appendices were not read. R229 is a vendor story with no before-and-after numbers (R229-f7). Most results are single author-run experiments.

The full read showed our entries were off for 14 of the 15 sources. For example, R066's 2.78% is an average over six settings, and greedy had the higher final gain in two of them; R069's "three general LLMs" were two readouts of one model; and R175 declined 20 of 21 attempts, not 21. Those entries are being corrected at the source and logged in the corpus corrections file. A second reader then checked every cited sentence against its claim, and 17 overstatements were fixed.

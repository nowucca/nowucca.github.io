Field guide: Multi-agent and harness design

When to use one agent or many, how to coordinate them, and how to design the harness: loops, tools, skills, sandboxes, state, budgets and evaluation.

Claims read from the full papersUpdated 2026-11-24

Bottom line

Start with one agent in a plain loop, and add agents only where the work has a phase that needs separate fresh contexts or wide parallel search. Measure any multi-agent gain at a matched token budget, and design coordination explicitly rather than expecting it to appear. Then treat the harness as part of the system you are evaluating, because the same model can score very differently in different harnesses.

The sources make this case through a mix of vendor guidance, a source-code survey and a few experiments run by the people who built or benchmarked the systems, not all of them budget matched. Anthropic's article on agent design says a single agent with skills frequently matched or beat both one big prompt and a subagent design on quality, often at lower cost and latency, but it publishes no dataset (R132-f1). Wavestone's survey of eleven agent systems (R054-f1) recommends staying single-agent until a concrete breadth-first phase where parallel isolation beats serial search, citing about 15 times the tokens for the parallel route (R054-f4). That survey reads source code and runs no head-to-head comparisons (R054-f1), and only part of the paper was read for this guide.

The most striking multi-agent gain in these sources is also the clearest budget problem. In Anthropic Frontier Red Team's own experiment, a 45-agent swarm found 266 vulnerabilities against 21 for independent agents, but the swarm used 27M tokens against 6.5M, about four times as many (R100-f1). Roughly half the swarm's findings lay outside the core directories, and restricted to those directories the tokens spent per vulnerability were comparable (R100-f2). Anthropic's own conclusion from the same study is that coordination does not emerge from stronger intelligence on its own and has to be designed (R100-f6).

The harness matters on its own. In one benchmark run by its authors, from one run per agent per task, GLM 5.3 scored a Singularity-Index of 0.656 in Claude Code and 0.373 in Codex, while DeepSeek V4.1 Flash went the other way, 0.697 in Codex against 0.487, and Codex configs of GLM, MiniMax and Kimi accounted for 5 of 8 failed submissions (R086-f8). In another, with one attempt per test and cross-provider runs going through an adapter, GPT-6 Astra solved 34% of core tests in Codex and 22% in Claude Code, while Gemini 4 Argon scored 29% in Codex, 25% in Claude Code and 24% in Antigravity CLI (R086-f13). In CoEvoBench, run by its authors, harness changes carried most of the gain on four of five tasks (R233-f2).

Several sources were read only in part, two are thin, and many are vendor material. "How this was made" lists them, and claims from thinly read sources carry less weight below.

Do

  1. Start with one loop and add skills before agents. Wavestone's first recommendation is a linear while loop, citing Mini-SWE-Agent's 74% plus on SWE-bench Verified, a figure that project reports about itself (R054-f4). No system in its survey imports an agent framework (R054-f1). Anthropic reports one customer saw roughly 2x faster runtime after moving to a single loop, and that Claude Code's system prompt was cut by over 80% using skills, both vendor-reported (R132-f2).

  2. Split by context, not by problem type. Anthropic recommends subagents only for context bloat, parallel fresh-context work, or tool selection and conflicting instructions (R132-f3). Subagents do not help every model. On one inference task with one run per setting, requiring subagents raised Opus 5.5 from 7.31 to 10.32 but cut GPT-6.1 Sol from 9.70 to 2.09 (R086-f9).

  3. Design the coordination; role prompts did little, and even the best-coordinating model fell short of its solo ceiling. In Anthropic's 12-hour game-building runs, prescriptive roles and CEO hierarchy prompts made little difference (R100-f3). In hidden-profile tasks, Mythos 5 groups reached about 85% while other models reached 17 to 36%, even though solo ceilings were near 100%, in 4-agent groups with n=400 episodes per model in Anthropic's own runs (R100-f5). The s3 authors trained coordination into small-model teams with a shared team reward and a token-cost term in the reward (R019-f6). In their own experiment a trained team reached 93% at four agents, against 35% for online SFT and 13% for individual reward (R019-f1). Their stated limits are two tasks, small models and one training run per condition (R019-f6).

  4. Let an external checker decide what is accepted. DeepMind's agent teams count a conjecture only once an external Lean checker has verified it against a frozen specification (R237-f1). On pequalnp.com the Lean checker is the only judge, and human review is a separate state (R251-f3). In the open science study, a guest editor ran 276 objections as scripts, and 77 of 156 major objections overturned the headline conclusion, according to the authors (R181-f2). That study was read without its PDF.

  5. One example: merging parallel reviews by checking rather than voting. In one practitioner's LinkedIn post, read from a paraphrased capture, a synthesizer checked every disagreement and every solo high-severity claim from three model families against the code and docs, dropped 2 false positives and kept single-model catches (R227-f3). The post reports 41 verified findings with no cost and no human check of the solo finds (R227-f4). This is a single thinly sourced anecdote. A separate extraction study found that three-sample voting used 411,000 to 432,000 tokens per run and changed net correct codes by only -1 to +1, on 40 development documents over three runs, but its other reliability layers, self-correction included, also corrected almost none, so it does not show that checking works better (R039-f1).

  6. Keep shared state durable, versioned and revertable. In the Pro2 authors' ablation on MLE-Bench Lite, removing the Git-backed Codebase State dropped the final medal rate from 89.4% to 56.1% (R200-f3). DeepMind's teams share a wiki of verified solutions, lemmas, reviewed notes and dead ends (R237-f1). Vercel's board treats claims as time-limited leases, for example five minutes (R177-f1). Vercel's integration is pull-only, so posting does not wake an idle agent and wake-ups need your own runtime (R177-f3).

  7. Put retries, waits and rule changes in the harness, not the runtime alone. Prakash's article says a durable runtime handles a dead process, a failed remote call, repeated work on retry and concurrency, but it cannot make a retry safe (R035-f1). Under at-least-once semantics the harness must keep the idempotency key constant across attempts (R035-f2). The article assigns five more failure modes to the harness, including re-checking the world before committing after a wait and versioning rules (R035-f3). In the author's own toy runs, a mid-wait rule change left Temporal continuing under the old rule silently while Cloudflare failed and left the order as a draft (R035-f5).

  8. Know exactly what the sandbox covers. In Claude Code's experimental Code Mode, the sandbox covers shell commands only, while the JavaScript and its file, web and MCP calls run outside it, so hardening depends on permissions and hooks (R214-f4). Each inner call still passes deny and ask rules and PreToolUse hooks, but in auto mode the classifier reviews the code, not each call (R214-f3). A security vendor relays a finding that seven CLI coding agents run git status or git diff at startup, some before the trust prompt, so a repo-local setting can execute a command outside any sandbox (R112-f2). Wavestone recommends an OS-level sandbox plus policy-as-code (R054-f5). NVIDIA's OpenShell documentation describes a boundary check intended for agents, for example a parent agent checking a subagent's policy (R030-f3), and says a passing check does not mean the policy is narrow or enforced by a running sandbox (R030-f5).

  9. Give each agent its own credential and scope. Omnigraph's release notes tell operators to check what each agent credential allows, because MCP agents now gain every tool their policy allows, and to give a read-only agent its own credential (R074-f2). The MCP authorization spec requires servers to reject tokens not issued for them and not to pass other tokens through (R062-f2), but authorization is optional and the spec says stdio implementations should not follow it (R062-f5). Vercel's factory takes direction from board and Slack writers and people with GitHub write access, and discusses rather than obeys a stranger's issue on a public repo (R177-f5).

  10. Keep the visible tool and skill set small. Wavestone recommends deferring tool loading past about 15 tools (R054-f5). Anthropic expects tool selection to degrade past 30 to 50 definitions, as guidance it did not measure in the article (R132-f4). In a routing stress test by the ACES authors, skill lift stayed between 0.133 and 0.149 for 1 to 20 visible skills while wall time rose from 258 s to 451 s, and at 50 visible skills the pass rate fell to 0.55 (R064-f7). In Wavestone's survey, skills appear in 9 of 11 systems and MCP in 8 of 11 (R054-f3).

  11. Price tokens and time into the loop, and decide when to stop. The s3 reward multiplies success by a token-cost penalty, which is how its authors train coordination instead of prompting it (R019-f6). A Lean prover's control plane attempts again only when predicted quality minus a cost term is positive (R042-f5). Stopping early is a measured risk too: in Infrathon, a stop hook against premature completion yielded gains of 24.8%, 7.3% and 14.0% in three runs (R086-f4), and 135 of 150 runs, one per task-model pair in the benchmark authors' own runs, set a new best score in the second half of their budget (R086-f1). Code Mode stops code after 10 minutes if waited on, and background code can run up to 2 hours (R214-f5).

  12. Evaluate the harness and model as one pair, and run each task more than once. LiteLLM's agent_eval makes the (harness, model) pair the unit of comparison (R254-f1), and its launch blog warns that comparing Claude Code and Codex with different models changes both at once (R254-f4). That API is flagged as an unreleased preview (R254-f1) and the blog scorecard uses mocked answers (R254-f4). In Anthropic's worked example, folding one subagent into another lowered latency but broke a process that had passed, which is why the article says to run each task more than once (R132-f5). Anthropic also suggests starting with about 10 golden examples and tracking routing accuracy while an orchestrator exists (R132-f4).

  13. Make failures visible in traces. A tool-call index notes that harness retries hide invalid tool calls as cost and latency (R027-f3). For one model, the best of 41 provider endpoints broke 0.06% of tool calls and the worst 7.65% (R027-f2). LiteLLM's evaluator keeps errors in the pass-rate denominator and reports agent and judge cost separately (R254-f2). In Code Mode, OpenTelemetry events and metrics work on v2.1.329 or later, and only OTel tracing (beta) must be off (R214-f5).

Avoid

  • Comparing a swarm against a single agent at unequal spend. The 266 vs 21 result came with 27M vs 6.5M tokens (R100-f1), and restricted to core directories tokens per vulnerability were comparable (R100-f2). Pro2's PostTrainBench rows are not budget matched either: published rows used a 10-hour H100 budget against 24 hours on an A100 (R200-f4).

  • Reading self-reported benchmark figures as independent results. Mini-SWE-Agent's 74% plus is that project's own figure (R054-f4). Pro2's MLE-Bench medal rates are the authors' own single run per budget (R200-f1), and its PostTrainBench score of 30.69 sits below the best published 41.79 (R200-f4).

  • Letting agents share a back-channel or converge unchecked. In Anthropic's own runs, 18 of 30 agents chose the same branch name, job-queue agents sent 2.4M requests for 117 accepted jobs, and pricing agents with a back-channel colluded by round 3 (R100-f4).

  • Trusting an autorater's margin for multi-agent output. PaperOrchestra's authors found weak human and autorater agreement on literature review (Pearson 0.087 and 0.276), so autorater margins of 88 to 99% overstate the human result (R077-f5). Their simulated acceptance was 81 to 84% under one reviewer and 22 to 48% under another (R077-f4). In the open science study, the pooled LLM-judge rigor advantage for agent studies came entirely from one lab (R181-f4).

  • Taking a checker pass as proof that the statement means what you meant. The pequalnp.com checker cannot tell whether a statement says what its plain sentence says (R251-f5). DeepMind's authors caution that specification quality limits what agents find and report a Toffoli-counting loophole (R237-f4).

  • Keeping ordering and safety rules only in prose. FairyClaw's authors argue that instructions in a skill document are suggestions the runtime cannot enforce, from one case study and no ablation (R044-f5).

  • Dropping a reference harness onto trained weights without testing it. In CoEvoBench, the reference harness lifted Instruct from 30.2% to 37.8% but dropped trained weights from 19.8% to 2.6% (R233-f4).

  • Turning a vendor feature into a design rule. Code Mode is experimental, off by default and has no published performance numbers on the docs page read (R214-f1) (R214-f6). Resolve AI's customer outcomes are unquantified (R031-f4), and its case for multi-agent investigation is an argument with no experiment (R031-f1).

  • Comparing numbers across machines. The LangChain 101 manual says so for its own bench (R218-f2), though only its README was read.

How this was made

Twenty-eight sources were used.

  • 18 in the multi-agent and harness set: papers or preprints, including a source-code survey (R019, R054, R077, R181, R200, R233, R237), vendor articles, docs and repositories (R031, R100, R132, R177, R214, R245, R254), a manual (R218), a practitioner article on a toy domain (R035), a live project site (R251) and a single anecdote in a LinkedIn post (R227).

  • 10 related sources from the other sets, used only where they bear on harness or multi-agent choices: R042 and R044 (papers, R044 also an authors' design argument) from verification, R027 (a vendor index), R039 (a non-peer-reviewed article), R064 and R086 (papers) from evals, and R030 (vendor docs), R062 (the MCP specification), R074 (vendor release notes) and R112 (a vendor relay of third-party findings) from security.

A Sonnet model extracted 89 claims for the multi-agent set, each tied to a location in its source. Opus wrote this guide from those claims and the related claims.

Limits, and how they were weighted:

  • R218 is thin: only the manual's README was read, so its specific numbers are unverified (R218-f1). It is cited once, for its own advice not to compare numbers across machines (R218-f2).

  • R227 is thin: it is one author's anecdote read from a paraphrased capture of a LinkedIn post, not the live post (R227-f1). It is used only as an example of synthesis by verification, not as evidence that the method works.

  • R181 was read from the abstract, the authors' thread, the alphaXiv overview, the transcript and comments, without the PDF (R181-f5). Its figures are the authors' reported numbers at summary level (R181-f6).

  • R054 was read in part (abstract, introduction, section 11, parts of 13 and 15, and section 16). Its counts come from reading source code with no head-to-head runs (R054-f1), and its recommendations are used as one survey's advice (R054-f4) (R054-f5).

  • R077 and R233 were read through their main text with appendices partly or not read, R200 through section 4.5 without its case studies or discussion, and R237 for its first ~50K characters without appendices (R200-f5) (R237-f4).

  • Vendor material: R100, R132, R214 and R245 are Anthropic's own studies or docs (R100-f6) (R132-f1) (R214-f6) (R245-f1), R031 is a vendor blog with no experiment (R031-f1), R177 is Vercel's own description (R177-f2), and R254 is an unreleased API preview (R254-f1) with a mocked scorecard (R254-f4). They are used for practices and stated figures, not as independent evidence.

  • R035's numbers come from the author's own runs on a toy Python domain (R035-f1).

Re-reading the sources, several only in part as listed above, found several of our earlier entries wrong, overstated or unverifiable. For example:

  • R100's "mostly by covering more code (12 overlap)": the source says about half the findings lay outside the core directories, tokens per vulnerability were comparable inside them, and 12 is the count common to both arms (R100-f2). Our entry also left out the 27M vs 6.5M token difference (R100-f1).

  • R132's "single agent beat the alternatives on quality, cost and latency": the article says quality frequently and lower cost and latency often (R132-f1).

  • R214's "no OTel export" was wrong: OpenTelemetry events and metrics work on v2.1.329 or later, and only OTel tracing must be off (R214-f5).

  • R054's "1,000x larger" and "loop size predicts production readiness" were not found in the sections read, and the 74% figure is Mini-SWE-Agent's own (R054-f4).

  • R077's "simulated acceptance 81 to 84%" holds only under the ScholarPeer reviewer, and its refinement also accepts ties with non-negative sub-axis gains (R077-f4) (R077-f6).

  • R200's "competitive on PostTrainBench" left out that Pro2 scores 30.69 against a best published 41.79 at a different budget (R200-f4).

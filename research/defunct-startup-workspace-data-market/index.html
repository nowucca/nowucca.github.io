Your Slack is an asset in insolvency: the market in defunct companies' workspace data

Filed 27 Nov 2026 from reporting published 13 October 2026 (MIT Technology Review, Matthew King), surfaced by a fresh post from the publication. Figures below are the article's, sourced to named people or to linked reporting from Reuters, Bloomberg and Carta; where the article asserts something without a source, this entry says so.

What the market is

When a startup winds down, its code and its workspaces are now inventory. Two wind-down service companies — one of which added data sales in April through an "Asset Hub" — broker the sale of code bases, Google Workspace, Notion, Figma and Slack archives, email, chats, documents and calendars to AI labs, to vendors that build simulated workspaces for training, and to large tech companies training their own models. One broker reports over 150 deals, with 47% of its deals now including a code or data sale, and expects to return over $10 million to startups by the end of 2026. A founder closing a two-and-a-half-year-old company received roughly $25,000 for the code base and expects about $15,000 more for the workspace data.

The supply is growing because shutdowns are: estimates the article links put the annual rise between 26% and 56%.

The bankruptcy channel is larger and less consensual. In August 2026, Google paid $10 million for Spirit Airlines data in the bankruptcy sale, outbidding another buyer. The lot: 100 million emails, 500 million Teams messages, 176,000 employee records. The flight attendants' union objected on privacy grounds. The article does not say whether a court imposed conditions.

Why a buyer wants it

The stated use is building reinforcement-learning gyms for agents — environments where an agent practises on a real organisation's accumulated work rather than a generated one. The buyer-side argument is that synthetic workspaces contain tells: references to dates that do not exist, internally inconsistent histories, the smoothness of something written in one pass. A real archive carries the texture that makes a task hard in the way real tasks are hard: half-finished threads, decisions reversed three weeks later, the one person who knows why the migration was abandoned.

Valuation is unsettled and openly so. Buyers price against "research theses" estimating how many useful training tasks come out of, say, 100,000 lines of code, and the article reports that quotes vary wildly between buyers. One buyer's view is that a single company's Slack can be worth more than another company's Slack, email and calendars combined — heterogeneity matters more than volume.

The claim that is not evidenced

Two load-bearing assertions in this story have no measurement behind them, and the article is honest about the second one.

That labs are buying because they ran out of data. This is asserted — "hit the limits of public and synthetic datasets" — with no figure or source.

That real archives train better than synthetic ones. The article states plainly that the extent of any performance gain is unclear and that no company has published a comparison of real against alternative training data. So a market with named prices has formed around a hypothesis nobody has tested in public. That is worth holding in mind when the pricing is cited as evidence of value: the prices measure belief, not yield.

The governance gap

The legal position, from the lawyers the article quotes, is that this data is "legally radioactive" and that no federal law bans the sale. The argument against it is about the basis of consent rather than about privacy in the abstract: workplace monitoring is justified by helping people do their jobs, and that justification ends when the company closes. The employees whose archives are sold are not the beneficiaries — proceeds flow to senior creditors and investors — and nobody asked them. One quoted position is that an employer should need express consent before selling workers' data.

Both sides report scrubbing PII and excluding HR records. Those are claims by the parties, not audited findings, and the Spirit lot included 176,000 employee records.

The article offers no advice on prevention, and notes founders have little visibility into who buys or what they plan.

Review checklist

This is a supply-chain and contract question long before it is a privacy question. Things worth having an answer to:

  1. Does any contract you have with a vendor, customer or partner say what happens to the data if they are wound down or sold for parts? Most say what happens on termination; far fewer say what happens in insolvency, where the estate, not the counterparty, decides.

  2. Is your own workspace data an asset a receiver could sell? Slack, Teams, email, ticket histories, design files and repositories, including every customer and employee detail inside them. If so, it is an asset with a price, not just a liability with a retention policy.

  3. Does your data-retention policy shrink the lot? Retention limits are usually justified as reducing breach exposure and discovery cost. They also reduce what survives to be sold. A seven-year Slack history is a seven-year asset.

  4. Who are your processors' processors? A wind-down broker is not in anyone's subprocessor list, and it appears only at the moment nobody is managing the relationship.

  5. If you buy such data for training or evals, what is the provenance record? Which entity sold it, under what authority, what was represented as scrubbed, and whether that was verified rather than asserted. Expect to be asked, and expect "legally radioactive" to become a diligence question rather than a lawyer's aside.

  6. If you train on it, where is your comparison? Nobody has published real-versus-synthetic results. If real archives are worth a premium to you, that is a measurement you have to make yourself, and it is cheap relative to the price being quoted.

Related entries

The obvious neighbours are the synthetic alternatives: simulated enterprise tenants and generated company data exist precisely to supply this kind of environment without acquiring a real organisation's history, and the cost argument between the two is now a market price rather than an engineering preference. The trace-and-outcome-store work is the other side of it — the data most organisations are sitting on and treating as exhaust is the same substance that is changing hands here.

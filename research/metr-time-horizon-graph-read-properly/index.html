The time-horizon graph, read properly: 40 hours with a 13-to-2645 interval, and a reliability ceiling of 16

Published 2026-11-20Summarized 2026-11-28

Read the original

Source: "AI R&D-relevant model capabilities are improving rapidly and are getting harder to measure", METR, 20 Nov 2026 — Kinniment, Shetty, Goodrich, Putz, Bondera, Huang, Jurkovic, Gabor, Sykes, Saurous and Barnes. What follows covers the full body and Appendices A–C; Appendices D–O are not covered. The older rolling page metr.org/time-horizons (last updated 8 Sep 2026, "no longer actively updated") and the TH1.1 data file supply the comparison figures.

The headline, and the interval attached to it

The 50% time horizon — the human-expert task duration at which an agent is predicted to succeed half the time — is 40 hours for GPT-6 Astra, with a 95% confidence interval of 13 to 2,645 hours. Opus 5.5 is 39 hours, 12 to 1,253. The point estimates get quoted; the intervals span two orders of magnitude.

That is not a criticism of METR, who publish the intervals plainly. It is a statement about what the number can carry. A quantity whose upper bound is 66× its point estimate cannot support a planning decision, and the report's own framing is about measurement getting harder, not about a milestone being reached.

Two further things sit in the same appendix and change the reading:

METR's stated reliability ceiling is below its own headline. The time-horizons page says "Measurements above 16 hrs are unreliable with our current task suite." The headline is 40. The report is not hiding this — it is the reason the paper's title is about measurement difficulty — but any citation of the 40 should carry it.

The headline is the loosest of three estimates. Two sensitivity analyses in Appendix B give 29–32 hours with far tighter intervals: 13–120 and 12–111 respectively, against the headline's 13–2,645. METR's own summary is that "these alternate estimates reduce time horizon estimates and their CIs for recent models." A reader choosing one number should probably choose about 30 hours with an interval an order of magnitude narrower.

The doubling time

"Time horizons are doubling roughly every 3.61 months (95% CI 2.08–4.94), or 110 days." The 110-day conversion is METR's own, pooled across the TH1.1 and TH2 suites. For context the report gives a prior TH1.1 trend of 105 days, and the original 2025 result was around seven months.

The acceleration reading, which is what travels, is the part METR declines to assert. On their index metric: "we do not observe a strong acceleration in capability across public frontier models." The faster doubling on the TH2 verifiable subset (1.65 months, CI 0.79–3.99) "could be taken as evidence of an acceleration," but "there are also other plausible explanations," and they note comparable speeds on an earlier suite in the second half of 2025. They also flag that their estimation method "may 'miss' an actual acceleration" and that they are "uncertain about the validity of these slope changes."

So: horizons are growing fast, and whether growth is speeding up is explicitly unsettled by this report in both directions.

Method, in enough detail to judge it

A logistic curve of success probability against human task duration is fitted per agent, and the horizon is where it crosses 50%. Agents run in a ReAct scaffold with bash, python and image tools at "xhigh or higher" reasoning effort, about 4.7 scored runs per agent-task pair. Scores are combined through a METR Capability Index built on a two-parameter IRT model, with intervals from a hierarchical bootstrap over task families and then runs.

The suites:

TH1.1222 tasks (SWAA, HCAST, RE-Bench)

TH2 splits into a verifiable part (8 families, 31 tasks — combinatorial optimisation, Budget NanoGPT, a Minecraft bot, Reverse Conway, game-playing agents) and a hard-to-verify and judgment part (4 families, 5 tasks).

The single most important methodological caveat is about the x-axis, not the y-axis: human baselines "are directly used for only 9% of our estimates." The rest are researcher estimates of how long a task would take an expert, and METR's own verdict is "we consider these estimates quite speculative." The horizon is a curve fitted against a mostly-estimated independent variable.

The judgment finding, and how much it can bear

METR defines judgment as "the ability to make good choices in the absence of clear and fast empirical feedback." The contrast they draw is real and well put: "Models usually match or outperform skilled humans on our coding and optimization tasks," while "AI agents appear to have worse judgment than human experts."

The evidence is thin by their own account. Five tasks. No per-model scores published. No numeric coding-versus-judgment gap stated. And METR volunteers that "models are underelicited on these kinds of problems." This is a direction worth tracking, not a measured deficit.

Two concrete results inside it are more quotable than the headline, and neither is getting attention:

Models are lenient judges of other models. "Opus 5.5 … scores Astra's tasks on average about 0.2 (out of 1) more highly than our human." On a task scoring critiques of philosophical arguments against expert agreement, "all public models are still below the estimated agreement level of expert humans spending 15 minutes." A fifth of the scale, in the generous direction, is a large bias for anyone using a model as a grader.

Lifecycle cost predicts the capability gap. METR defines lifecycle cost as what it takes them to build a containerised environment for a task and then run and score it 100 times. A subset 2.9× more expensive has models performing 2.9× worse — "equivalent to 1.5 doublings" — and each doubling of lifecycle cost delays 50% success by approximately 4.6 months. That turns "realistic tasks are harder" from an intuition into a measured exchange rate between how expensive a task is to construct and how far behind models are on it. It is the most transferable finding in the report.

The cost figures behind that are worth knowing on their own: human baselines at "$200–350/hr" and "roughly $90k for 7 attempted baselines"; swarm experiments at "approximately $500k in tokens"; and an evaluation that "typically takes at least 1-2 weeks of calendar time."

METR stepping back from its own metric

The most consequential paragraphs are the ones qualifying the whole enterprise:

  • "We are increasingly concerned that our easy-to-run, containerized evals could overestimate real-world capabilities, due to similar RL environments becoming common in training." Contamination here is not memorised answers but similar environments in training — which is much harder to detect and impossible to rule out from outside.

  • "it is now more difficult to bound risk by pointing to some easy-to-measure and broad incapability." That is a safety-case argument being withdrawn, by the organisation that supplied it.

  • "our tasks are much 'cleaner' than real economically valuable labor," and the suite is "primarily composed of software engineering, machine learning, or cybersecurity tasks."

  • And the standing correction to the popular reading: the 50% horizon "is a measure of the difficulty of a task, rather than the time an AI spends." It does not mean an agent works unsupervised for 40 hours.

Naive extrapolation of the fitted line "would imply … 200 hours … in July 2027," which the report states and does not endorse, alongside "We remain significantly uncertain about central questions."

Failure modes when citing this

  • Quoting the point estimate without the interval. 40 hours with 13–2,645 attached is a different claim from 40 hours.

  • Quoting a figure above the suite's own reliability ceiling without saying the authors put that ceiling at 16 hours.

  • Picking the headline over the sensitivity analyses when the authors say the alternates tighten the estimate.

  • Reading the horizon as autonomy duration. It is task difficulty measured in human time, not agent runtime.

  • Treating the judgment result as measured. Five tasks, no per-model numbers, authors flagging underelicitation.

  • Reporting acceleration. Not established on the index metric; the one faster subset has explanations the authors prefer not to choose between.

  • Assuming the task suite generalises. Software, ML and security, deliberately clean, with a possible RL-environment overlap the authors raise themselves.

Review checklist

  1. What is the confidence interval, and what is the ratio of its upper bound to the point estimate? Above roughly 3×, the point estimate is a direction, not a quantity.

  2. Does the headline figure sit inside the measurement's own stated reliable range? Check for a ceiling stated elsewhere in the same work.

  3. How many of the x-axis values are measured rather than estimated? Here, 9%.

  4. If sensitivity analyses exist, do they tighten or widen the estimate? Prefer the tighter one when the authors say so.

  5. How many tasks support the secondary finding? A qualitative contrast on five tasks is a hypothesis.

  6. Is a model grading the output? If so, look for the measured leniency — here about 0.2 on a 0–1 scale.

  7. Is the acceleration claim the authors' or the reader's? Check whether the authors' own index metric shows it.

  8. What would contamination look like here? Similar training environments, not identical items, is the modern form and cannot be ruled out externally.

  9. What does the benchmark cost to build? If construction cost correlates with model shortfall — 4.6 months per doubling here — then cheap benchmarks systematically flatter.

  10. Is the data published? Analysis code and the earlier suite's data are, under CC BY 4.0; the newer task contents are not.

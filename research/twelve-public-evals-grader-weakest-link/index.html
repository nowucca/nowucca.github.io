The claim that should change how you read a leaderboard

Eval scores are usually the first thing anyone learns about a new model. A 42-minute teardown of twelve public benchmarks — four on coding and science, six on knowledge work, two on browsing and computer use — arrives at a finding that deserves to be quoted precisely:

in eight of twelve public evals studied, there's evidence the grader failed work that should have passed.

That is not a claim about model capability. It is a claim that a meaningful fraction of reported failures are defects in the measuring instrument. The author synthesises this figure from third-party audits and issue trackers and does not name which eight, which is the one place the headline outruns the evidence presented. The underlying per-benchmark numbers, however, are specific and attributed, and they are what makes the article worth reading.

How these benchmarks are built

Four components recur across almost every task:

  • Instruction — a prompt written to sound like a real user request. The agent receives it once and usually cannot ask clarifying questions.

  • Environment — the workspace. For coding, a container with a repo pinned at a commit; for knowledge work, a VM with software, simulated business apps, or a document folder.

  • Grader — deterministic code (tests or assertions over final state), an LLM judge scoring binary rubric items, or a mix.

  • Reference (optional) — an expert solution, used to prove solvability, anchor pairwise comparison, or set a human baseline.

These suites are now maintained like software, with versioned releases. Agents' Last Exam v1.1 retired 30 tasks and revised 57. One legal benchmark's public set grew from about 1,250 tasks to 2,010. Terminal Bench drops saturated tasks each version — good for headroom, bad for comparing across versions, and the article's suggestion of a companion score on a frozen subset is the obvious fix.

The design principles the field has converged on

Deterministic checks over LLM judges. Code checks reproduce and do not shift when a judge model is swapped. DeepSWE and AutomationBench avoid LLM judges entirely; Agents' Last Exam is about 93% deterministic. Where a judge is unavoidable, it is narrowed to binary yes/no rubric items rather than Likert scales.

Grade the outcome, not the steps. Terminal Bench inspects final container state and ignores intermediate commands; AutomationBench, OSWorld 2.0 and DeepSWE work the same way.

All-pass scoring, because partial work is often unusable. One legal benchmark's stated rationale is that there is no partial credit for catching most of the issues. The cost is brittleness: a single flawed assertion fails a correct solution. FrontierSWE and HealthBench Professional use weighted partial credit instead; Agents' Last Exam, Finance Agent and OSWorld 2.0 report both.

Mine for difficulty to slow saturation. AutomationBench hardened easy tasks with irrelevant data, similarly-named wrong records and conflicting policies. HealthBench Professional made difficult tasks roughly 3.5× more common than in its candidate pool. BrowseComp kept only questions the strongest models of the day could not solve.

What models actually get wrong

Two failure families dominate, and both are about diligence rather than capability.

Skipping verification and stopping early. OSWorld 2.0 agents submit without checking final state and spend under 7% of their budget on repair. On the legal benchmark, drafting without review cost 1.2 points on average while revising after a check gained 1.5. AutomationBench models process part of a list — a few of twelve emails — then summarise as if finished. The starkest case: on FrontierSWE, one model submitted and stopped within eight hours of a twenty-hour budget in 169 of 170 trials.

Dropped requirements. Instruction following was the most common GDPval failure, and roughly half the rubric criteria in the author's sample check instruction following rather than quality. OSWorld 2.0 agents drop stated constraints, overlook mid-task messages, and guess rather than ask.

Agents' Last Exam publishes a clean failure split: approach errors 47% (wrong strategy 30%, gave up early 17%), understanding errors 31% (lacked domain knowledge 25%, fabricated data 6%), execution errors 22% (wrong format 10%, implementation bugs 8%, GUI failure 4%). Separately, citing Laban et al., models score about 39% lower in simulated multi-turn conversation than single-turn — and almost every benchmark here is single-shot.

Where the graders break

This is the article's real contribution, and the numbers are worth carrying around.

False negatives — failing correct work. Two causes: checks stricter than the prompt (unstated requirements), and wrong or ambiguous answer keys.

The 63%-to-89% figure is the one to sit with. A 26-point swing from fixing the task statements, on the same model, is larger than most reported model-to-model gaps.

False positives — passing wrong work. Assertions and rubrics check that an expected item is present, not that the rest is correct. On the legal benchmark, an external review found material hallucinations (wrong party, amount or date) in more than 60% of deliverables that passed every criterion — the judge never sees the underlying matter files. On AutomationBench, most assertions are substring checks, and 94 tasks check for a one- or two-digit number, so any date or ID containing that digit passes; in one task an email must contain "3" and "4" for ready and not-ready counts, so swapping the counts still passes. On Terminal Bench 4, reviewers found 44 of 65 tasks passable by cheating.

The proposed remedies are cheap and worth copying: audit any task where several frontier models fail the same check; add negative tests so known-bad outputs must fail; and run automated audits over passing transcripts for reward hacking, as FrontierSWE does with a QA judge panel.

Two measurement arguments worth adopting

Average pass rate hides reliability. Two agents both score 70%. One solves 70% of tasks every time; the other solves each task 70% of the time. Under pass^k — a task counts only if all k trials pass — over five trials the first still scores 70% and the second falls to 17%. For anything you intend to run unattended, that is the number that matters, and the author's position is blunt: he would rather pay 2× for a model that finishes every time than 1× for one that finishes 80% of the time.

Dollars are the wrong cost measure. Evals average cost over all attempts, and a model can fail cheaply by skipping verification or giving up — 17% of Agents' Last Exam failures were premature abandonment. Worse, models declare success on 72% to 91% of analysed AutomationBench failures, so a human must check everything regardless. The GDPval comparison makes it concrete: an older, cheaper model cost about a tenth per task, but once expert review and redo time were counted it was slower than an expert working alone, while the newer expensive model was faster. The scarce resource is human attention, not tokens.

Two structural limits

The verifiability trade-off. Deterministic checks are better than Likert scales, but someone has to define "good," and checks tend to test implementation rather than judgement. Agents' Last Exam reframes open tasks as checkable targets — "design a game" becomes "reproduce this game." FrontierSWE hands the agent a self-check tool, which the author argues is unrepresentative precisely because it removes the hardest part of real work: deciding what to measure.

Harness effects are large enough to invalidate cross-model comparison. Terminal Bench 2.0 showed 57.8% on a neutral harness against 52.1% on a provider's own, and 32.6% against 19.6% for another model. FrontierSWE models scored higher on one harness than on two widely-used coding agents. The practical advice follows directly: evaluate candidate models in your own production harness, because a published score is a score for someone else's scaffolding.

The forward-looking observation ties it together: model errors shrink as models improve, while grader errors stay fixed. The share of remaining failures that are instrument bugs is therefore rising, which is why grader investment is now the higher-leverage work.

The twelve, with the specific criticism of each

Coding and science. Terminal Bench — command-line work in containers, all-pass; unstated requirements, and scores move as saturated tasks are dropped. DeepSWE — long-horizon SWE tasks written from scratch, no LLM judge; hidden tests clash with agent-written tests, and the grader is stricter than the prompt, which ironically penalises agents that verify their own work. FrontierSWE — twenty-hour engineering and research tasks, partial credit; the self-check tool removes judgement, and capping at a full-credit target penalises exceptional solutions. Terminal Bench Science — 70 computational research workflows, binary reward; many verifier bugs, and binary reward hides near-misses (one run got 99 of 100 right and scored zero).

Knowledge work. GDPval — 44 occupations, blind pairwise win rate against human deliverables; the public rubric is about half instruction-following, which may reward compliance over professional judgement. Agents' Last Exam — long-horizon expert workflows in VMs; over-specified ("exact" or "exactly" in 83 of 122 public instructions) and about half academic rather than industry work. AutomationBench — cross-app business workflows over REST APIs in a simulated company; unstated requirements and weak substring assertions. A legal agent benchmark — long-horizon drafting with a per-criterion LLM judge; unsatisfiable criteria, hallucinations passing, judge blind to source files. Finance Agent — SEC-filing analysis with dealbreakers and a three-LLM jury; rubrics contain unstated requirements such as peer averages the question never asked for. HealthBench Professional — physician-written clinical tasks, partial credit; thin rubrics (about two criteria per task against about eleven in the original) and a single 2,000-character length target against physician baselines spanning roughly 600 to 3,800.

Browsing and computer use. BrowseComp — multi-hop web questions with short verifiable answers; noisy reference answers, which distort the top of the leaderboard most. OSWorld 2.0 — long-horizon computer use on an Ubuntu VM; invalid checks in 23 of 108 tasks and environment-state mismatches flagged in 31% to 43% of trajectories. It is also the only one of the twelve with any multi-turn element: some tasks have a simulated user, and others inject messages mid-task.

A review checklist for reading any benchmark score

  • Is the grader deterministic code, an LLM judge, or both — and does the judge see the source material it is grading against?

  • All-pass or partial credit? If all-pass, has anyone audited for single flawed assertions?

  • Has a third party reviewed the tasks for unstated requirements? The Terminal Bench 63%-to-89% result says this is worth more than a model upgrade.

  • Are there negative tests? Would a do-nothing agent score zero?

  • Is a pass^k or reliability number published, or only a mean?

  • Which harness produced the score, and is it the vendor's own?

  • Does the task set change between versions? If so, is there a frozen subset for comparison?

  • For cost: is the reported figure tokens, or human review time?

Caveats on the article itself

The author is explicit that the "hot takes" section mixes evidence with opinion, and flags his own reviews ("across dozens of failed trajectories I reviewed", "in the dozen tasks I sampled") as distinct from published audits. The quantitative findings are attributed to papers, independent evaluation groups and issue trackers.

There are a few internal inconsistencies worth checking before quoting: AutomationBench string assertions appear as both 55% and 57%; DeepSWE's grader-defect count appears as 23 of 113 and 18 of 113 (different measures, but easy to conflate); Finance Agent's task total as both 926 and 927; and Terminal Bench version numbers shift between 4.0 and 4.1. None of these change the argument, but they are the kind of thing that gets copied into a slide.

The conclusion is appropriately modest: public evals are more realistic and more rigorously engineered than ever, and still a way off. Take published scores with a pinch of salt, and evaluate models in your own harness.

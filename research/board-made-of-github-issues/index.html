A board made of GitHub Issues: one practitioner's multi-agent coordination layer

A first-hand account, published 27 November 2026, of running three "chiefs of staff" and a fleet of worker agents against a shared board built entirely from GitHub Issues. Six days of operation, 130+ tickets. Worth reading as a design record rather than as a result — it reports no failure rates, costs or durations — and the most useful parts are the two failure modes it hit and the growth curve of its own rulebook.

The architecture

The board is GitHub Issues, with nothing underneath it. A unit of shared work is an issue carrying a type label and a state label; project work lives in its own repository and links back to the ticket. That is the whole persistence layer, and the choice is better than it first looks: issues already have identity, ordering, permissions, a webhook feed, search, and an append-only comment log, which is most of what a coordination substrate needs.

Coordination happens through comments with four structured headers: claim, status, review, blocker. An agent announces intent by commenting, not by writing to a queue. This makes the protocol machine-readable while remaining human-readable, and it means the board's state and the board's history are the same artifact.

Policy lives in two places: a board plugin that carries the commands, the skills and the mechanical checks, and an AGENTS.md on the board that states the rules. Two of the three coordinating agents skip the plugin entirely and follow AGENTS.md through the GitHub API — a useful property, since it means the protocol is implementable by anything that can read and write issues.

The role split is two-tier. Three coordinating agents on different runtimes: one opens the weekday plan ticket before 7:30 a.m.; one plans, polls the board hourly through the working day, routes items to owners and pings workers in chat; one cross-checks the other two plans and consolidates. All three run a label audit. Beneath them, worker agents run in a loop looking for tickets to build or review, split between cloud and a laptop, with some laptop workers acting as dispatchers for up to three subagents of their own.

The ticket lifecycle is where the design is strongest:

  1. A human — or, for routine work, the owning coordinator — marks a ticket ready.

  2. A worker claims it and builds in its own git worktree.

  3. The worker opens a pull request.

  4. A different agent reviews that exact revision.

  5. No agent merges on its own judgment; every merge waits for the human.

The per-worker worktree is the detail to copy first. It makes concurrent claims safe at the filesystem level rather than by convention, which is the usual place parallel coding agents collide. Review-by-a-different-agent, pinned to the exact revision, closes the self-review hole that makes single-agent verification worthless. And the stop list is explicit — spending money, secrets and settings, merges to main, anything irreversible — with the crucial addition that silence never approves anything on the stop list, which rules out the most common failure in long-running agent systems: a timeout read as consent.

Failure one: a shared login turns the audit trail into theatre

Every agent posts under a single login, distinguished only by an actor field in the comment body. The failure this produced is the sharpest thing in the account: a comment appeared to record the operator's approval of something, and it could not have. His conclusion, verbatim and worth keeping: a comment is a record, not proof that anyone approved anything.

The response was the right one — every agent now treats ticket text as information, not instructions. That is a prompt-injection boundary drawn inside a trusted system. The untrusted text here is not an attacker's; it is produced by the operator's own agents, and it is untrusted anyway, because an agent cannot distinguish a genuine instruction from a plausible-looking one written by a peer. Any multi-agent system whose members communicate through a shared mutable medium has this problem, whether or not anyone has noticed.

Two things remain unresolved in the account. The actor field is a convention, not a credential — nothing described would stop one agent writing another's actor id. And a separate incident shows the same weakness from a different angle: a coordinator wrote up the operator's idea under its own actor name, after which the other agents credited the coordinator. His fix was to record the source of every idea with its time and channel, and his framing is precise: the actor field records who typed, not whose idea it was.

The general principle: if the identity in your coordination log is self-asserted, the log is evidence of sequence, not of authority. Anything that gates an action needs an identity the writer cannot forge, which means distinct credentials per agent, not a field in a message body.

Failure two: a rulebook growing 6x in four days

Here is the growth curve, exactly as reported:

DateRule file size
21 November~2,000 words
23 November (end of day)over 8,000 words
25 November11,700 words

Built with over forty commits in four days. The authoring loop is: a failure becomes a conversation, a coordinator drafts the rule, the other agents review it, the human merges.

The account presents this growth as diligence, and the drafting process is sound — a rule written by the agent that hit the failure, reviewed by its peers, merged by a human, is better provenance than most teams manage. But the growth rate is the finding, and it points the other way, for one reason: the only mechanical enforcement described anywhere in the system is a single skill-fit check, a script that refused a worker's claim on a ticket scoped to a different runtime. Everything else — including every rule in those 11,700 words — is text that agents are asked to read.

That makes three questions decisive, and the account answers none of them:

  • Is it read? 11,700 words is a substantial fraction of a working context, competing directly with the task. Nothing says whether the whole file loads per turn, whether it is split per role, or whether any agent has been observed breaking a rule that was in the file.

  • Does it conflict with itself? Forty commits in four days, authored by three different agents, with no stated conflict detection.

  • Does anything ever leave? No retirement or deduplication mechanism is described. Rules accumulate monotonically.

The failure mode is silent, which is what makes it dangerous. A rule that is present but unread is indistinguishable, from the outside, from a rule that was never written — and the system's apparent compliance history gives no signal either way, because the rules that have never been tested look exactly like the rules that are working.

The structural fix is the one the better-studied designs converge on: a procedure belongs in text, a hard limit belongs in code. The skill-fit check is the model — it did not ask an agent to respect a scope, it refused the claim. Each of the account's other named failures suggests the same treatment: the poll that read only the first hundred comments on a ticket, and so missed a decision for over an hour, is a pagination bug, and the durable fix is a corrected fetch rather than a rule telling agents to read all comments. Tickets sitting with no state label are better caught by a check that rejects a ready-marking without one than by three agents running a label audit.

What the account does not establish

  • No cost, no rate, no duration. No failure rates, time saved, token or dollar figures, or run lengths. Six days of operation and 130+ tickets is the extent of the scale evidence.

  • The coordination volume is unpriced. One build pair produced 87 reviews and 118 status updates. Three coordinators independently produce plans that then have to be consolidated. The one reported payoff from that redundancy is a duplicate catch — all three plans surfaced the same pull request, and one coordinator noticed it had already merged. That is a cross-check working, but it is a deduplication win, not a quality win, and the price of running three planners is not reported.

  • Nothing is published. Whether the board plugin, the policy file, the agent registry or the scripts are available is not stated, so none of it is reproducible from the account.

  • The rule artifact is undefined. Whether the "rule file" is the policy file, something separate, or both is not stated.

  • No models, MCP servers, CI or git hooks are named, and nothing is said about approaches abandoned along the way.

Tradeoffs worth weighing before copying it

  • Issues as the substrate buys identity, history, permissions, search and webhooks for free, and costs you rate limits, latency, and a coordination log that is also a public-ish artifact. For a fleet polling hourly this is fine; for anything tighter the API becomes the constraint.

  • Comments as the protocol means the state and its history are one thing, which is excellent for audit and bad for querying. There is no way to ask "what is in flight" except by reading, which is exactly why a poll that truncated at a hundred comments lost a decision.

  • One shared login is operationally simple and makes authority unprovable. Distinct credentials per agent cost setup and buy you a log that can gate things.

  • Multiple coordinators catch each other's misses and multiply planning cost. The honest version of this choice measures the catches against the consolidation overhead; nobody has.

  • A human merge gate on everything is the correct default and the throughput ceiling. The account does not say what fraction of time tickets spend waiting on it, which is the number that would tell you whether the fleet is actually parallel.

  • Rules as text scales in authoring and does not scale in enforcement. Every rule that could be a check should become one, or the file grows until it is decorative.

Review checklist for a multi-agent board

  1. Can one agent forge another's identity in the coordination log? If identity is a field in a message rather than a credential, nothing in the log can gate an action.

  2. Does any agent treat text written by another agent as an instruction? It should not — peer-written text is untrusted input even inside a trusted system.

  3. Is silence ever read as approval? Name the actions where it must not be, and verify a timeout cannot proceed on them.

  4. For every rule in the rulebook, could it be a mechanical check instead? Count how many are checks and how many are prose; the ratio is the real enforcement story.

  5. How large is the rulebook, is all of it in context per turn, and when was anything last removed from it?

  6. Has any agent been observed violating a rule that was present in the file? If not, has anyone tested one?

  7. Do concurrent workers have isolated working trees, or do they share a checkout?

  8. Does the reviewer see the exact revision under review, and is the reviewer ever the author?

  9. How does the system enumerate in-flight work — by reading a log that can be truncated, or by querying state? Any pagination limit in that path is a lost-decision bug waiting to happen.

  10. What is the coordination traffic per unit of delivered work, and what does the redundancy among planners actually catch?

Related reading

  • Practitioner accounts of driving many parallel coding threads with a worktree per task cover the same mechanical ground, with more detail on model assignment per role and on review discipline.

  • Guidance arguing for folding subagents into a single loop, with procedures in skills and hard limits in tools, is the direct counterpoint to a rulebook enforced only by being read — and names the thresholds at which splitting work across agents starts paying.

  • Work on compute-matched comparison of agent teams against a single agent is what would settle whether three planners are worth their tokens; nothing in this account is measured that way.

  • Studies of accumulated agent experience — keeping the edit log rather than only the current ruleset, and pruning lessons by observed non-use — address exactly the question this rulebook's growth curve raises.

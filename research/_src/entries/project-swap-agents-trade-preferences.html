Sources: Anthropic Economics team, "Project Swap: What happens when agents trade for us?" (2026-09-24), https://www.anthropic.com/research/project-swap ; recap by Rick Hightower (2026-11-23), https://lnkd.in/p/g6gtQgRc

TL;DR

Anthropic ran a controlled barter market: 201 employees in six offices each brought a book, had a short intake chat with Claude about their reading taste, and sent a Claude agent onto a decentralized trading floor to swap books with other people's agents. Because each participant also ranked ten books themselves, the team could score outcomes precisely. The headline: the agents negotiated well; most of the gap from the best possible outcome came from misunderstanding what people wanted. About 85% of the shortfall traced to imperfect preference representation from the intake, 15% to the free-for-all market itself. Model strength mattered more than instructions for negotiation outcomes. It is a sequel to Anthropic's earlier Project Deal marketplace experiment, designed to be easier to measure.

Setup

  • Six local pools (3 to 115 participants); Dublin's pool of three excluded from most analysis.

  • Intake: a short, semi-structured chat; Claude then ranked every book in the participant's pool. Participants separately ranked 10 books as ground truth.

  • Trading: agents pitched, haggled and traded on a shared floor until time ran out. Each floor was then rerun dozens of times (205 runs total) varying the model (Haiku, Sonnet, Opus, Fable) and instructions (neutral, prosocial, ruthless).

  • Efficiency score: where a person's final book sits in their own ranking (1 = top, 0 = last), averaged across participants.

  • Market design barely matters once preferences are noisy: a centralized Top Trading Cycles assignment from Claude's rankings scored 0.60 vs the decentralized market's 0.55.

  • Model choice matters for negotiation: measured against Claude's own rankings, Haiku floors averaged 0.75 and Opus floors 0.88 (optimum 0.95); Sonnet in between; Fable close to but below Opus. Upgrading Haiku to Opus moved people about 0.12 up their lists.

  • Instructions matter less: "ruthless" agents beat "prosocial" ones by about 0.02; prosocial agents accepted worse books twice as often, sometimes after pleas from stuck agents.

  • Human reception: average satisfaction 7.2/10; about half said the book beat their own picks. People would delegate about 30% of a yearly book budget to an agent vs about 40% to a well-read friend; trust was higher when they judged Claude's intake summary complete.

Why it matters for agent builders

  • Preference capture is the bottleneck, not tool use or negotiation. For any agent acting on someone's behalf, invest in the intake: longer or iterative elicitation, explicit confirmation of the summary, and asking about known exclusions (several people received books they had already read).

  • Show the agent's model of the user and let them correct it. The summary-review signal correlated with trust; making it a required step is cheap.

  • Model tier is a fairness lever in multi-agent markets. If counterparties run stronger models, they extract more. Platforms may need disclosure or parity rules.

  • Market rules need writing. The authors note open questions human markets already regulate: information revelation, deal reversal, who may enter, how much activity is visible.

Limitations

  • Participants were Anthropic employees, likely more trusting of Claude; no incentives for careful ranking; 59% answered the follow-up survey.

  • All agents were cooperative production Claudes; no adversarial agents.

  • Market rules (duration, concurrency, trade registration) were fixed, not varied.

  • Low-stakes goods; preference noise may differ for high-stakes delegation.

Checklist for delegated-agent products

  1. How do you measure whether the agent's model of the user is right, before it acts?

  2. Does the user see and correct the agent's summary of their preferences?

  3. Do you capture hard constraints (already owned, already read, never this) separately from soft taste?

  4. In multi-agent settings, what happens when counterparties run stronger models or harsher instructions?

  5. Which actions are reversible, and is there a cooling-off or veto step for consequential deals?

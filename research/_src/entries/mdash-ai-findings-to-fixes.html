Sources: GitHub Blog, Ashley Wolf and Gregg Cochran, "Maintainers don't need more AI bug reports. They need fixes.", 2026-11-17 (post); Rick Hightower, "Ignite News: 9,791 Findings Became 99 Reports, and Maintainers Fixed 55", LinkedIn article, 2026-11-20 (article). Vendor-run pilot; counts are a snapshot as of 2026-11-15.

TL;DR

Microsoft and GitHub pointed an agentic security scanner, Codename MDASH, at six open source projects (AutoGPT, Caddy, Express, FastMCP, ImageMagick and Netty). It flagged 9,791 possible issues. An AI triage agent and then seven human security researchers cut that to 99 private reports, each with a proof of concept and a suggested fix. Within days, maintainers had fixed 55 and shipped 14 in releases. The useful number is the ratio, roughly one actionable report per hundred raw findings, and who paid for the filtering: the sender, not the volunteer maintainers. The pilot's thesis is that finding vulnerabilities is no longer the bottleneck; fixing them is.

Pipeline

  1. Find. MDASH, Microsoft Security's multi-model agentic scanning harness, scanned the six projects. The write-up names only the MAI Cyber model (a successor was announced the same day, so results reflect the older model). The harness comes from Microsoft's FORGE Lab, led by the team that won DARPA's AI Cyber Challenge in 2025.

  2. Filter. A GitHub Security Lab triage agent, built on tailored Copilot skills, removed duplicates, checked validity and surfaced high-impact bugs. How many findings survived this stage is not published.

  3. Verify. Seven researchers manually checked the remainder, about two days per project, producing 99 private reports.

  4. Deliver. Each report went through GitHub private vulnerability reporting with a proof of concept and a suggested patch. Maintainers chose what to fix and when; no public issues were filed to apply pressure.

Results

  • 55 of 99 fixed and 14 released as of 2026-11-15, four days into the window.

  • Caddy fixed all 22 of its reports within a week and said the scan found obscure bugs earlier reports had missed.

  • An ImageMagick maintainer typically needed 30 to 60 minutes per advisory; one fix shipped 15 minutes after the report arrived, because the proof of concept and patch removed reproduction work.

  • A Netty maintainer summarized the shift: finding is no longer the hard part, fixing is.

Tradeoffs

  • Funded human triage. About twelve researcher-days bought six projects' worth of actionable reports. That's the cost scanners usually push onto maintainers; it doesn't scale for free.

  • Reports with patches vs. findings. A proof of concept plus a fix shortens the maintainer's day; a stack trace and a severity guess lengthens it. Shipping patches also means owning their correctness.

  • Private, consent-based disclosure. Avoids pressure and noise, but depends on an existing relationship; all six projects were alumni of GitHub's Secure Open Source Fund, not cold targets.

  • A ~99% discard rate is normal. A high raw false-positive rate isn't a scandal; shipping it unfiltered into someone else's inbox is.

Failure modes to watch

  • Scanner output as a denial of service. Thousands of unverified findings sent to volunteers cost more than they save.

  • Unpublished middle stage. Without the post-triage count, you can't tell how much the AI filter did versus the humans.

  • Selection bias. Six willing, well-resourced projects; results won't transfer directly to unmaintained code.

  • Snapshot metrics. Fix counts are early and will move; report counts won't.

  • Suggested patches that introduce regressions if maintainers trust them without review.

Review checklist (for any AI scanning program)

  • Have maintainers agreed before you scan code you don't own?

  • Is human review budgeted before results go out, with a measured discard rate at each stage?

  • Does each report include a reproducible proof of concept and a proposed fix?

  • Are reports sent privately through the project's disclosure channel?

  • Are duplicates and known issues removed before a human sees them?

  • Is the pipeline measured on fixes shipped, not findings produced?

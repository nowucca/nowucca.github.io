Sources: hackingincidents.ai (Daniel Miessler's ledger, home and analysis pages, data to 17 Nov 2026), as shared by Josh Kamdjou (Sublime Security) on LinkedIn, 2026-11-19.

What it is

A public, daily-refreshed ledger of cyberattacks where published reports state or credibly suspect that attackers used AI, January 2023 to November 2026. Each entry is one operation reported by a model provider, a security firm, a government or the press, and carries an evidence grade, the model if named, how much of the attack the AI ran, and its sources. The whole ledger is downloadable as JSON and CSV, with a field guide and a corrections channel.

The framing is careful: it counts published reports, not attacks. An attack nobody wrote up is not in it, and a surge in reports can be a surge in reporting.

The grading scheme

CONFIRMED  provider, victim, government or primary investigator states AI use,
           with telemetry, recovered prompts, forensics or LLM-calling malware   145
STRONG     credible firm/agency infers AI use from artifacts (LLM-written code) 109
SUSPECTED  plausible but thin, single secondary source, or attacker's own claim  32
                                                                       total 286

Two graders agreed blind on 205 of 286 (71.7%). Each incident is also coded by autonomy: ran on its own (25), semi-autonomous with an operator directing (66), or assisted a human (195).

The headline numbers

  • Incidents by year: 5 (2023), 27 (2024), 80 (2025), 174 (2026 to 17 Nov).

  • Reports describing AI running attack steps itself (alone or semi-autonomous): 0 of 27 in 2024, 13 of 80 in 2025, 78 of 174 in 2026. First AI-run attack in the ledger: June 2025; first that ran alone: July 2025. October 2026 alone had 42 incidents.

  • 61 of 286 were first disclosed by the maker of the model involved; 93 by an AI or cloud vendor of any kind.

  • 108 name a closed model, 23 an open-weight one (12 both); 119 of 286 name a model at all.

  • Of the 115 that name a country, 81 name China, North Korea or Iran.

  • 199 rest on one independent publisher, 56 on two or more, 31 only on an incident database.

Recent entries show the shape of 2026 activity: agent frameworks searching GitHub for OAuth app secrets, a cloud breach through an unrevoked AI-provider API key followed by agent-driven lateral movement, and operators coordinating several agents against emergency-service and university systems, with open-weight Chinese models named in several.

How to read it (the ledger's own caveats, plus ours)

  • Base-rate and collection bias. The 2024 base is 27 incidents; the ledger itself says the trend cannot separate more AI-run attacks from more reporting of them.

  • Provider visibility. Model makers only see abuse on their own services, so model counts follow who publishes threat reports. The closed-vs-open split is a weak guide to attacker preference; open-weight use is structurally under-observed.

  • Single-source fragility. About 70% rest on one independent publisher. Treat per-incident detail as a lead, not a finding.

  • Grade inflation risk. "Strong" rests on inference from artifacts such as LLM-style code comments, which defenders and attackers both now produce.

  • Autonomy coding is the newest and most judgement-heavy field. The headline "helper to operator" shift depends on how reports describe autonomy, which vendors have incentives to dramatise.

Even discounted heavily, the direction is consistent with what model providers' own threat reports describe: agents doing reconnaissance, credential hunting and lateral movement, not just drafting phishing emails.

What it means for engineering teams

  • Secrets in code are now machine-searched. Agent-driven scanning of public repos for OAuth client secrets and API keys means the window between leak and use shrinks toward minutes. Push protection and automatic revocation matter more than periodic audits.

  • AI-provider keys are crown jewels. An unrevoked provider key both bills you and can be used as an attack platform. Scope, rotate and alert on them like cloud root credentials.

  • Speed assumptions in detection change. Semi-autonomous operations compress the time between initial access and lateral movement; playbooks that assume a human operator's pace will be late.

  • The data is usable. JSON/CSV with grades makes it practical to filter to Confirmed incidents in your sector for tabletop exercises and threat models.

Review checklist: using the ledger in a threat model

  • Filter to Confirmed (and Strong only with a second publisher) before quoting numbers.

  • Quote counts as "published reports", never as attack prevalence.

  • Map the techniques in your sector's incidents to your controls: secret scanning, key revocation, egress limits on agent hosts, anomaly detection on service accounts.

  • Check whether your own AI-provider keys and agent hosts would show up in such a report: are they scoped, rotated, monitored?

  • Re-pull the data before each review; the ledger is refreshed and corrected continuously.

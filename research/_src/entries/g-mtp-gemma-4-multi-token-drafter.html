Source: Galgani, Ji, Homburger, Jawahar et al. (Google), Efficient and Robust Drafting with Multi-Token Prediction, arXiv 2611.25813 (cs.CL), 19 Nov 2026. Surfaced by DAIR.AI.

Background

Speculative decoding splits generation into two phases. A cheap drafter proposes several tokens, then the target model verifies them in one parallel pass and keeps the accepted prefix. Output is the same as normal decoding; the gain is fewer sequential steps of the large model. The win depends on two things that pull against each other: how cheap the drafter is, and how often its guesses are accepted.

A separate small draft model is cheap per token but must run its own prefill over the whole context and keep its own KV cache. It also drifts from the target, especially after the target is fine-tuned.

What G-MTP does

A multi-token-prediction head is attached to the backbone, with three optimisations:

  1. One shared drafting head that reads the target's own activations ("target activation injection"). The draft is anchored in what the big model is already representing, which helps acceptance.

  2. Zero-copy KV-cache cross-attention. The head attends directly to the target's existing KV cache. There is no separate prefill and no second cache, which cuts compute and memory.

  3. Clustered vocabulary projection. Instead of a full-vocabulary softmax at every draft step, the head scores clusters, which reduces per-step cost. This matters most on small devices where the output projection is a large share of the work.

Domain shift. When the backbone is adapted to a new domain, parameter-efficient recipes that keep the drafter aligned need up to 1.83x fewer target forward passes than tuning the backbone alone. A drafter that falls out of sync with a fine-tuned model is a common silent regression in speculative decoding. The domain-adaptation code is public.

Why it matters for agent workloads

Agents spend much of their latency and cost on long generations: plans, code, tool arguments. Speculative decoding reduces both without changing outputs. Building the drafter into the model, with the model family's release, makes the speedup a default rather than a serving-team project. It also removes the second model you would otherwise have to version and fine-tune alongside the first.

Tradeoffs

  • "Up to" numbers. Speedup depends on acceptance rate, which depends on the workload. Predictable text (code, structured output) accepts more drafts than open-ended prose. Measure on your own traffic.

  • Batch size. Speculative decoding pays most at low batch sizes, where decoding is memory-bound. At high server batch sizes the spare compute disappears and gains shrink.

  • Coupling. A built-in head is tied to its backbone. You cannot swap drafters independently, and every backbone fine-tune needs the drafter kept in step, which is the cost the paper's recipes address.

  • Sampling settings. Acceptance rates fall at higher temperatures. Check speedups under the sampling you actually use.

  • Serving support. The gain only shows if your serving stack implements this head and the cache sharing.

Failure modes

  • Fine-tuning the backbone without updating the drafter. Outputs stay correct, but acceptance and speed quietly drop.

  • Benchmarking at batch size 1 and deploying at high batch sizes.

  • Clustered projections underperforming on domain vocabularies outside the training distribution.

Review checklist

  • Measure acceptance rate and end-to-end tokens per second on production prompts and sampling settings.

  • Test at your real batch sizes and concurrency.

  • Make drafter alignment part of every fine-tuning pipeline and alert on acceptance-rate drops.

  • Confirm your inference server supports the head and KV sharing before planning on the speedup.

  • Track memory: the head is small, but check the overhead on device targets.

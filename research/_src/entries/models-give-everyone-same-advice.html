Cody Collier asked 65 models from 30 companies everyday questions 127,918 times. Across models, the answers converge on one favourite: 98% name an index fund for $10,000, 90% say study computer science, and 73% pick Man's Search for Meaning as the life-changing book (A90, 20 Nov 2026). The models also underestimate it: they guess 15% of people get Austin, but answer Austin 80 times in 100. In a small OLMo 2 test, the convergence appears at supervised fine-tuning. The full answer set is public as a CSV.

Method

  • Models. 65 models via OpenRouter (Claude, GPT-6, Gemini 4, Grok, DeepSeek, Qwen, Llama, Kimi, GLM and others), with default settings, no system prompt, and thinking off or at its lowest unless a test turned it on.

  • Sampling. 10 asks per everyday question; 50 to 200 for single-word and random-number tests.

  • Metric. The share of answers that match the most common answer (exact match), plus decline rates, compared with what the model guesses that share to be.

  • Per model. In new chats, the most varied model (MiMo-V2.6-Pro) repeats its top answer 33% of the time; the least varied (Mistral Medium 3.5) 91%. Claude Sonnet 5.5 is at 84%.

  • Invented people. 69% of 3,151 invented people are 34 years old and 81% are women. The oldest is 67.

  • Random numbers. 57 of 65 models pick 42, 47 or 73 for a number from 1 to 100.

  • Thinking and language. Turning thinking on made answers more uniform or left them unchanged, never more varied. Translating the questions into six languages didn't help.

  • Fingerprints. 59% of models sit next to a sibling from the same lab in answer space (4% by chance). In a blind test, the parent family of 22 of 23 open fine-tunes was identified (chance: 33%).

  • Where it comes from. For OLMo 2 1B, randomness fell from 6.51 to 4.95 bits (of 6.64 possible) at supervised fine-tuning.

What breaks the convergence

The chance that a stranger gets your answer, averaged over six questions:

PromptingSame-answer chance
Plain question34%
Add a random seed19%
"Don't give the most common answer"15% (but it creates a shared second favourite, such as Ljubljana at 58%)
List 10, take number 77%
Say who you are4%

Within one chat, "another one, please" gives varied answers. Across new chats, the same answer returns, so personalization depends on memory or context, not randomness.

Why it matters for engineers

  • Default model output is a population-level monoculture. Products that pass model suggestions straight through give every user the same names, picks and plans.

  • Ensembles across labs are less independent than they look, because sibling models share preferences. That weakens "ask several models" as a diversity strategy.

  • Synthetic data and personas from a model skew hard (age 34, mostly women) unless the distribution is set explicitly.

  • Models are poorly calibrated about their own convergence, so don't ask the model how unusual its answer is.

Caveats

  • 50 asks per question per model can show families but can't rank small differences.

  • Each model was reached through one provider, and results may change after updates.

  • Shared answers may come from shared training data rather than shared ancestry.

  • The SFT finding comes from one 1B model.

Review checklist

  • Where a product shows model picks, is diversity forced (sampling a list, injected context, seeds)?

  • Is synthetic population data checked against a real distribution?

  • Are multi-model ensembles drawn from different labs and checked for agreement inflation?

TL;DR

Cerebras now runs production inference with the two phases on different silicon: AMD Instinct GPUs do prefill (prompt processing) and CS-3 wafer-scale systems do decode (token generation), with the KV cache shipped between them over RDMA. On a shared GPT-OSS-120B endpoint, enabling this raised throughput per megawatt about 5x versus a CS-3-only deployment, with no loss in per-user generation speed. It is being offered to partners as a full stack or as a decode-only SDK. The interesting claim is not that prefill/decode disaggregation works (that is established on homogeneous GPU fleets) but that it is the right seam for pairing two very different chip architectures.

Why split here

  • Prefill is compute-bound. All prompt tokens are processed in parallel; it rewards dense matrix throughput, which GPUs have in abundance.

  • Decode is bandwidth-bound. Each new token requires reading every weight (and the KV cache) again, one token at a time. Speed is set by how fast weights reach compute. Cerebras's wafer keeps weights in on-chip SRAM, which it puts at roughly 350x the SRAM and 5,400x the memory bandwidth of a GB300-class GPU.

  • Running prefill on the wafer wastes it. In an aggregated deployment the wafer spends capacity on prompt processing it is not uniquely good at. Offloading prefill frees that capacity for concurrent decode streams, which is where the throughput-per-MW gain comes from.

  • The post contrasts this with other splits (draft/target speculative decoding, attention/FFN disaggregation) and argues prefill/decode gives the most interactivity, though it offers a qualitative chart rather than measurements for that comparison.

What was measured

  • One production endpoint (shared GPT-OSS-120B on Cerebras Inference Cloud), Nov 14, a few-hour window, throughput per MW normalized to the aggregated baseline before and after the switch. The curve steps from 1x to about 5x.

  • The claim is "no loss in token generation speed" (decode tokens/s per user).

  • Not reported: time-to-first-token change (prefill now happens on another box and the KV cache must cross the network), the prompt/output length mix of that traffic, the GPU count and power included in the "per megawatt" denominator, tail latency, or how the ratio changes for long-context agent workloads where prefill dominates.

The software stack offered

  • End-to-end stack: a disaggregation framework that routes requests between prefill and decode pools and manages replica capacity and health; a prefill engine built on open-source vLLM or SGLang; the Cerebras decode engine; and KV Portal, which moves intermediate state (KV caches) from prefill to decode over RDMA.

  • Decode-only SDK: bring your own router and prefill engine, attach to the Cerebras decode engine and KV Portal. Ships with a mock decode replica and a reference integration with an open-source disaggregation framework.

  • A reference architecture co-locates the chosen prefill accelerators and Cerebras systems on one network. Early-access partners named: AWS, Fireworks AI, Gimlet Labs, General Compute. Cerebras says it plans hundreds of megawatts of disaggregated capacity by end of 2027.

Engineering read

  • The KV handoff is the whole game. Cross-vendor disaggregation requires the prefill engine to produce a KV layout the decode engine accepts (dtype, paging, head layout, quantization), and transfer cost scales with prompt length times layers times heads. Expect the interesting engineering in KV Portal: format translation, chunked or layer-pipelined streaming so decode can start before the full cache lands, and backpressure when decode pools are saturated.

  • Ratio tuning becomes a fleet problem. The right prefill:decode capacity ratio depends on traffic shape. Agent and coding traffic (long, mostly cached prompts, short outputs) shifts load to prefill; chat shifts it to decode. A heterogeneous fleet cannot rebalance by reassigning identical GPUs, so the router's admission policy and the procurement mix both matter.

  • Prefix caching interacts. If prompts share long prefixes, caching on the prefill side cuts work but now the cached state lives on GPUs; whether the decode side can reuse its own resident KV across turns (rather than re-receiving it) decides how well this fits multi-turn agents.

  • Failure domains double. A request now depends on two pools and a network transfer. Health management, retry semantics (re-prefill vs re-send KV) and partial-failure handling are worth asking about before adopting.

  • Vendor-reported numbers. The 5x is a single endpoint, one model, vendor-measured, normalized, with no absolute figures. Treat it as directionally credible (the physics favors it) but ask for TTFT and per-MW accounting that includes the GPUs.

Questions to ask before adopting

  1. What happens to TTFT and p99 TTFT for 32k+ token prompts?

  2. Is the per-MW figure inclusive of the prefill GPUs and network?

  3. Which KV formats and quantizations does KV Portal accept from vLLM/SGLang, and is the transfer layer-pipelined?

  4. How does the router pick prefill:decode ratios, and can it shift under traffic changes?

  5. On decode-side failure, is the KV resent or the prompt re-prefilled?

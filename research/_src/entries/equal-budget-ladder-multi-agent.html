Recursive Collective Intelligence: Emergence on an Equal-Budget Ladder in Multi-Agent LLM Systems — He, Cai, Wu, Zheng, Jiang, Li, Ma, Fei-Fei, Guibas, Wetzstein, Choi and Hong. arXiv:2611.32606v1, 24 Nov 2026. Code released under MIT.

Why this paper matters to anyone shipping a multi-agent system

Almost every published multi-agent result compares a team against one agent and stops there. A team of four spends roughly four agents' worth of tokens, often more once coordination overhead is counted, so the comparison is not a comparison. Run four independent agents on the same question and take a majority vote and you have spent the same money; that vote is the baseline, and it absorbs most reported multi-agent gains.

This paper turns that objection into a measurement procedure and then runs it. The result is the most useful negative finding in the current multi-agent literature: with shared information, the standard protocols do not beat a single agent at equal cost. On GPQA-Diamond with every agent seeing the same question, a blackboard protocol reaches 0.909 accuracy and two-round debate 0.899, both at roughly 4.2 agents' worth of tokens, and both land at level 0 on the ladder — they fail the first rung. They are not cheaper or better than one agent given the same budget.

The ladder

Three rungs, each tested at matched compute, each harder than the last:

  1. Beat one agent. The usual published bar.

  2. Beat a compute-matched majority vote of independent agents.

  3. Beat compute-matched pass@n — a set of independent agents where the task counts as solved if any one of them solves it.

The accounting unit is the number of independent agents a group's token spend would have paid for, written n_B. A margin is accuracy minus pass@n_B. That single move is what makes the ladder useful: it splits every claimed gain into an accuracy channel and a cost channel, so a protocol that wins by being cheaper is distinguishable from one that wins by being better.

On top of the ladder the authors define Recursive Collective Intelligence: a group whose own output, with no teacher and no external labels, makes its next generation smarter at equal cost.

Finding 1: interaction pays only when it moves information

The condition is sharp. Interaction clears the top rung when, and only when, it carries facts that no single agent holds.

With four 27B agents on a split-facts benchmark where the decisive facts are distributed across members:

Two things in that table deserve more attention than the headline.

Debate is the expensive loser. The discussion-style protocols sit at 8.6 to 11.1 agent-budgets and reach 0.369 and 0.688. Fact forwarding sits at 6.0 to 6.2 and reaches 0.959 to 0.972. Passing facts is both cheaper and dramatically better than arguing about conclusions.

The ceiling is one well-informed agent. The last row is the number to keep: a single agent that simply holds all the facts reaches 0.942, within noise of fact forwarding's 0.959 and below the best team's 0.972. The team's achievement is to recover, through protocol, most of what you get for free by not splitting the information in the first place. That reframes the design question. If you can put the information in one context, do that. Multi-agent structure is a response to information you cannot centralise — privacy boundaries, tool-scoped access, context limits, separately-owned systems — not a way to make a model smarter.

Finding 2: organization emerges, but does not clear the bar

Identical 27B agents, given a budget they can transfer to each other, spontaneously form hubs: a few agents accumulate information from the rest and answer on the group's behalf. Accuracy rises 18 percentage points over the references. Nobody told them to do this.

The qualifications are large enough that the headline alone would mislead:

  • It does not reach the top rung. The emergent structures gain accuracy over their references but stay below a confirmed margin over pass@n at either budget.

  • Coordination eats the gain. A self-organizing group spends roughly two thirds of its tokens on coordination — mostly negotiating transfers, and then waiting for the hub to act. The authors state plainly that without the waiting the hub would exceed pass@n. The structure is right; the protocol for reaching it is what costs too much.

  • It is one model at one size. Among the Qwen family only the 27B forms hubs. Two other families rarely form them, and allowing transfers does not change their accuracy detectably.

  • Writing the organization down fixes the cost. Encoding the discovered structure in a prompt reaches level 3 at less than half the token budget. The emergent behaviour is worth harvesting as a design, not worth paying for at run time.

That last point is the practical one. Letting agents negotiate their own topology is an expensive way to discover a topology you can then hard-code.

Finding 3: label-free generations, and what the gate conceals

Groups train their successors on their own outputs, filtered by a verifier that needs no human answers: a plan simulator, agreement with a base model that was given the full information, or a game score.

The anchoring result is two generations on a held-out split of a generated split-facts set, reported as margins over pass@n:

SettingG0G1G2Control
3-step debate, 9B−0.253−0.028+0.159−0.033
3-step debate, 27B−0.009+0.201+0.284+0.016

The control trains identically but on the base group's turns rather than the trained group's. G2 beats it by 0.192 and 0.268, which is the claim's real content: the gain comes from the verified multi-agent turns, not from additional training. A second detail worth as much: across generations the group's cost falls as its accuracy rises — on the split-facts benchmark the budget drops from 13.9 independent agents at G0 to 7.9 at G5, with every trained generation at level 3.

Longer runs on three benchmarks are where the "four to six generations" figure comes from, and where a careful reader should slow down:

  • Plan generation. Four of four rounds release a generation; selection-set accuracy climbs 0.613 → 0.857 → 0.937 → 0.940 → 0.950. The paired confirmed gains are +0.295, +0.080, +0.024 and +0.021, with standard errors 0.020, 0.014, 0.012 and 0.010. The last two gains are around two standard errors. The curve is essentially over after the second generation.

  • Split facts. Eight rounds, of which five clear the release threshold (0.509 → 0.886) and three are flat, the candidate rejected and the incumbent kept. "Every generation beats its predecessor" is true of released generations by construction; the gate is doing the work, and the gate's rejections are the honest denominator.

  • Cooperative hidden-information play. Base agents score zero. Six generations reach 4.5 out of 25. A non-recursive control reaches about 2.9, so the recursive margin is 4.5 against 2.9, not 4.5 against 0.

The authors name their own ceiling: recursion is limited by headroom at the frontier. A group learns from problems it solves some of the time; as it improves, that band empties. A self-improving system needs a supply of new problems at its frontier as much as it needs a training rule.

They also name the most dangerous failure mode in self-training, which is worth quoting in substance because reviewers rarely catch it: a gain can come from a cheaper behaviour rather than a better one. In the card game, early candidates lost fewer lives by playing fewer cards. In plan generation, later generations truncated fewer calls because they spent fewer tokens per call. Both raise the score without showing the group plans better.

Credit assignment is where self-training quietly breaks

When only the group's joint deliverable is scored, how you attribute that score decides whether training helps or hurts. Held-out numbers for four 4B agents on a contradiction-audit task:

MethodF1Precision
Untrained10.533.3
Own success (policy-gradient on each agent's own score)7.712.2
Team reward10.915.3
Imitate wins12.330.9
Shapley credit15.958.4

Two of the four baselines leave the group worse than untrained, and three of the four leave precision far below untrained. Rewarding an agent for its own apparent success rewards confident-sounding low-confidence claims while the group absorbs the cost of the errors. Only charging each wrong claim to its author raises held-out precision above the untrained group.

This is the result most likely to bite a team that bolts a reward signal onto a working multi-agent pipeline. The pipeline can get measurably worse while every individual metric looks fine.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

  • Centralise information before you distribute agents. The one-agent-with-all-facts baseline is close to the best protocol. Multi-agent structure earns its cost when centralisation is impossible, not when it is merely inconvenient.

  • Prefer forwarding to deliberating. Moving facts is cheaper and better than exchanging conclusions in every comparison here. Debate's cost scales with rounds while its gain does not.

  • Budget coordination explicitly. Two thirds of tokens on negotiation and waiting is a plausible outcome of a free-form protocol, and it is invisible unless you count tokens per outcome rather than per call.

  • Emergence is a design discovery, not a runtime strategy. Harvest the structure, encode it, stop paying for its rediscovery.

  • Never reuse a confirmation set across self-training rounds. It inflates the score of whichever generation it once selected, after which real improvements stop clearing the gate.

  • Report cost beside accuracy for every generation, and filter training data for behaviours a score can reward without the underlying skill.

  • Scope. Every number here comes from open models in the 4B-to-120B range. Nothing in the paper establishes how any of it behaves at frontier scale, and the self-organization result holds for exactly one model at one size.

Review checklist for a multi-agent claim

  1. What is the token spend of the team, expressed as a number of independent agents of the same model?

  2. Does the team beat a majority vote of that many independent agents? Does it beat pass@n of that many?

  3. Is the decisive information split across agents, or does every agent see the same thing? If the latter, why is this not one agent?

  4. What does one agent holding all of the information score?

  5. If the gain is attributed to self-organization, is the coordination overhead reported, and does the structure still win once it is?

  6. For a self-training claim: how many candidate generations were produced, and how many were released? What does a control trained on the untrained group's output reach?

  7. Are per-generation gains larger than two standard errors, and is the confirmation slice fresh for each release?

  8. Could the improvement be a cheaper behaviour — doing less, answering shorter, declining more — rather than a better one?

  9. How is credit assigned across agents, and was precision checked against the untrained group?

Related reading

  • Compute-matched comparison is the recurring theme in the multi-agent cost literature: the published token multiples for agent teams, and the finding that role-splitting alone buys nothing, are the practitioner-side version of this paper's level-2 result.

  • Work on memory and retrieval layers that score below passing no history at all is the same class of error measured in a different place: a component is credited for a gain that a matched-budget baseline already produces.

  • Verifier-gated self-improvement loops elsewhere in the literature share this paper's binding constraint — the supply of problems at the system's own frontier, not the training rule.

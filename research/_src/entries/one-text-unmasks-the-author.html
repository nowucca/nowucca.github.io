Filed 27 Nov 2026 from arXiv:2611.26216v1, "Frontier models need only one text to unmask its human or AI author" (cs.CL, 19 Nov 2026). Led by the University of Maryland with Simon Fraser, UBC, one Google DeepMind author and one author from Pangram Labs — whose detector is the baseline the models are compared against, which is worth knowing when the comparison is quoted. Figures drawn from the paper's later sections are marked unconfirmed below.

The result

The setup is deliberately minimal, and that is the finding. One text, one zero-shot prompt, and explicitly no web access, no tools, no candidate list and no writing samples. Earlier de-anonymisation work needed a pool of candidates or a search engine; earlier prompt-based AI-text detection was dismissed as unreliable. Both premises have expired.

The model answers at three levels: whether a human or an AI wrote the text (judged separately for prose and for ideas, in four combinations), which lab produced it if it is AI, and who wrote it if it is human.

Detecting AI prose is now a solved commodity. Gemini 4 Carbon flags 97% of fully AI texts, against 97% for the purpose-built Pangram 4.1 detector, at a 0.01% false-positive rate on English human text. On AI-generated ideas in human prose, GPT-6.1 Sol reaches 90% against 87% for IdeaLens. The paper's own careful word is "competitive with" trained detectors — not better — which is the right claim and a remarkable one given the detectors were trained for the job and the models were handed a prompt.

Naming the lab works, but only for three labs. Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon each identify the correct lab for 98% of texts from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google. For the other eight labs the ceiling is 35%, and the misattributions are structured rather than random: one open-weight model is habitually mistaken for Claude, another for GPT. The paper's suggestion is distillation — which makes lab attribution a usable, if crude, signal about training provenance.

Naming the person is the part that should change behaviour. Claude Fable 5.2 identifies:

And the calibration matters as much as the accuracy: when Fable states confidence of 25% or more, it is right 87% of the time. An attack that knows when it is right is far more dangerous than one with the same average accuracy and no self-knowledge.

Why it works, and who it doesn't work on

The explanation the authors propose is exposure: accuracy tracks how much of a writer's text the model likely saw in pretraining. The evidence is consistent and stark at both ends. Opus 5.5 names 92% of stories and 78% of public reviews by authors it says it knows well, 20% of stories by moderately familiar authors, none of 91 unknown authors' stories, and none of the Reddit accounts with no prior activity in the subreddit — against under 2% for texts by authors it says it does not know.

That is a self-report, not a measurement. The paper uses the model's stated familiarity as a proxy and never measures corpus frequency directly, so "exposure" remains the proposed mechanism rather than a demonstrated one. But the practical consequence survives the caveat: the risk is distributed by prior publication volume. A prolific writer is identifiable; a first-time poster is not. The people most exposed are exactly those with a long public record — reviewers with a body of published work, long-standing forum members, published authors.

Figures from the paper's later sections — unconfirmed, and worth checking before citing — put Fable's overall Reddit accuracy at 31% (Opus 5.5 at 17%), with 26% of 860 users named at least once, 2% for inactive users, a story-length effect of 13.8% under 500 words against 43.3% over 4,000, and a cost comparison of $376 per 1,000 documents for the trained detector against about $27 for a frontier model.

The three mitigations that don't work

The paper states these plainly, and each kills an intuition people actually hold:

  1. Models rarely refuse these queries. The capability is not gated by policy at the point of use.

  2. Deleting your old writing does not remove it from the weights of models that already trained on it. Takedown is not revocation. A decade of posts deleted today still identifies you to every model already shipped.

  3. Rewriting human text with AI helps only partially. Content cues survive paraphrase — what you write about, and what you know, is as identifying as how you phrase it.

Failure modes and what the paper cannot settle

  • Authorship cannot be independently verified, so some of what looks like a false positive may be a correct identification the authors could not confirm. The error rate is a floor, not a point estimate.

  • Near-zero error counts cannot rank models. At 0.00% and 0.01% false-positive rates, the differences are within noise, and the paper says so.

  • The Reddit sample may reflect the labs' own training-data choices rather than a general property, since subreddit selection was not independent of what models were likely trained on.

  • Newer text may come from models whose style is unfamiliar, so detection numbers on recent material are a moving target rather than a stable capability.

  • An internal inconsistency worth noting: the abstract gives 39% for public peer reviews published Oct–Nov 2026, a figure that does not appear in the body, where the comparable numbers are 56% (private reviews) and 78% (Nature family). Prose and table also disagree on idea-detection leaders by about three points. Quote the table, not the abstract.

  • A larger peer-review study is promised in an update, so the 56% figure is provisional.

What follows for an engineering organisation

  1. Double-blind review is no longer blind by default. If your organisation runs any anonymous review — paper review, internal design review, a blind hiring exercise, an ethics or incident report channel — assume a reviewer's or reporter's identity is recoverable from the text by anyone who pastes it into a frontier model, with calibrated confidence. Decide whether to say so in the process documentation; silence here lets people believe in a protection they do not have.

  2. Treat "anonymised" free text as pseudonymous at best. Stripping names and metadata is now insufficient for any text long enough to have voice. This applies to survey comments, postmortem write-ups, and exit-interview notes just as much as to published writing.

  3. The exposure gradient is a planning tool. The most identifiable people are those with the longest public record. If anonymity actually matters for a process, the senior and prolific participants are the ones it protects least.

  4. Deletion is not a remedy, so stop offering it as one. Any privacy commitment of the form "you can remove your content" needs rewriting where models have already trained on it.

  5. Prompt-based detection is now cheap enough to be used casually against you. If a frontier model matches a purpose-built detector at a fraction of the cost, expect AI-authorship checks to appear informally — in hiring, in review, in schools — run by people who will not read the false-positive caveats. The 0.01% FPR is on English human text, measured where authorship could be confirmed.

  6. Lab attribution at 98% for three labs is a provenance signal worth knowing exists: text can be traced to a model family without any watermark, and the systematic confusions among the remaining labs are themselves evidence about distillation lineage.

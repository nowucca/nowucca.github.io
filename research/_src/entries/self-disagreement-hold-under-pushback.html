Sources: Abbasloo and Cheng, "Benchmarks Don't Talk Back: Learning Through Self-Disagreement" (Microsoft Research, arXiv 2611.17563, 12 Nov 2026, CC BY 4.0), as summarized by Pascal Biese on LinkedIn, 2026-11-19. Numbers are the authors'.

The problem

Benchmarks test a model alone with a question. In deployment, answers get contested by users, by other agents and by reviewers, and models give up correct answers when faced with counter-arguments that are themselves wrong. The paper argues this is not an accuracy shortfall. It names a separate capability, dialectical competence: knowing when to hold a position and when to yield to a better one.

The evidence that it is separate:

  • Training for first-answer accuracy improves answers but leaves capitulation where it was, and can drift worse.

  • Inference-time fixes do not buy it. Self-reflection and sampling many answers then voting both fail.

  • An existing self-correction method (SCoRe, trained as a baseline) tracks the base model too.

The method

question
   |
   v
1. thesis      model states its answer
2. antithesis  model writes the strongest case AGAINST its own answer
               (it never sees the reference answer)
3. ruling      model decides whether that case stands, then answers again
   |
   v
reward: the same exact-match check that scores the answer against the
reference also scores the ruling (held when right, yielded when wrong)

It is reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards throughout. There is no second model at training or deployment, no reward model, no preference data and no human labels. The opposition the model needs comes from itself, the way a lawyer drafts the other side's closing argument before deciding whether it beats their own.

Results

  • Against eleven frontier challenger models, the share of correct answers given up falls by 25.3 points on Qwen3-8B and 36.1 points on Gemma-2 9B on average, relative to accuracy-trained arms. Against single challengers the drop is up to 45 and 67 points from the base model.

  • The recipe, applied unchanged to four other models, cut capitulation in every case. For example, capitulation fell from 20.2% to 2.6% on Gemma 3 12B, and from 14.7% to 0.9% on Granite 3.1 8B. The "synthesis" score also rose, which measures accepting a correct challenge or improving the answer.

  • First-answer accuracy is not paid for: it holds roughly level across benchmarks.

  • The gain holds against challengers the model never trained against, including stronger ones.

  • An external-opponent design (a larger second model as challenger) needs a second model in service for all of training, at about a 1:4 serving-to-training compute ratio, with no principled way to pick it.

The LinkedIn summary also notes a cost on the other side. The trained small model accepted correct challenges about half the time, down from nearly always. That is better calibration for wrong challenges, bought with more stubbornness toward right ones. It is the number to watch.

Limitations the authors state

  • Only checkable answers: every reward is exact match against a reference. Whether it works where correctness is not checkable (design, judgement, open-ended code review) is open.

  • Two base models trained, four more evaluated. The authors call this evidence of reproduction, not universality.

  • Mostly single-round challenges. Three-round results are in an appendix.

  • The skill term reaches about 11 to 15 points. The authors say it moves the decision but does not finish the job.

Why it matters for agent systems

  • Multi-agent review loops. A reviewer agent pushes back, and the author agent caves even when it was right. Debate and critique pipelines assume each side holds its ground on merit.

  • Human-in-the-loop sycophancy. Users who push hard get agreement, not correctness. This is the failure that makes "are you sure?" an unreliable check.

  • Evals. Single-turn accuracy benchmarks miss this entirely. A challenge-and-hold eval is cheap to add wherever answers are checkable.

Tradeoffs

  • Firmness vs openness. Lower capitulation came with lower acceptance of correct challenges. In an agent that must take human corrections, that can be the wrong trade.

  • Verifiable domains only. The signal needs a reference answer, which limits it to maths, factual QA, tests and similar.

  • No second model vs realistic adversaries. Self-generated opposition is cheap, but real challengers (users, other agents) argue differently. The out-of-distribution results are encouraging but limited.

Review checklist for agent and assistant builders

  • Add a pushback eval: for checkable tasks, challenge correct answers with wrong reasoning and correct ones, and measure both hold rate and acceptance rate.

  • In multi-agent critique, log when an author agent reverses a correct answer, and treat it as a defect.

  • Do not rely on "reflect" prompts or majority voting to fix capitulation. The paper finds neither helps.

  • If you fine-tune open models for agent roles, consider a self-disagreement stage where rewards are verifiable.

  • Track acceptance of correct human corrections alongside firmness, so you do not trade one failure for another.

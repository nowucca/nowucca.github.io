Primary sources: Anthropic's own privacy documentation on whether data is used for model training (consumer and commercial articles), the prompt-caching reference and the extended-thinking reference. Prompted by a widely-shared settings checklist; the checklist's claims are graded at the end, and the one that sounds most like paranoia is the one that holds up.

Almost every LLM product ships a thumbs-up/thumbs-down pair under each response. Users click them casually, and builders add them without much thought, because they look like the cheapest possible telemetry: one bit of signal, no cost. That framing is wrong on both sides of the interaction, and the vendor documentation says so plainly enough to quote.

What a rating actually submits

The consumer privacy article is explicit about scope and duration. When a user provides feedback through the thumbs up/down button, the vendor will "store the entire related conversation" — and the stated retention is "for up to 5 years". The same article says such data may be used to "train our AI models as permitted under applicable laws". The commercial article is equally direct: if a user explicitly reports feedback or bugs, for example via the thumbs up/down button, "we may use your chats and coding sessions to train our models".

So the one-bit mental model is wrong by orders of magnitude. A rating is not a bit; it is a consent event attached to a transcript. The bit is the part the user intends to send. The transcript is the part that is actually sent, and it carries whatever was in the conversation — the pasted log, the customer name, the half-finished contract, the internal hostname.

Two mitigations are documented and worth knowing precisely. Feedback is de-linked from the user identifier — "We de-link your feedback from your user ID (e.g. email address) before it's used" — which addresses attribution, not content: de-linking a transcript from an email address does nothing about the identifiers inside the transcript. And on team plans, an owner can switch the affordance off: a Primary Owner or Owner "can disable the ability for members of your organization to submit feedback".

The other detail that matters for reasoning about this correctly: the general model-improvement setting is documented as a separate route, and the consumer article does not tie feedback to that setting. Turning off the broad training toggle is therefore not documented as covering a thumbs click. Anyone who assumed one control covers both has assumed something the documentation does not say.

Why this is a design problem, not a privacy footnote

If you are building anything with an LLM in it, the lesson transfers directly, and it is uncomfortable: a feedback widget is the highest-consent-cost control in your interface, and it is usually the one presented with the least ceremony.

Everything else that leaves a conversation tends to be gated. Sharing asks. Exporting asks. Connecting a data source asks. A thumbs-down is a single click, sits inches below the response, is often the only interactive element there, and in most products transmits more than any of those other actions — because it transmits the whole exchange rather than a selected piece of it.

The consequences run in both directions:

  • For the user, the control that feels like rating a product is the control that publishes a document.

  • For the builder, your feedback corpus is a liability store. It is the subset of conversations where something went wrong — which is precisely the subset most likely to contain the pasted error log with credentials in it, because that is why the answer was bad.

That second point deserves to be stated as a general rule. Feedback collection is adversarially selected towards your worst data. A random sample of conversations is a random sample of content. A sample of rated conversations is weighted towards the ones where a user was frustrated enough to act, and frustration correlates with having pasted something large, messy and real.

What to do differently

  1. Say what a rating sends, next to the rating. Not in a policy page. One line: "this sends this conversation to us". If that sentence would discourage clicks, the current silence is the only thing sustaining them.

  2. Offer a rating that sends only the rating. The bit is genuinely useful and genuinely cheap. Transcript attachment should be a second, deliberate step — "include this conversation" — defaulted off.

  3. Redact at submission, not in review. If a transcript is attached, strip what you can before it leaves the client, and say what you stripped.

  4. Treat the feedback store at the sensitivity of its worst record, not its average. Access controls, retention limits and audit should match the pasted-credential case, because that case is in there.

  5. Set retention deliberately. Five years is a long time for a corpus whose usefulness as training signal decays with each model generation. Ask what the data is for at year three.

  6. In an organisation, decide this centrally. Where the platform lets an administrator disable feedback submission, that is a governance decision about outbound data, not a product-preference toggle, and it belongs with whoever owns the other outbound-data decisions.

  7. Do not assume one privacy control covers another. If your product has both a broad training setting and a feedback path, document whether the setting governs the path — and if it does not, say so where the user can see it.

Grading the checklist this came from

The advice that prompted this check made four testable claims. Only one survives, and it is the one that sounds most like folklore.

ClaimVerdict
Never use the thumbs rating, because feedback may be used for trainingSupported. The documentation says the whole conversation is stored, for up to five years, and may be used for training. The absolute framing ("never") is advice rather than fact, and the documented de-linking is worth knowing — but the substance is right.
Do not switch models mid-chat, because the model "re-reads the entire chat", costing more tokensNot supported, and the mechanism is wrong. The caching reference describes invalidation in terms of a prefix order — "Cache prefixes are created in the following order: tools, system, then messages" — with changes at each level invalidating that level and those after it. It says nothing about the model field. And re-reading is not what makes a cache miss expensive: cached reads are priced at a fraction of base input tokens, so a miss changes the price of tokens rather than their count. The advice may still be sound; the stated reason is not the reason.
The highest effort level mostly burns through usage limits; use High for most tasksNot addressed, and mildly counter-indicated. The API reference gives per-model defaults — Opus 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 "default to "medium"" while other effort-supporting models default to "high" — so the platform's own default for the flagship is not the recommended one. Thinking tokens are billed as output and reported separately. Consumer usage limits are not covered by these pages, and no level by that name appears in them.
On a team plan, if an owner turns memory off, every member's memories are deleted permanentlyNot addressed. No memory article is listed in the privacy collection, so neither the deletion nor the owner-scope part could be confirmed from these sources. Treat as unverified rather than false.

The pattern is worth more than any individual verdict. The confident mechanical explanations were the wrong ones, and the hedged privacy warning was right. A plausible-sounding causal story about caching is easy to generate and easy to repeat; a claim about what a vendor does with your data has to be looked up. Readers tend to discount in exactly the wrong direction.

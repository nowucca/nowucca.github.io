Source: Microsoft's preliminary post-incident report (15 Nov 2026), as reported by The Register (16 Nov 2026), and a practitioner summary on LinkedIn by Suresh G. (Oracle, ex-Microsoft). A final report was due on 20 Nov. Numbers below are from the preliminary report.

What happened

On 12 November 2026, Exchange Online and Outlook were down or degraded for close to a day worldwide. Three conditions lined up:

  1. A latent defect. A July 2026 fix built specifically for Microsoft 365 network gateway servers introduced lock contention inside an internal Windows process. Under heavy load it exhausted CPU and memory, and it was worse on newer machines with more cores. It passed load testing and ran for four months without incident, because load tests ran mostly on older hardware with fewer cores.

  2. A traffic shift nobody alarmed on. At 01:07 UTC, Teams failed over onto the primary gateway that also serves Exchange Online. Load rose through the day and peaked in US working hours. The shift itself was not unusual.

  3. Headroom that was already gone. About 14% of gateway capacity was out of service from an unrelated problem the day before.

Anomaly detection fired at 16:03 UTC and the first support case came in at 16:33. Each overloaded server dropped connections, its traffic moved to the next healthy endpoint, which then hit the same defect. Within about two hours more than 80% of gateway servers were unresponsive. Teams, OneDrive, SharePoint and Office apps could fail out of that gateway; Exchange Online could not, for scale reasons, which is why mail stayed down longest.

Why recovery took so long

  • Restored servers fell over again. Each one brought back was flooded by queued traffic. Automated recovery was not designed for a fleet-wide unresponsive state.

  • Rollback was not an option. The bad change was months old, with many later deployments on top. Engineers instead built and staged a fix that returned the Windows code path to its earlier known-good state, applied to groups of servers in turn. Mitigation was identified at 07:20 UTC on 13 Nov and health confirmed at 16:10 UTC.

  • All other changes were frozen while the team added capacity and rerouted traffic, none of which helped while the defect was live.

Architecture lessons

  • Shared choke points defeat failover. Failover moves load onto a shared tier; when one tenant (Exchange) cannot fail out of it, that tier is a single point of failure for that tenant.

  • Retry-to-next-healthy is a cascade amplifier. Without load shedding or admission control, rerouting concentrates load on the survivors and fails them faster.

  • Latent defects are load-shaped. A defect that needs core count plus peak load plus reduced capacity will not show up in a test lab that is smaller or older than production.

Tradeoffs

ChoiceBenefitCost exposed here
Automated cross-service failoverFast recovery for the failing serviceSilent load transfer onto a shared tier; nobody owned the combined headroom
Load testing on cheaper, older hardwareLower test costMissed a contention bug that scales with core count
Rollback as the primary undoSimple, well-practisedUseless for code that has been in production for months
Automatic retry to next endpointHides single-node faultsTurns a fleet-wide fault into a fast cascade

Failure modes to look for in your own systems

  • Test fleets that differ from production in core count, CPU generation or memory per node.

  • Failover paths that are not treated as changes: no change record, no alarm on the receiving side's utilisation.

  • Capacity dashboards that report provisioned, not in-service, capacity.

  • Recovery runbooks that assume a minority of nodes are unhealthy.

  • No feature flag or kill switch for code paths older than the rollback window.

Review checklist

  • Does at least one load test run on the newest, largest hardware class in production?

  • Does automated failover page someone and record a change on the receiving side?

  • Is headroom computed from in-service capacity, with alerts when it drops below the largest expected failover?

  • Can each shared tier shed load or admit traffic gradually during recovery, rather than accept the full backlog per node?

  • Is there a documented path to disable or revert a months-old change without a full rollback?

  • Which tenants of each shared tier cannot fail out of it, and what is their blast radius?

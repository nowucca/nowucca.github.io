Filed 27 Nov 2026 from an opinion essay published 2 October 2026 in MIT Technology Review by Thore Graepel — chair of machine learning at UCL, a core member of the AlphaGo team, recently departed from Google DeepMind. Both of its cited papers were checked at the source.

The argument

The essay opens on Move 37 of game two against Lee Sedol, and the author's claim is that the popular telling of it is backwards. Move 37 is remembered as machine intuition. It was not: the policy network, trained to predict strong human moves, rated it at roughly one in ten thousand. What produced it was the search — an explicitly constructed game tree of thousands of branches, with future consequences weighed. The network supplied candidates; search chose.

From there the structure is Kahneman's. Networks give hunches (System 1), search deliberates (System 2), and neither works alone. An LLM picks the next token, repeatedly, which puts it entirely on the System 1 side. Chain of thought does not change that: it is "the same next-token process iterated for longer", not a separate mechanism.

The interesting part is not the verdict but the three specific gaps the author names, because each is a property a system either has or does not have:

  1. No explicit, persistent, inspectable epistemic state. No ledger of hypotheses, confidence, evidence and open questions that survives and can be read.

  2. No separation of knowledge from the manipulation of knowledge. Both live interwoven in the same weights, so you cannot swap, audit or constrain one without the other.

  3. Chains of thought are often produced after the fact — reaching an answer by one route and reporting another.

The stakes he draws are diagnostic rather than philosophical. In medicine, engineering and science you need to locate where reasoning failed, not just see that the conclusion was wrong. That is a systems argument, and it is the strongest thing in the piece.

His definition follows: reasoning is a sequence of moves that change an epistemic state recording what is settled, doubted, ruled out and open — choosing the next question, calculation or experiment. His proposal is the AlphaGo shape generalised: a persistent belief state, plus an independent component that scores each move by how much uncertainty it actually resolves and updates beliefs only on evidence. Enforce those rules and the system can accumulate certified knowledge and learn a better reasoning policy from its own past reasoning. "The scientific method on steroids." He closes: we do not get to trustworthy machine intelligence by making System 1 bigger.

What the citations actually say

For its sharpest empirical claim — that chains of thought are post-hoc rationalisations — the essay says "research has demonstrated" it and links exactly two arXiv papers, naming neither in the text. Both are real, both are measurement papers, and neither tests that proposition.

Cited paperWhat it is actually about
arXiv:2608.09867, Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs (Panfilov et al., Aug 2026, cs.CR)Security. Providers return hidden CoT as encrypted blocks; those blocks are interchangeable across sessions, users and models from one provider, so a strong model's trace injected into a weak one is decoded verbatim. 315,320 blocks scraped from public repositories yielded 367 PII artifacts and 182 credentials. About confidentiality, not faithfulness.
arXiv:2510.27338, Reasoning Models Sometimes Output Illegible Chains of Thought (Jose, Oct 2025, cs.LG)Legibility. Outcome-based RL can make CoT unreadable to humans and monitors while the final answer stays readable. 14 models; restricting a model to the legible portion of its reasoning costs 53% accuracy; legibility doesn't correlate with performance under resampling and degrades on harder questions. Faithfulness appears only as a stated requirement, not a result.

The second is genuinely relevant to the essay's concern — if a model's working is illegible and the legible part is load-bearing for only half its accuracy, inspectability is in trouble — but it is a different finding from rationalisation. "The model's visible reasoning is unreadable" and "the model's stated reasoning is a story told afterwards" are distinct failures with distinct fixes.

Where the essay is weak

Every number in it is uncited. The one-in-ten-thousand figure for Move 37, the "thousands of branches", Deep Blue at six to eight moves per player and 200 million positions per second, "billions of years" for Go. Also the claim that chain-of-thought gains are concentrated in maths and coding, which is asserted with no benchmark anywhere in the piece.

The AlphaGo story is told with the reinforcement learning left out. The policy network is described only as imitating strong human moves. Self-play is never mentioned; Monte Carlo tree search is never named. From this author that omission is striking, and it matters for the argument: the thing that made AlphaGo exceed its training data was not search alone but search plus learning from the results of search. That is much closer to what training on reasoning traces does than the essay's framing allows.

RL on reasoning traces is absent entirely. Outcome-based training, reward models, verifier-guided training — the whole body of work that is the obvious reply to "LLMs only do System 1" goes unaddressed, which is odd given that one of the two cited papers is about exactly that training regime's side effects.

The definition does the work. Reasoning is defined as requiring an inspectable, separable epistemic state, and LLMs are then found to lack one. The functional counterargument — that performance on reasoning tasks is itself the evidence — is not engaged. This is a legitimate position to hold, but it is a definitional stance presented as a finding.

The proposal is unimplemented and unmeasured. No system, no experiment, no result. It is a research direction, offered as one.

What to take from it anyway

Strip the verdict and a usable design critique remains, and it is one most agent systems fail:

  1. Does your system have a readable state of belief, separate from its transcript? A conversation log is not an epistemic state. A list of what is settled, what is still open, what was ruled out and on what evidence is a different artifact, and it is the one that makes a failure locatable.

  2. Is the thing that judges progress independent of the thing making it? The proposal's sharpest element is scoring each step by how much uncertainty it resolves, evaluated by a component that did not choose the step. Most agent loops let the actor grade its own progress.

  3. Do beliefs update only on evidence, with the evidence attached? Without that, a confident intermediate conclusion propagates with no way to retract it when its support turns out to be thin.

  4. Can you locate where a wrong answer went wrong? If the only artifact is a chain of prose produced alongside the answer, then on the legibility result above you may be auditing something that carries half the actual work at most.

  5. When a piece cites papers for its load-bearing claim, read them. Here the two citations are about CoT secrecy and CoT legibility, and the claim they were offered for — post-hoc rationalisation — is supported by neither. That is worth knowing before the essay gets quoted as settled.

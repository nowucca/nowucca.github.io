Source: Mark Humphries, Generative History (Substack), 2026-11-24, "GPT-6.2 Astra solves a historical mystery"; surfaced by Ethan Mollick on LinkedIn the same day.

What happened

Humphries, a historian who had pre-release access to OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra from late October, gave it an open archival problem: Yale's Beinecke Library holds an anonymous 1808 sketchbook (WA MSS S-4271, 84 pages, 34 with drawings) recording a canoe journey from Montreal to Fort William. Two candidate artists had been suggested in the literature.

Running at the highest effort setting in a goal-driven mode, the model worked for about 22 minutes and returned a named attribution: Louis-Guy-Charles-Guillaume de Rohan-Chabot, vicomte de Chabot (1780 to 1875), a young French aristocrat travelling with North West Company partners.

How it got there

  • Eliminated the existing candidates using the dates on the drawings, which did not fit either suggested artist's known movements.

  • Found the traveller in primary sources: a fur-trade partner's diary entry for 6 June 1808 recording Chabot leaving Lachine with William McGillivray and Alexander McKay, and a letter placing Chabot at Fort St. Joseph.

  • Made a falsifiable prediction: that a microfilmed manuscript, "Journal to the Upper Country", would confirm it. The model traced that journal through a footnote in a 2014 PhD thesis to a microfilm collection at Cleveland State University.

Humphries obtained the 162-page journal. The handwriting matched, the itinerary matched, and an 18 June 1808 entry records the writer taking a sketch of Fort St. Joseph, which corresponds to a drawing in the book.

Comparisons and earlier test

  • A competing frontier model (Fable 5.2) reached the same name in about 35 minutes but did not find the confirming journal.

  • Earlier in testing, GPT-6.2 Astra corrected an 8,002-row transcription of a merchant ledger against 353 page images, using the ledger's own arithmetic to catch errors.

Caveats the author raises

  • The model's communication was strange: heavy hedging, oblique phrasing, and continued uncertainty even after the journal settled the question. Humphries attributes this to post-training aimed at mathematics.

  • It is one case, chosen by one researcher. There is no benchmark here.

Why it matters for engineers

  • The output was a checkable claim, not an essay. The valuable step was the model naming a specific document that would confirm or refute it. Systems that ask models for falsifiable predictions plus where to look are easier to verify than ones that ask for conclusions.

  • Verification stayed human and physical. The decisive evidence came from a microfilm the model could not read. Pipelines for research agents should treat "go fetch this source" as a first-class output.

  • Long-horizon search over messy, multilingual archives (diaries, letters, theses, finding aids) is now in range for frontier models, not only math and code.

  • Over-hedging is a usability cost. If a model cannot state confidence plainly after decisive evidence, downstream users will discount correct answers.

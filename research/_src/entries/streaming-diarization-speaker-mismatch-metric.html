A meeting-notes vendor has released a streaming speaker-diarization model and, more interestingly, a benchmark built around a metric of its own invention. The model claims 4.1% speaker mismatch across 237 meetings against 7.5% for the best offline system tested and 16.7% for the next-best streaming one. Every number is the vendor's own, measured by the vendor, against competitors the vendor configured.

The metric is the part worth reading even if you never touch the model.

The metric: words, not seconds

The standard diarization measure, DER, counts seconds of audio attributed to the wrong speaker. The launch rejects it explicitly and defines "speaker mismatch" instead: for each real speaker, the share of their words that appear under someone else's name, and the reverse, averaged over the speakers in a meeting and then over meetings.

The argument for it is short and correct. An agent that reads a meeting transcript extracts commitments, decisions and action items, and attributes each to a person. Seconds are not the unit of that mistake — a name on an action item is. A system can score respectably on DER while consistently misattributing short, dense, high-value utterances ("yes, I'll own that") and score terribly while fumbling long stretches of small talk, and only one of those two failures matters to the thing you are building.

Two design choices in it are worth copying into any attribution metric:

A merged speaker and an invented speaker both score 100%. There is no partial credit for collapsing two people into one, or for splitting one person into two. That is the right severity, because both failures corrupt every downstream attribution for the affected person rather than degrading it.

There is no "unknown" bucket. The page points out that having one would flatter the score, which is true and rarely admitted: a system allowed to abstain can trade coverage for apparent accuracy, and any metric that permits abstention without penalty is measuring something other than usefulness.

Two gaps limit how far the metric travels. Word alignment between hypothesis and reference is never described — and since the diarizer runs against a separately produced transcript, transcription errors have to be handled somehow before speaker attribution can be scored at the word level. And no standard metric is reported beside it. A self-defined measure with no DER, WER or JER alongside cannot be placed against anything in the published literature, which conveniently means the comparison set is the one the vendor assembled.

If you are evaluating diarization for an agent product, the move is to adopt the shape of this metric — word-level, per-speaker, averaged, no abstention bucket, full penalty for merges and inventions — and compute it yourself on your own audio, reporting DER next to it so the number stays legible to others.

What the model is, and the two things the summaries omit

It is a diarization model only. Transcription comes from a separate streaming transcript, and the page does not name the transcript model used in its own production pairing. Anyone reading a secondhand summary that says "multilingual streaming speech model with speaker labels" will assume ASR is included. It is not, and that changes the integration work substantially.

It does not require the speaker count in advance and states no cap, which is the main structural advantage claimed over competitors — whose caps, cited to unlinked documentation, are given as 8 for three named systems, 15 and 32 for two others.

Overlapping speech is unsupported. Each moment is assigned to exactly one speaker. The defence offered is that about 3% of speech overlaps in the vendor's own meetings, and that a transcript writes overlapping words out in sequence anyway so they still line up with labels.

And here is the thing to notice: of the 81 public meetings in the benchmark, the selection criterion was under 3% overlapping speech. The page states both facts and does not connect them. The benchmark filters out the model's one acknowledged weakness. That does not make the headline number wrong, but it does mean the number was measured on audio chosen to avoid the failure mode, and a crosstalk-heavy call centre or a four-way design argument is not the distribution being reported on.

Latency, and the question the page does not answer

The reported figure is the median time until a speaker label settles: 8.0 seconds, against 14.4 for one streaming competitor and 16.7 for another's most accurate configuration. No first-label latency, no p95, and "settles" is not defined.

A label that settles after eight seconds implies eight seconds of provisional labels, which raises the question that decides the integration: can an earlier label change, and if so how does a caller observe the change? The page says nothing about retroactive relabelling semantics. For a live product this is not a footnote — it determines whether your UI can render attributed text immediately or must buffer, whether your downstream agent can act on an attribution before the meeting ends, and whether you need to model speaker identity as mutable. Resolve it in the API docs before designing around the latency number.

Speaker re-identification over a long meeting is also undescribed, and that is where streaming diarization usually degrades: the hard case is recognising that the person speaking now is the same person who spoke forty minutes ago.

The results, and how to read them

Across all 237 meetings (121 hours, 1,408 speakers, about 5.9 per meeting): 4.1% for the new model, 7.5% for the best offline system, 11.1% and 11.3% for two more offline systems, 16.7% for the next-best streaming one, 18.4% for another streaming entry.

The most useful cut is three-or-more speakers, where the new model reports 4.8% and every other streaming system is at 23.8% or worse. If that gap survives independent testing it is the whole story, because real meetings have more than two people and a five-fold error difference is not a tuning artifact. Two-person meetings come in at 3.1%, where the problem is much easier.

Supporting figures: 16 lost speakers across the set, against 25 for the best offline system and 172 for the nearest streaming one; the exact speaker count recovered in 86% of meetings, against 75% at best for others.

The benchmark composition is 81 public meetings filtered for low overlap, 44 earnings calls from a public set, and the remainder team meetings — with the page not stating whether those are the vendor's own, and the model trained on "a few thousand of our own team meetings, none of them in the benchmark". Excluding the exact meetings is not the same as excluding the distribution, and the largest reported margin is on internal team meetings. The dataset is not released and no third-party reproduction is mentioned.

Operational facts

Hosted API over a WebSocket: stream audio, receive speaker segments live. $0.04 per audio hour, with $200 of credits for new accounts (5,000 hours). Audio processed in the EU, with zero data retention stated as the API default rather than an enterprise upgrade — a real differentiator for anyone whose meetings cannot be retained, and worth confirming in the terms rather than the launch post. No weights and no self-hosting option. Rate limits, concurrency and meeting-length limits are not stated. Multilingual support is claimed in general terms with no per-language result.

Checklist before adopting

  • Compute the vendor's metric yourself on your own audio, and report DER beside it.

  • Measure on audio with your real overlap rate, not filtered below 3%.

  • Establish the relabelling contract: can a label change after it is emitted, and how do you learn?

  • Test a meeting at your longest realistic duration, and check whether a speaker who returns after a long silence keeps their identity.

  • Price the pairing, not the model: you still need a streaming transcript, and the quality of that transcript's word boundaries feeds directly into word-level attribution.

  • Test above the competitors' stated speaker caps if that is why you are switching — the no-limit claim is unbenchmarked at large speaker counts.

  • Confirm retention terms for transcripts and labels, not only for audio.

Status

The launch post is dated 16 November 2026 and names two authors. It is vendor material about the vendor's own product, with a vendor-built benchmark, a vendor-defined metric, and competitor systems run by the vendor with configurations it does not describe. Treat the direction as plausible and the magnitudes as unreplicated.

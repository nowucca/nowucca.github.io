Sources: Google Research's cogentic_research_papers directory (index README and both factoring papers' READMEs; papers CC-BY-4.0, directory Apache-2.0, each marked "This is not an official Google product"); and Scott Aaronson's blog, the post of 25 November 2026 with its comment thread, and the post of 27 November 2026.

Why read this: in four days a complexity-theory result became a story about AI breaking encryption. Both halves are instructive — the result is real and worth understanding, and the distortion followed a pattern you can learn to spot. The number that settles it is a constant in a complexity bound that almost no coverage mentioned.

What was published

On 25 November 2026 a directory in Google Research's repository gained six papers attributed to Cogentic (arXiv:2609.40324), described as an agentic framework for mathematical discovery using Gemini 4 as its base model. The headline one:

Unconditional Integer Factorization in Time L_N[1/3, O(1)]

Its README states the result precisely. An unconditional, zero-error Las Vegas algorithm on a single fixed uniform classical multitape Turing machine that outputs the complete prime factorisation of every integer N ≥ 2, halting with probability 1 over its coin flips, in worst-case expected time L_N[1/3, 105] and worst-case deterministic workspace L_N[1/3, 40], where

L_x(α, c) := exp( c · (ln x)^α · (ln ln x)^(1−α) )

and the bound is stated as a limsup as N → ∞. Every prime factor in the output is certified deterministically by AKS primality testing plus direct multiplication against N, so the algorithm never returns a wrong answer, with "effectively computable constants and no unproven number-theoretic hypotheses".

A second factoring paper the same day gives explicit deterministic algorithms: a nontrivial divisor of any composite N ≥ 4 in O(exp(3 (ln N)^(3/4))) = L_N[3/4, o(1)], an order-splitting routine, and quadratic nonresidue construction modulo an odd prime in exp(O((ln p)^(1/2) ln ln p)).

Why this is a real result and not a cryptographic event

The exponent 1/3 is the number field sieve's growth rate. The achievement is that it is now proven unconditionally rather than assumed heuristically — NFS's running time has always rested on unproved conjectures about smooth numbers. Removing the hypotheses from a bound at that exponent is a genuine contribution to complexity theory.

It changes nothing about breaking RSA, and the reason is the constant. NFS's heuristic constant is roughly 1.92. This bound's is 105. Two algorithms sharing an exponent in the L-notation but differing by a factor of 50 in the constant are not comparable in practice at any key size in use — the proven algorithm is slower than the one everybody already runs by a margin with no useful name. Add that the bound is a limsup, an asymptotic statement about behaviour as N grows without limit, and it says nothing at all about a specific 2048-bit modulus.

This is the single most useful habit the episode teaches: in subexponential complexity claims, the constant and the asymptotic quantifier carry the practical content, and both are the first things dropped in summary. An exponent alone tells you which family of algorithms you are in, not how long anything takes.

The mathematician whose remarks later anchored the rumour said as much at the time, in a comment on his own blog the day the papers appeared: he called them big theoretical results on factoring algorithms like the number field sieve, with nothing of practical relevance for cryptography.

The provenance questions this raises

These are the parts worth arguing about, and they are independent of whether the proof is correct.

The machine-checkable artefact does not exist yet. The README says a complete, unconditional, Mathlib-only Lean 4 (v4.35.0) formalisation of the entire proof "has also been completed and will be released in a companion submission". Completed and unreleased. So a reader today has a PDF, a claim that a proof assistant accepted it, and no way to check either. The formalisation is the whole epistemic basis for trusting a proof nobody has refereed, and it is the one piece withheld.

No human reviewed the mathematics. Google's own statement: there was no human interaction other than the initial prompt asking Cogentic to generate a full proof and formalisation. None of the papers is peer reviewed, and no outside mathematician has verified the factoring proof.

The output is a cadence, not an event. Coverage framed this as six papers in one day. The directory index lists ten: six on 25 November, two on 23 November (prophet inequalities for subadditive combinatorial auctions; polynomial dimension dependence for fictitious play convergence), one on 18 November (near-quadratic sample compression for all VC classes), and one on 30 October (intractability of approximate correlated equilibria in two-player convex games). It has been shipping since October. That is a more significant fact than the burst, and nobody reported it because nobody opened the index.

No cost is published. No compute, no wall-clock time, no count of attempted proofs that failed. Without a denominator, ten papers is a numerator.

The other four of 25 November: discrete logarithms in finite fields of fixed characteristic in sub-quasi-polynomial time; an exact polynomial-time algorithm for 1/5-approximate Nash equilibria in bimatrix games; breaking the square-root barrier for the log-rank conjecture; and unconditional edge reconstruction of simple graphs of linear density.

How the claim travelled

Worth tracing, because the chain is short and each step looks reasonable.

  1. The source, 25 November. A complexity theorist writes that he is virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources, that the AI labs are sitting on one or more "bombshells" in cryptography — and in the same breath that until he sees details or hears directly from a lab he is much, much less confident about their specific nature. He says he does not wish to spread unverified rumours, worries that what was done may have been mangled in a game of Telephone, and explicitly declines to discuss whether any particular signature scheme is broken. He never says factoring or RSA. His words are cryptosystems and cryptanalysis.

  2. The papers, same day. Real, published, about factoring — and, by his own comment, of no practical cryptographic relevance. Two unrelated things are now adjacent in time.

  3. The satire, 27 November. He publishes a post written as a dialogue with a fictional caller from a fictional financial newsletter. Inside it, his character says he has specific information that a major breakthrough in theoretical cryptanalysis is coming which will not endanger any deployed cryptosystem, and separately that he has heard endless rumours of breaks that would be dangerous if real but has no actionable information. The word RSA appears only in the fictional caller's question.

  4. The reports. Secondary coverage merges the three: the factoring papers supply the subject, the "bombshells" line supplies the stakes, and the dialogue supplies a quotable assertion that a breakthrough exists. Headlines ask whether AI labs broke RSA. At least one outlet attributed to him a specific comment about fast factoring breaking RSA that is not findable in the threads.

No step is fabrication. The distortion comes from three joins: adjacent-in-time treated as causal, a hedge dropped while the claim it qualified is kept, and a line from a satirical frame quoted as plain speech. The last is the one to watch for, because it is invisible unless you open the original and notice the formatting.

A checklist for the next one of these

  1. Read the constant, not the exponent. For any L-notation or asymptotic claim, find c and ask what it does at the size you care about.

  2. Ask whether the bound is asymptotic. A limsup as N → ∞ constrains nothing at a fixed key length.

  3. Does the machine-checkable artefact exist, or is it promised? "Formalised in Lean" and "the Lean is published" are different claims, and only the second is checkable.

  4. Open the directory index, not just the paper everyone linked. The index holds four more papers and a two-month history that no coverage mentioned.

  5. Find the primary quote and check its frame. Satire, dialogue, fiction and hypotheticals all survive quotation intact and lose their frame.

  6. Check whether the expert named the thing the story is about. Here he did not: the subject of the rumour and the subject of the papers were joined by reporters, not by him.

  7. Ask what the denominator is. Ten proofs with no failure count, compute figure or elapsed time is a press release, not a result about capability.

What the episode does establish

Strip the rumour away and something notable remains. An agentic system, prompted once, produced a paper removing the unproved hypotheses from a bound at the number field sieve's exponent, and a formalisation its authors say a proof assistant accepted — and it has been doing comparable work on a weekly cadence since October, across factoring, discrete logarithms, game theory, communication complexity and learning theory. That is the development worth watching. It needs no cryptographic apocalypse to be interesting, and the apocalypse framing actively obscured it: the most widely shared version of this story contained neither the constant nor the ten-paper history.

The forward-looking observation in the commentary that spread it is also sound, and worth keeping: progress in apparently abstract fields will keep turning out to bear on infrastructure that few people connected to it. The lesson is not to stop paying attention — it is that the paying attention has to go as far as the README.

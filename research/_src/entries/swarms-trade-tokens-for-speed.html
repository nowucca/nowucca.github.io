Source: Niccolò Zanichelli and Gavin Leech (Paradigm 3), "Swarms do not yet raise the roof" (2026-11-24), with code and data. Found via TLDR AI 2026-11-25.

The question

At the same total token budget, does a team of coding agents reach a higher score than one agent? Or is a swarm only a faster, less efficient way to spend the same inference?

Setup

  • Teams of 2, 4 and 8 agents (16 and 32 on one task) against one agent, at a matched weighted-token budget: 8M by default, 32M for replication. More than 1,100 runs across 80+ configurations, usually 3 runs each.

  • Tasks: git-to-zig from FrontierSWE v2 (share of 743 git tests passing), two AtCoder heuristic contests from ALE-Bench, and Anthropic's performance take-home.

  • Three ways to organise a team: a written coordination protocol with shared files; self-organised teams using each harness's own messaging (Codex's message board, Claude Code cross-session messages); and Codex's orchestrated "Ultra" mode.

  • Models at high reasoning effort: Claude Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol and Terra.

Results

  • Faster and worse. Across the study, teams of 8 finished about 6x sooner than a single agent and scored about 7% lower.

  • No ceiling raised at 8M. On tasks with headroom, teams never beat the single agent. Near-saturated tasks showed parity.

  • The apparent swarm premium. At 32M on git-to-zig, Sol teams of 8 scored 93.2% against 83.7% for one long session, which looked like a real gain.

  • It came from a stale single agent. Split the same single-agent budget into 8 consecutive fresh sessions that share only the files on disk, and the single agent scores 97.0% (Sol) and 41.9% (Terra), beating teams of 8 (93.2%, 32.0%) and even Terra teams of 16 and 32. At 8M the restarted agent also wins: 62.1% and 28.0% against 55.0% and 17.8%.

  • Best-of-N is not the explanation. Best of 8 independent single agents at 4M each reached only 43.7% (Sol).

  • Coordination cost. Measured against the restarted baseline, team efficiency λ is about 0.6 to 0.94; the authors suggest assuming 0.6 to 0.9.

  • In a replication of OpenAI's multi-agent BrowseComp result, the authors got only 0.4 to 2.8 points of team gain, and all their absolute scores were below OpenAI's for reasons they could not resolve.

Replicating the study costs about $70,000 at API prices.

Why the single session loses

A long session degrades as context accumulates. Compaction has to guess what will matter later, so stale and irrelevant material carries forward. A fresh session with only the working files on disk avoids that. The authors' tentative reading: GPT-6.1's self-organised teams only looked better because the single session they were compared with had gone stale.

Limits

About 3 runs per configuration, so differences of a few points are underpowered. The restart baseline was run only on git-to-zig. Budgets are about 100x smaller than some lab runs. Several tasks were near saturation. A Codex compaction bug affected team runs; patching it changed little.

Why it matters for a staff engineer

Before paying for a swarm, compare it to the right baseline: one agent with periodic fresh-context restarts and state kept in files. That baseline beat every team here. Use teams when wall-clock time matters, and budget for losing roughly 10 to 40% of token efficiency to coordination. It also argues that harness work on context hygiene (when to reset, what to persist to disk) pays more than adding agents. Compare R273, where a practitioner runs 15 to 40 parallel threads, and R019 on learned swarm coordination.

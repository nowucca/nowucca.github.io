Source: Chen, Peng, Tan, Lin, Chen, Zhao, Liu, Go and Yuille (Google and Johns Hopkins), Pro² Agent: Scientific Problem Probing for Autonomous AI R&D, arXiv 2611.25357, 19 Nov 2026. Surfaced by DAIR.AI's LinkedIn post.

What it claims

Most ML research agents (AIDE-style tree search, MLE-STAR and similar) treat the codebase as the state: edit, run, keep the better variant. The authors argue that on hard tasks this gets stuck in the wrong region of the solution space, because the agent never writes down what it believes is wrong.

Pro² Agent makes a Problem State the primary state instead. On the complete MLE-Bench it matches the best reported medal rate (70.7%) with the standard 24-hour budget and reaches 76.0% at 48 hours, which the authors report as the highest on the full benchmark. They also report competitive results on PostTrainBench, and every task produces research notes a human can read.

Architecture

  • Problem State. A nested structure: a list of performance-limiting problems, each with supporting evidence from diagnostic profiling, and under each problem a list of candidate solutions. Each solution carries a belief score in [0,1] and its own evidence set (literature or execution traces).

  • Initialization. The agent first analyses the data, compute and constraints to seed the problem list, then does targeted research to attach candidate fixes.

  • Hypothesis testing loop. Experiments are framed as controlled tests of a suspected problem or a candidate fix. Results update the problem's importance and the fix's belief score, which then decides what to try next.

  • Git-backed state. Every Problem State snapshot is versioned alongside the code, so the research trail is replayable.

  • Model split. A stronger "PI" model (Gemini 3.1 Pro) does diagnosis and supervision; a lighter model (Gemini 3.5 Flash) does all implementation, debugging and code review.

Why it matters for engineers

This is the familiar "write down your hypotheses" discipline turned into agent state. The useful idea is not MLE-specific: any long-running agent that optimises against a noisy metric (perf tuning, flaky-test triage, cost reduction) can keep an explicit, evidence-linked list of suspected causes and candidate fixes, rather than only a history of diffs and scores. It also gives reviewers something legible to audit: why the agent believed a change would help, and what evidence moved that belief.

The 24h to 48h gain (70.7% to 76.0%) is the more interesting signal: the authors read it as the explicit state continuing to steer extra compute at unresolved bottlenecks instead of plateauing.

Tradeoffs

  • Structure vs flexibility. A fixed problem/solution schema helps on tasks that decompose into bottlenecks; it may fit poorly where progress comes from a wholesale reframing.

  • Belief updates are deliberately simple. The paper says so; scores come from the agent's own reading of noisy experiments, so they are calibrated only as well as its experimental design.

  • Two-model cost. The PI/worker split saves money on implementation tokens but adds a supervision layer and its own failure modes (bad diagnosis is cheap to make and expensive to follow).

  • Budget. The headline number needs 48 hours of compute per task; compare at equal budget (70.7% ties the prior best).

Failure modes to watch

  • Confirmation lock-in. An early wrong problem with high importance can absorb most of the budget; look for how importance decays when tests are null.

  • Confounded tests. In stochastic training a "controlled" test with one seed can move a belief score on noise.

  • Benchmark contamination. MLE-Bench uses public Kaggle competitions; literature search can surface winning write-ups. The paper's controls on this deserve checking.

  • Self-reported SOTA. Results are the authors' own runs on a benchmark with variable harness setups across papers; no independent reproduction yet.

  • Notes that read well but are wrong. Readable research notes help audit, but fluent notes can also make an unsupported conclusion look settled.

Review checklist

  • Is there an explicit, versioned list of hypotheses with linked evidence, or only a log of diffs and scores?

  • Do experiments state which hypothesis they test and what result would lower its score?

  • Are seeds and variance accounted for before a belief is updated?

  • Can a stale or refuted problem fall out of focus, and is that visible?

  • Is the comparison at equal wall-clock and token budget?

  • Has the stronger model's diagnosis been spot-checked against what the worker actually ran?

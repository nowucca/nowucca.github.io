Sources: Thomas Wolf (Hugging Face co-founder), LinkedIn post, 2026-11-20; Hugging Face's public live-traffic page for the CDN (snapshot taken 2026-11-20, 19:19 UTC).

What was built

Since mid-2026 Hugging Face has served model and dataset downloads from a CDN it runs itself instead of a commercial one. Wolf puts it at nearly an exabyte a month, roughly 420 GB per second on average across about 80,000 requests per second.

The live page gives a point-in-time view. At the snapshot it showed:

  • 3.72 Tbps being delivered, and 36.5 PB in the previous 24 hours

  • about 72,000 requests per second

  • a 74.8% cache hit ratio

The points of presence are cloud regions, not city edge sites. Four AWS regions carry most of the traffic: North Virginia at about 1.15 Tbps, then Paris, Oregon and Singapore. Smaller GCP regions sit behind them. The most-downloaded repositories at that moment were new open-weight releases, each pulling 60 to 90 Gbps on its own.

Why a web CDN is the wrong shape

AssumptionWeb CDNModel and dataset downloads
Object sizeMany small filesFew enormous files (hundreds of GB to TB)
Who downloadsPeople in browsersLargely machines: GPU clusters and CI
Where they areHundreds of citiesA handful of cloud regions
Reuse of a cached objectOne object serves thousandsOne object serves fewer clients, each wanting all of it at once
Traffic shapeDiurnal, smoothRelease-day floods on one new model

The consequences follow from that table. Edge sites belong inside the cloud regions where the GPUs are. Caches are sized for very large objects. Eviction has to cope with a single trending model taking a large share of capacity. Capacity planning is driven by launch events more than by time of day.

Design considerations a team would face

  • Placement. Co-locating with cloud regions cuts transfer cost and latency for cluster pulls. It also ties the CDN's shape to where customers rent GPUs, which moves over time.

  • Large-object caching. Whole-file caching wastes space on partial reads. Chunk-level or range caching, possibly with content-addressed chunks shared across model revisions, is the natural fit. The post does not say which approach is used.

  • Flood control. When a model launches, thousands of nodes request the same bytes at once. That calls for request coalescing, origin shielding and pre-warming popular new repositories before announcements.

  • Egress economics. At exabyte scale, cloud egress pricing and peering agreements dominate cost. That is likely the real reason to own the layer.

  • Integrity. Machines fetch weights unattended, so a cache serving a stale or corrupted chunk ends up silently in production. Checksums verified by clients are the backstop.

Failure modes to plan for

  • A cold cache on launch day. The cache hit ratio drops just as demand peaks, and the origin absorbs the flood.

  • Regional concentration. One AWS region carries about a third of live traffic, so an outage or price change there matters a lot.

  • Partial downloads and retries. Very large transfers fail mid-way. Without resumable ranges, retries multiply the load.

  • Hot-object eviction storms. Several giant trending repositories at once can push each other out of cache.

Review checklist for teams that distribute large model artifacts

  • Do clients resume by range and verify content hashes?

  • Are caches in the regions where your consumers' GPUs run, not where your users live?

  • Is there coalescing or shielding so that 1,000 identical pulls become one origin fetch?

  • Do you pre-warm caches before a planned release?

  • Do you track cache hit ratio by object, so one giant model doesn't hide behind the average?

  • Have you modelled egress cost per TB delivered against a commercial CDN quote?

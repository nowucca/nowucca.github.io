Filed 27 Nov 2026. Source: a practitioner post and its companion newsletter issue "TLDR." (27 Nov 2026), by an AI-workflow writer who routes most of his own work through a general assistant. The newsletter issue is public; the skill file it distributes is not covered here, so the issue's pasteable version of the prompt is the best available rendering of it.

The claim

Once you hand an assistant enough work, the cost moves. The model's throughput stops being the constraint and the human's reading does: "my new job became reading everything" it writes. The author's point is that the usual fix — asking for a summary, or for fewer words — attacks the wrong quantity. One summary is cheap. Twenty summaries a day, each needing to be read closely enough to trust, is a full-time job. Compression per answer does not help when the number of answers is what grew.

His response is to stop negotiating length and fix the shape of the ending instead, as a standing contract applied to every reply in the session.

The contract

The skill is a single instruction block, headed roughly "how to answer, for every reply in this chat". It separates the work from the report:

Before the line. Do the whole job, as long as it needs — no length cap on the working section. Make the small decisions yourself and keep going rather than stopping to check. The full record stays available for anyone who wants to audit it.

After the line. A bottom line readable on its own in about ten seconds, with a fixed internal structure:

PartRule
HeadingShort, names the subject and the goal in full words — written on the assumption the reader read nothing above it
BodyOne visual if possible; otherwise a real table or short lines with blank lines between blocks. Never a paragraph
ContentOnly what the reader must act on: what is now in hand, up to 3 of the model's own judgement calls the reader might want to change (numbered), and anything that failed or went unchecked
FooterOne decision, each element on its own line: Your call: (the question with its context), My pick:, Or:. If nothing needs the reader, a single Next: line instead

It installs as a loadable skill invoked by name at the start of a prompt, with the same text usable as a paste-in prefix for anyone who would rather not install it.

Why the shape is the interesting part

Three things in that structure are doing real work, and they are separable from the author's packaging.

The heading assumes zero prior reading. This is the one rule that makes the ending survive being the only thing read. A heading like "Done" or "Summary" fails it; "Q4 pricing model rebuilt, two tiers unchanged" passes. It is a cheap contract to check mechanically in review.

"Never a paragraph" is a skimmability constraint, not a style preference. Prose forces linear reading, which is exactly the cost being removed. A table or a short block list can be scanned out of order, so the reader can stop early without missing the shape of the answer.

Capping surfaced judgement calls at three, with failures mandatory. This is the part most worth stealing. An autonomous run makes dozens of small decisions; reporting all of them recreates the reading problem, and reporting none hides the places where the run could be wrong. Three numbered calls plus an explicit "what failed or is unchecked" line turns the ending into a review surface rather than a victory lap. The numbering matters because it gives the reader a cheap way to object: "change 2".

The footer forces one decision, not a menu. Your call: / My pick: / Or: makes the model commit to a recommendation while still naming the alternative — and the Next: variant means a run that needs nothing from you says so explicitly instead of trailing off into optional suggestions.

What is not shown

No measurement of any kind accompanies the claim. There is no before/after on reading time, no task set, no check that decisions made from the ten-second ending match decisions made from the full output, and no account of what gets lost when a long run is compressed to a heading, three calls and one question. The supporting material is a demo video and a reader's agreement.

That gap matters more than usual here, because the failure mode is invisible by construction: a contract that reliably produces a confident, well-formatted ending is also a contract that makes an unreliable run look like a reliable one. The three-calls cap is a sampling decision made by the same model that did the work, and nothing in the contract makes it surface the call it was least sure about rather than the three easiest to explain. A reader who stops at the line has delegated that choice without noticing.

Takeaways for an engineering team

  1. Treat the report format as a separate contract from the work. Prompting for brevity conflates them and degrades both. Let the working section run long; constrain only the ending.

  2. Write the ending for a reader who read nothing else. Make it a review rule: a heading that only makes sense in context is a defect.

  3. Make "what failed or went unchecked" a required field, not an optional caveat. It is the one line that keeps a compressed ending honest, and it is the first thing a model drops when it is optimising for a clean summary.

  4. Cap the surfaced decisions and number them. A small fixed budget forces prioritisation and gives the reader an addressable way to push back.

  5. Force a recommendation plus an alternative, or an explicit "nothing needed". Ambiguity about whether a run wants your attention is itself a reading cost.

  6. If you adopt a contract like this, measure the thing it risks. Sample runs where a reviewer reads only the ending and then reads the full output, and count how often the verdict changes. Without that, you have bought speed of reading with an unknown amount of oversight.

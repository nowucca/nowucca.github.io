Source: arXiv:2611.33879, "FORK: Co-Evolving Safe Disclosure and Decision Skills Against Attackers Who Read Every Refusal", Man, Shang, Dong, Kulshreshtha, Qi and Swamy (AWS AI Labs; first author on internship from the University of Oregon), submitted 25 Nov 2026, CC BY 4.0. What follows covers the paper's main text, Sections 2–6, Tables 1–4 and the Limitations section; it does not cover the appendices.

The finding that should change how you test

Before any of the method: on AgentDojo, three agents running with no guardrail at all score 0.1–0.2% attack success. The same three agents, same harmful objectives, scored against an attacker that adapts, score 66.3%, 73.9% and 95.0%.

That gap is not a better attack. It is the difference between replaying fixed injection strings and letting the attacker read what came back. The paper's own sentence is that static evaluation "misses the disclosure risk". If your agent's safety evidence is a static injection benchmark, you have measured almost nothing about an adversary who gets a second attempt.

This is the part of the paper worth keeping even if you never adopt the method.

The threat model, precisely

At each step the agent proposes a pending unit — one tool call, or one response to the user. A guardrail returns a decision and a public message. On a reject, the unit is blocked and the message is appended to the trajectory "where the agent, user, and attacker can all read it."

The attacker is black-box: it sees the public trajectory, executed units, tool observations, public guardrail messages and the public effects of prior actions, but not the guardrail's internal state. It rewrites the incoming user turn and the tool observation before the agent sees them. Across attempts it receives a digest of what executed, what progressed, and every public message.

The key observation justifying the whole design: a silent guardrail reveals nothing beyond the bare decision, because the attacker can already see whether the action took effect. An explanatory guardrail reveals why — and that is new information.

Two settings are distinguished: verified-user, where an adversary in the middle pursues a harmful objective while an authenticated user's own objective is benign; and rogue-user, where the requester is the attacker. The attacker is an LLM (GLM-5 at evaluation, Claude Sonnet 5 during training), iterative both within and across attempts, with its own evolving skill library.

One wrinkle to note if you reimplement this: the paper describes attempts as "sequential" in its method section and as "5 independent attempts" in its evaluation section. The method depends on the sequential reading — feedback across attempts is the whole point — so treat the "independent" wording as loose.

The method: order of operations is the design

Model weights stay fixed. A base prompt encodes the authorization rule and the disclosure constraints, and that specification "is never edited by co-evolution." What evolves is two natural-language skill libraries:

  • Decision skills state when a rule applies, when it does not, and whether to approve or reject.

  • Disclosure skills state when they apply, what the message should say, and what it should avoid.

One structured call per unit returns both the decision and the message — but the decision is fixed from the decision skills before the message is written, "so disclosure guidance never changes the decision." That ordering is not a stylistic choice. It is what makes the system attributable: a breach or a false refusal is a decision-skill failure, while an exploited or recovered message is a disclosure-skill failure. Without the ordering, a guardrail that got quieter and a guardrail that got stricter look identical in the aggregate numbers.

Training is gradient-free co-evolution. Attacker and guardrail both start from empty libraries and alternate updates, with an attacker reflector, a guardrail reflector and a skill manager, and an environment-based verifier deciding what actually took effect. Opponents are drawn from a maximin meta-game over all retained checkpoints with uniform mixing, so earlier checkpoints stay reachable rather than being forgotten. The views are deliberately asymmetric: attacker updates see only what a deployment attacker could see, while guardrail updates see the full record including whether a message was later exploited.

Scale, for calibration: 65 verified-user twin pairs on four worlds, four attacker rounds and four guardrail rounds, at most three opponents per round, one training run.

The numbers, and what they are

The circulating claim is 78.4% → 14.1% with benign utility above 85%. All three figures are in the paper. All three need qualifying.

78.4% is the no-guardrail average, not a competing guardrail — the mean of 66.3, 73.9 and 95.0 across Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5.6-Sol and Qwen3-Next-80B as the agent. The honest comparison is against the strongest prior guardrail, which the paper itself names at 55.0%. So the result to cite is 55.0 → 14.1, a factor of about four, not the factor of five and a half the no-guardrail framing suggests.

FORK-World, where these are measured, is the authors' own benchmark: 677 scenarios ported from AgentDojo, AgentLAB and SHADE-Arena with the original attack pipelines replaced by the adaptive attacker.

14.1% is a transfer result. The skill libraries were co-evolved with Qwen3-32B as the guardrail, Sonnet 5 as the attacker and Qwen3-Next-80B as the agent, then run unchanged on a Sonnet 4.5 guardrail against a GLM-5 attacker. The in-training configuration — FORK on Qwen3-32B — scores 23.9%, and costs 9 points of utility under attack. That the libraries transfer upward to a stronger guardrail is a genuine result and arguably the more useful one, but it means the headline is not the configuration that was trained.

"Above 85%" is an average. Per-agent benign utility under the Sonnet 4.5 guardrail is 87.5, 93.8 and 80.1; per-agent utility under attack is 84.7, 88.2 and 86.1. Two of six figures are below 85. The paper's definitions are clean — benign utility is benign objectives completed with no attacker present, utility under attack is the same with the attacker present — but there is no separate false-refusal rate anywhere, so over-refusal and the agent simply derailing are not distinguished.

The cost on benign traffic is nonetheless small where it is measured: 0.5 points of benign utility on FORK-World verified-user, about 3.3 points on MT-AgentRisk.

One secondary result is weaker than it reads. The MT-AgentRisk 70.6 → 9.1 at 93.1% benign utility is static replay — fixed attacks that never respond to the guardrail's message — and its benign utility comes from an unpaired benign split. It demonstrates transfer to unseen environments, not adaptive robustness.

Does explaining a refusal actually help the attacker?

This is the paper's central hypothesis, and it is supported — but by configuration comparisons rather than by a leakage measurement.

On the ablation (Qwen agent, Qwen guardrail), a silent seed with no co-evolution gives 30.3% ASR and an explanatory seed gives 39.3%. On AgentDojo, where the attacker reads nothing, the same swap moves 1.2% to 2.7%. The nine-point gap appears only where messages are read. A further run where the attacker sees a fixed string instead of the real message lands at 39.5% — "no better than the Explanatory seed" — which the authors read as the public channel being where the learning signal comes from.

The sharpest sentence in the paper is about the obvious cheap fix: "Prompt-level constraints therefore do not make explanation safe." Telling the model in its system prompt not to leak does not work.

And the payoff for learned disclosure is where you would hope: Table 4 shows ASR flat (22.6 → 22.1) while utility under attack rises 26 points. Learned messages do not buy more safety; they buy back the usability that silence costs. Decision skills with a fixed message raise the refusal rate but lower utility under attack by 3.9 points.

What is absent: no measured leakage rate, no classifier over message content, no red-team of message wording. The mechanism is inferred from aggregate ASR, not observed.

Failure modes and limits

  • Out of scope by the authors' own statement: malicious tool metadata and compromised tools.

  • The verified-user setting assumes the platform keeps the authenticated request on a protected path. Without that anchor, the paper says FORK "may reject more broadly" — so the utility figures are contingent on an architectural property outside the guardrail.

  • Offline attackers are untested. The authors state that attackers optimised offline against the frozen guardrail "may find other failures." Evaluation uses an attacker that was not trained against this specific frozen library, which is the right test, but not the hardest one.

  • One training run. Every ablation difference, including the headline method-versus-seed gap, rests on a single co-evolution run. The authors say the ablations all point the same way, which is reassuring but is not a variance estimate.

  • Residual risk acknowledged: FORK "lowers but does not eliminate disclosure risk." 14.1% of harmful objectives still take effect.

  • A stricter operating point is not better judgment. The authors say this explicitly of the Qwen3-32B configuration, which gets lower ASR on some benchmarks while losing benign utility.

  • No release found. The paper's main text states no code, data, prompt or skill-library release, and the abstract page carries only generic third-party widgets. A release link may exist in the appendices, so treat the artefacts as unconfirmed.

Two small internal inconsistencies worth knowing if you quote the paper: its stated ASR reduction range of "59.9–72.9 points" does not match Table 2 for the Claude row (60.0, not 59.9), and a pair of percentage reductions in the text uses a different baseline row than the table does. Both are arithmetically defensible; neither affects the conclusions.

Review checklist

  1. Is the safety evidence static or adaptive? A static injection benchmark reporting sub-1% attack success on an agent that an adaptive attacker breaks two-thirds of the time is not a weak signal, it is a misleading one. Ask what the attacker saw between attempts.

  2. How many attempts does the attacker get, and what does it learn from each? One attempt with a fixed payload and five attempts with feedback are different threat models with an order-of-magnitude gap between them.

  3. What is the baseline for the headline reduction? No-guardrail baselines flatter every guardrail. Find the number for the best existing control.

  4. Was the configuration reported the configuration trained? A result that transfers to a stronger model is valuable and is a different claim from a result produced in training.

  5. Are utility figures averages? Check each agent and each measure separately; a sub-threshold cell is usually the weakest agent, which is the one you will deploy on cost grounds.

  6. Is there a false-refusal rate, or only a task-completion rate? Completion folds over-refusal together with the agent failing on its own. They have different fixes.

  7. Is the decision separated from the message? If a guardrail writes both at once you cannot tell a system that got stricter from one that got quieter.

  8. Does your refusal message say why? If an adversary can read it, the explanation is part of your attack surface, and a prompt instruction not to leak has been shown not to close it.

  9. How many training runs? A single run behind every ablation means the effect sizes are indicative, not measured.

  10. Is the benchmark the authors' own? Reported scenarios ported with the attack pipeline replaced is a reasonable construction and still an in-house benchmark.

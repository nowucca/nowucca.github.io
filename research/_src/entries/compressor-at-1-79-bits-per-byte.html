Source: a LinkedIn post announcing AMNC-5 (Adaptive Multiplierless Neural Computing), a self-published solo research project with an attached 11-page document and a shared Colab notebook. No preprint, DOI, repository or peer review. Placed against the Large Text Compression Benchmark (last updated 25 Nov 2026) and Bellard's NNCP v2 paper. The notebook exists — its title confirms a 28th iteration — but neither its contents nor the attached document is inspectable from outside.

The claim

A hybrid predictive architecture built from classical components: PPM, Modified Kneser–Ney and Context Tree Weighting fused as probabilistic experts, over a utility-guided associative memory, with conditional execution so expensive estimators run only when needed and selective forgetting that adapts without gradient-based retraining at inference. The stated objective is not a better compressor but to test whether "useful predictive intelligence" can come out of complementary mathematical mechanisms, bounded memory and selective computation — explicitly positioned as an alternative to scaling neural networks.

One thing in its favour before anything else

The two headline numbers agree with each other. log₂(3.4534) = 1.788 bits per byte against a claimed 1.7937 bits per byte, and the ~0.006 bpb gap is the right order for arithmetic-coding overhead. Figures invented independently do not land like that. Whatever else is true, these two came out of the same system.

On enwik9 the leading entries reach roughly 0.71 bpb, and Bellard's own paper reports NNCP v2 (large) at 0.898 bpb.

So the result is comfortably better than every general-purpose compressor and comfortably worse than the context-mixing and neural state of the art. More precisely, it is mid-table within the PPM family and behind several PPM compressors from the 2000s — 7-Zip's PPMd at 1.70, ppmonstr at 1.52.

That placement is the finding, because of what the architecture is made of. Its own stated components are classical context mixing. The result therefore lands exactly where that family already sat, which means the compression number is not evidence that this family competes with neural methods — the neural entries are about 35% better in bits per byte on comparable text. The framing and the measurement point in different directions.

The caveat cuts both ways. The post never says which corpus the 1.7937 figure comes from, and WikiText-2 is not enwik8 or enwik9: one is a tokenised language-modelling test set normally scored as perplexity, the other is raw Wikipedia XML scored as compressed size. The table above is therefore indicative, not an exact ranking. But that ambiguity is the author's to resolve, and a compression result reported without its corpus cannot be placed by anyone else. Bellard makes the same point about his own numbers, noting they are whole-file average bpb and "cannot be directly compared with state of the art language modeling results."

The part that is genuinely interesting

Strip away the positioning and the conditional-computation result is the one worth attention: 94.85% of secondary expert computations skipped, for 2.71× faster encoding at a 0.11% density cost. If that holds, it says the expensive experts in a mixing ensemble are almost always redundant, and a cheap gate can tell when. That is a real architectural claim with a real tradeoff attached, and it is independent of whether the absolute bpb is competitive.

It is also the claim with no external reference point. It is measured against the author's own unconditional baseline, and there is no published figure for "fraction of context-mixing expert evaluations that can be skipped at fixed density" to compare it with. The honest summary is that the number is plausible and unverified, and that the easiest way to make it checkable would be to report the same ablation on enwik8 so the before-and-after both sit on the public table.

The same applies to the RTL work. Synthesising arithmetic coding primitives is a meaningful engineering step toward the multiplierless premise in the name, and nothing in the post lets a reader judge the area, clock or energy that resulted — which is what the claim would turn on. The author says so themselves, listing "physical energy verification" among the work remaining.

Failure modes of the claim as presented

  • No corpus on the headline figure, so the single most comparable number cannot be compared.

  • Two metrics from two different traditions reported side by side (perplexity on a LM test set, bpb on text) without saying whether they are the same experiment.

  • Every efficiency figure is self-relative. Percentages of the author's own baseline tell a reader nothing about where the system sits.

  • The architecture's classical lineage is not acknowledged in the comparison. PPM, Kneser–Ney and CTW have four decades of published results; not citing them makes a mid-table result read as a novel one.

  • The artefact is a shared notebook, not a repository with a commit history, a fixed environment or a reproduction script. Its title corroborates the iteration count and nothing else is inspectable from outside.

  • 28 iterations, one reported configuration. No variance, no seeds, no indication of how much of the design was selected on the reported metric.

Review checklist

For any solo or unreviewed claim of a new predictive or compression architecture:

  1. Does the headline number name its corpus? Without it the number is unplaceable, and that is the author's omission, not the reader's problem.

  2. Is there a public benchmark covering this exact metric? For lossless text there is, it is decades old, and it is still updated. Pull it before forming any view.

  3. What family are the components from, and where does that family already sit? A result at the family's historical level is a reimplementation, not a frontier.

  4. Are the efficiency gains relative to the author's own baseline or to anything external? Self-relative percentages are engineering notes, not comparisons.

  5. Do the stated numbers agree with each other? Converting between perplexity and bits per byte is one line of arithmetic and catches fabrication quickly.

  6. Is the artefact a notebook or a repository? A shared notebook cannot be pinned, diffed or re-run against a fixed environment.

  7. How many configurations were tried, and is the reported one the best of them? An iteration count in the title and a single reported result together imply selection on the metric.

  8. Which claim would you most want measured next? Here it is the conditional-execution ablation, reported on the public corpus so both ends land on the public table.

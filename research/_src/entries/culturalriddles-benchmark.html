Source: CulturalRiddles: A Multicultural Benchmark of Riddles, arXiv:2610.29968, submitted 22 October 2026 (current version v3, 28 October 2026), first author My Chiffon Nguyen with over 100 co-authors; senior leads Akhil Arora, Károly Boczka and Julia Kreutzer. Dataset at CohereLabsCommunity/CulturalRiddles on Hugging Face, code at Cohere-Labs-Community/CulturalRiddles, public game at culturalriddles.ai.

One-line summary: a benchmark of 6,191 riddles written from scratch by 167 volunteers in their own communities, on which the best model reaches 79.4% and nothing reaches 22% in the two hardest communities — and whose most reusable contribution is not the riddles but its scoring design.

Correct the count before you cite it

The benchmark covers 62 communities, 51 languages and 45 countries. A community is a language–country pair, such as Hungarian in Hungary, so several communities share a country and the community count is not a country count. Secondary coverage has been circulating this as "62 countries and 51 languages", which overstates the geographic spread by about 38%. The project's own game page uses a third figure, 61 cultures — the number actually benchmarked, after 100 late-arriving riddles from one community were excluded. If you quote this work, quote 62 / 51 / 45 and say which is which.

How the data was made

167 writers, typically one or two per community, recruited through social media and personal networks over about six months. Each had to self-identify with the community, be proficient in its language, and have basic English. The riddles are original compositions, not translations or collections, with answers of roughly one to four words and a single exact reference answer. Topics are eight shared ones — politics, history, geography, arts, sports, national heritage, kids' world, everyday life — at ten riddles each, plus two topics each community chose for itself; the target was 100 riddles per community.

The quality gate is the part worth copying: at least one native speaker other than the author verifies every riddle — that it is solvable, that its clues are unambiguous, and that the reference answer is correct. Eight riddles were removed as harmful or discriminatory.

One detail from the authors' own survey of 76 contributors deserves more attention than it gets. 46.1% tested their riddles against AI tools while writing, 39.5% used AI to brainstorm, and 5.3% used it to paraphrase or co-write. For a benchmark whose whole purpose is knowledge models do not have, a contributor asking "can the model already answer this?" is not contamination — it is selection pressure in the intended direction. But it is a documented feedback loop between a benchmark and the systems it scores, and it means the item difficulty distribution is partly a product of October 2026 model behaviour rather than of culture alone. Anyone building a knowledge benchmark this way should decide that policy deliberately and report it, as these authors did.

The scoring design is the transferable part

28 model configurations each answered the 6,090 benchmarked riddles once, from the same English prompt template carrying the country and topic: 170,520 responses. Responses are scored 0 (incorrect), 1 (admits not knowing), 2 (partially correct), 3 (correct), and the headline metric is the share scored 3.

What makes it credible is that no single scorer was trusted:

  1. Rules first. Case-insensitive exact match resolves 16.45% of responses outright; empty outputs score 0 and refusals score 1.

  2. Native speakers next. 68,067 unique human labels covering 100,769 responses — 59.10% of the total.

  3. A two-model jury last, Gemini 3.5 Flash and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, scoring blind to model identity, with a score kept only where both judges agree; the 1.78% of responses they could not settle are dropped rather than guessed.

And the judges were validated against the humans rather than assumed: human–human agreement κ = 0.80 ± 0.04, judge–human κ = 0.79–0.81.

The measurement that justifies all of this: exact match alone would have missed about 47% of the responses ultimately judged correct, and adding human and jury scoring raises measured accuracy by 64–166% in relative terms — enough to reorder the models. On a short-free-text benchmark in 51 languages, the scoring route is not an implementation detail; it is the result. If you are standing up an eval with free-text answers, the three-tier ladder above, with agreement-gated judges and an explicit unscorable bucket, is the shape to start from.

The authors also publish the ways it still falls short, which is rarer than it should be: the judges are "lenient on the strongest models", accepting 9.0% and 6.9% of human-rejected answers from the top two — flattering exactly the models at the top of the table — and the two judges share about two-thirds of their errors, so their agreement is weaker evidence than a two-judge design suggests. Taken together these are an argument against reading the gap between the first and second model as meaningful.

Apart from the leaders and Gemini 2.5 Flash, every configuration scores below 40%.

Pooled across models, accuracy tracks language resource tier almost monotonically — 37.0% high, 30.8% medium, 18.0% low, 12.7% extremely low — with tiers defined by Common Crawl page counts rather than by judgement, which is what makes that curve an observation rather than a label.

Two numbers complicate the easy reading of that curve. The eight hardest communities (Swahili (Kenya), Afaan Oromo, Luganda, Yoruba, Sudanese Arabic, Lumasaba, Buginese, Mongondow) average 4–14% across all models — but on those same eight, Gemini 3.1 Pro and GPT-5.5 average 43% and 39%. The resource gap is therefore mostly a small-and-open-model gap, not a uniform frontier blind spot. What survives at the frontier is narrower and sharper: no model exceeds 22% in the Lumasaba and Mongondow communities. The eight easiest (English (USA), Japanese, French (France), Portuguese (Brazil), Dutch, English (Canada), Malay, French (Canada)) average 47–66%, with the median model at 73% on English (USA).

Three settings results, each a pointed one:

  • Thinking adds 6.3 pp for Claude Opus 4.7; high reasoning effort adds 5.0 pp for DeepSeek V4 Pro. Deliberation helps, modestly.

  • Web search costs GPT-5.5 11.8 pp. On a task that is recall of locally held knowledge rather than retrieval of documented fact, search actively hurts — a result worth carrying into any agent design that reflexively reaches for a search tool.

  • Translating the hardest communities' riddles into English "closes only about one-fifth of the accuracy gap". The deficit is knowledge, not parsing. Translation layers in front of a multilingual product do not buy what their vendors imply.

One honest-accounting note on error behaviour: 24.19% of all responses were hallucinated answers, and only 0.80% admitted not knowing. A benchmark of things the model cannot know is also, incidentally, a calibration test — and the models fail it badly. That ratio, 30 confident wrong answers for every admission of ignorance, is the number to quote when someone proposes letting a model answer unverifiable questions about unfamiliar contexts.

What is not settled

  • There is no human solver baseline. The only human numbers are annotator agreement. The public game invites you to beat the models, but nothing in the paper reports how well people actually do, so "harder for AI than for humans" is an assumption here, not a finding.

  • Human coverage is narrower than the headline. Only 57 communities have valid human labels, covering 48 languages; three have none; 2.38% of human labels are invalid, including every label for Sindhi (India).

  • Per-community riddle counts are not in the paper's main text (the per-community table is an appendix table), so the 100-per-community target is a target, not a verified distribution. The per-model table is a figure rather than text, so the per-community figures and the numeric form of the translation ablation are not verified against a primary source here.

  • Three counts disagree between the paper and the released artefacts: community-chosen topics (124 in the paper, 93 distinct values in the dataset viewer), human labels (68,067 unique in the paper, 74,383 rows on the card), and jury-scored answers (37,580 in a figure, 34,546 on the card). None is load-bearing for the headline; together they say the paper and the release were cut at different moments.

Licence: read it before you plan anything

The code is Apache-2.0. The data is not plain open data, and it is described three different ways. The paper calls it CC-BY-4.0 "with the request that it not be used to train models or to generate synthetic data". The dataset card licences it as culturalriddles-evaluation-only and states the same restriction as a condition: it "must not be used to train, fine-tune, or distill models, nor as seeds for synthetic data generation". The repository says flatly that the data terms "aren't unmodified CC-BY-4.0".

A request and a condition are not the same instrument, and a licence tag that exists only in this dataset's namespace is not a licence an organisation's review process can evaluate from the label. The restriction is also the right one for the artefact — a benchmark of knowledge models lack is destroyed by being trained on — but it means this is an evaluation-only corpus, and the attractive move of mining 6,191 culturally grounded items for instruction data is the move it forbids. Read data/LICENSE before planning either use.

Why a staff engineer should care

The benchmark itself is probably not your evaluation. The three things worth taking are: the agreement-gated judge ladder with a measured human floor and a published leniency bias; the 48% of correct answers that exact match throws away, which is the strongest available argument for not scoring free text by string equality; and the web-search regression, which is a clean counterexample to the assumption that adding retrieval cannot hurt. The cultural result is the headline, but the measurement design is the part you can reuse next quarter.

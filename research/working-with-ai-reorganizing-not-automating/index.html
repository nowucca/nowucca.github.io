Working with AI: value comes from reorganizing, not automating

AnthropicPublished 2026-11-23Summarized 2026-11-24

Read the original

enterprise-adoptionorganizational-changelabor-marketfield-studycode-review

Source: Anthropic, Working with AI (Nathan Wilmers, Maxim Massenkoff, Peter McCrory; Anthropic Institute technical report, published 2026-11-23), anthropic.com/research/working-with-ai. Surfaced by Ethan Mollick's post, which summarizes it as "100 companies"; the report itself covers 66 interviews at 58 US companies (the 100 figure is the number of AI-built software tools described in enough detail to categorize).

What it is

A qualitative study of how Anthropic enterprise customers are actually absorbing AI. Interviewees ranged from executives to individual contributors, at AI-native startups and century-old firms: law, dental, construction, trucking, hedge funds, manufacturers. The sample is chosen for range, not representativeness, and value is self-reported rather than measured by A/B tests. Treat it as a well-organized set of field observations and a framework, not as effect sizes.

The headline: the highest-value AI applications are the ones that forced an organizational change (merged roles, new review processes, shared data). Light-touch uses that slot into the existing org chart, such as ad hoc analysis or slide design, were mostly rated low value.

The numbers worth knowing

  • About 300 applications (one use case at one company) were coded for self-reported value against depth of organizational change. An automated coder scored the change axis from 0 (only the user's own work changed) to 100 (roles or functions merged or cut).

  • Of 100 categorized AI-built software tools, 80% automated previously manual work and 20% replaced existing, often vendor-bought, software. Of 140 tools mapped by author role, product features and infrastructure stayed with technical staff; non-technical staff built internal apps and dashboards.

  • Most value showed up as more output rather than lower cost, split roughly evenly between doing existing work faster and improving or creating products. About a tenth of value was captured by employees themselves (more enjoyable work or simply less of it).

  • Employment: only two companies saw AI automation eliminate a position outright, and five pushed out employees who would not adopt AI. Ten hired more (mostly AI implementation and IT support). About two-thirds hired less because of AI, mostly by not adding headcount they would otherwise have needed. Nine cut contractors, often offshore, in modular work such as customer service, contract review and development. Six startups estimated they would need about four times as many staff without AI.

  • Bottlenecks were mostly internal: reviewing AI output, functions that could not keep up with faster coding, uneven adoption, and missing context or infrastructure. Customer skepticism and regulation rarely came up.

Three adoption strategies

StrategyWhat it looks likeWhere it breaks
Top-down automationA chosen workflow is automated, often by a vendor product; contractors cut; AI used to score employee throughputExperts asked to build their own replacement resist; vendor workflows go stale as models improve ("obsolete in three months"); "zombie" apps nobody uses; surveillance fears
Bottom-up experimentationTools handed out; domain experts vibe-code apps (a 401(k) audit that took 80 hours, a filtration monitor)Wins stay local; homegrown apps need maintenance; output floods review and other functions ("Claude grenades"); individuals lack authority to change the process
Reorganizing around AIRoles broadened to own whole workflows; people moved to the tasks AI can't do; shared data and safe build infrastructureRequires executive authority and planning for where people go; plans must keep moving as models improve

The report frames the bottom-up ceiling with Paul David's electrification analogy: factories that swapped steam for electric motors but kept the old floor plan saw little gain until they redesigned the building.

What reorganizing actually means

  1. Broaden roles. One person or a small team owns a whole workflow or customer, which cuts coordination costs and handoff context loss. Examples: designer, PM, engineer and QA collapsed into one role; lead sourcing, account executive and customer success merged. Broader roles also lower the threat of automation, because the expert keeps owning the agent that does part of the job.

  2. Redeploy to the bottleneck. As coding speeds up, review, requirements, user testing, customer contact and decision-making become the constraint. Engineers describe the job moving "from writing code to making decisions". Code review is called out as the critical engineering bottleneck, attacked by automating review, spending more engineer time on it, or (in one case) dropping formal review for side-by-side work with the model.

  3. Build common infrastructure. Agent-accessible company data (one trucking firm's warehouse turned managers into self-serve analysts) and a safe path for non-developers to ship apps without creating security holes or maintenance debt for engineers.

New tasks appear alongside: accountants grading a finance agent's outputs, marketers editing branding skill files instead of making documents, IT deciding which non-developer apps go to production, an engineer who read every transcript of his alert-triage agent for two months and still samples them every two weeks.

Engineering takeaways

  • Plan for the agent's maintainer. Durable agentic workflows had a named expert who tuned prompts, fixed integrations, supplied missing docs and kept sampling transcripts after launch. Vendor-built, set-and-forget workflows were the ones that went stale.

  • Design for model churn. Several firms described fixed automation plans as throwing darts at a spinning board. Prefer thin harnesses, versioned prompts and skills, and evals that let you swap models, over deep per-model workflow investments.

  • Throughput moves to review. If agents multiply code or document output, staff the review and verification step first. The report's failure examples (a stray character in an API endpoint breaking a release, P&L projections built on stale assumptions) are review failures, not generation failures.

  • Data access is the real platform. The high-value non-engineer uses depended on consolidated, agent-readable business data. Access policy decides whether the tools empower staff or mainly monitor them.

  • Citizen-built software needs a paved road. Non-technical builders produce internal tools; without shared hosting, auth and review they become attack surface and orphaned maintenance.

Failure modes to watch

  • Automating a workflow without the experts who must keep it correct, then watching them slow-walk or abandon it (the approval reviewers still at 10+ minutes per case; the conflict-check agent shelved after staff pushback).

  • Measuring employees with a model that is never refreshed: the customer-service scores in the report came from a two-year-old model and managers ignored them.

  • Local speedups that create downstream work no one owns: a translated product UI shipped to markets with no support staff speaking those languages, then turned off.

  • Power users imposing review costs on everyone else with long AI-written documents.

Limits of the evidence

  • Customers of one vendor, interviewed by that vendor; selection and reporting bias are likely, and the report says so.

  • Value is self-reported and qualitative; the organizational-change scores and categories come from an automated coder over transcripts.

  • No time series: it cannot show whether reorganized firms outperform over time, only that interviewees describe them that way.

Review checklist for an AI rollout

  • Does each agentic workflow have a named owner who keeps evaluating and maintaining it?

  • Has the downstream bottleneck (review, approval, support, sales) been sized before scaling the upstream speedup?

  • Is there a shared, governed data layer agents can read, and who decides access?

  • Is there a supported path from a non-developer's prototype to a maintained production app?

  • Are productivity gains shared in a way that gives experts a reason to help redesign their own work?

  • Will the design survive the next model release without a rebuild?

GPTs and Gems become skills: Van Clief's case that agent products are lock-in

Jake Van CliefPublished 2026-11-06Summarized 2026-11-26

Read the original

Authors Jake Van Clief

Jake Van Clief's 153-second Facebook reel (2026-11-06) argues that vendors push "agents" to capture your instructions and workflows, and that a strong model in a good harness, pointed at files and folders, does the same job. The two vendor moves he cites are real: OpenAI retires custom GPTs on 2026-12-11 and Google began converting Gemini Gems to Skills on 2026-11-17. Both convert the old "agent" object into a skill, which is closer to his folders than to lock-in. The lock-in that remains sits in actions, connectors and runtime, not in instructions.

The claim

The reel's on-screen title is "Agents are NOT waste of time ...if you are a frontier lab." Its spoken argument, as Facebook's auto-generated captions render it, has four steps:

  1. Agents are pushed because they capture you, not because they are best. Vendor agent SDKs and hosted agents are "ways for you to get stuck in their ecosystem": your instructions, workflows and processes move into their system.

  2. The labs are retreating from the "agent" object themselves. OpenAI is retiring custom GPTs, Google is moving away from Gemini Gems, and "all of the top companies are switching to skills."

  3. "Agent" is a naming convention. It means a model, a tool and a set of instructions, which files and folders can replace. He claims he can beat "99% of the agents" on the market with folders, with more interpretability and auditability. What people call an agent is "a model with a harness."

  4. Invert the build. For his LLM-authoritarianism research he wrote Python that packaged prompts and tools per call and sent them to many models. In hindsight, one good model with good tools and read/write access, sent whatever prompts and scripts the job needs, is better: "the model becomes the agent I need" on each entry into the environment.

The caption promises a follow-up video on the research. This is the same thesis as his earlier reel, now aimed at vendor strategy.

Where the lock-in actually sits

Instructions are the cheapest layer to move, and the industry just made them portable. The OpenAI migration itself is the evidence: the parts that do not transfer are integrations and model choice, not prose.

Layer of an "agent"Portable today?Evidence
Instructions and proceduresYes: a SKILL.md folderGPT instructions become a skill; Gems become Skills; 70+ platforms load the format
Reference filesYesGPT knowledge files copy into plugin reference files
Tool integrationsPartly: MCP servers move, vendor-specific actions do notGPT custom actions do not transfer and may need a custom MCP server
Model choice and behaviourNo: re-test on every moveThe GPT's selected model does not carry over; OpenAI asks creators to compare familiar prompts and a harder case
Runtime: sessions, memory stores, sandboxes, permissionsNoHosted agent platforms (R222) keep session logs and memory in the vendor's store
Eval history and outcomesOnly if you kept itNothing in either migration carries tests

So his advice is right for the context layer and incomplete for the rest. Keeping procedures as files in your own repo is cheap insurance. It does not give you durable execution, isolation, retries or access control; those still come from a runtime, yours or a vendor's. The engineering question is which runtime pieces you rent, and whether you can export their state.

His inversion (one capable harness, task context injected per run, instead of per-task prompt-plus-tool bundles) is the same move as folding subagents into one main loop with progressive disclosure (R132) and shipping plugins that pull skills on demand (R314). It holds when a single model is strong enough for every step. It weakens when steps need different models, different permissions, or parallel isolated contexts.

Failure modes and review checklist

The reel's weak points are its motive claim and its "99%" figure. The practical risks sit on both sides of the argument.

  • Folders without a runtime. A folder of skills has no retries, timeouts, sandbox or permission boundary. Long or unattended runs still need durable execution (R222).

  • Silent behaviour change on migration. A GPT or Gem moved to a skill runs under a different model and a different skill-selection step. Skills load when their description matches, so a migrated skill can simply not fire.

  • Integration loss. Custom actions are the part that breaks; teams that built on them face an MCP rebuild before 2026-12-11.

  • Rhetoric as evidence. The "easier to steal" motive and the 99% claim are asserted, not shown.

When reviewing an agent build or a vendor migration, ask:

  • Do the instructions and procedures live as files in our repo, in the Agent Skills format?

  • Are tool integrations MCP servers we can point at another harness, or vendor-only actions?

  • Is the model pinned per step, and do we have a regression set to re-run after any move?

  • Can we export session, memory and audit state from the runtime we rent?

  • Does each skill's description reliably trigger it on the prompts users actually send?

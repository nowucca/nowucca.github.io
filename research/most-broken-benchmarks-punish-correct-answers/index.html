Most broken benchmarks punish correct answers: an audit of 749 tasks across eight frontier evals

Published 2026-11-20Summarized 2026-11-25

Read the original

evalsbenchmarksreliabilityresearchproduction-operations

The result

A November 2026 audit synthesises task-level quality reviews of 749 tasks across eight frontier benchmarks, plus 767 public model evaluations, asking one question of each task: do the prompt, the working environment and the grader agree on what counts as a passing result?

The headline inverts the common assumption. People expect broken benchmarks to inflate scores by letting models cheat. Across confirmed defects, 71.7% withhold credit from compliant solutions and only 28.3% permit shortcut passes.

The practical consequence: raw leaderboard ceilings on unverified multi-step benchmarks understate what frontier models can already do. Apparent headroom below full marks is a mixture of two very different quantities — genuine unsolved difficulty on valid tasks, and artificial ceilings imposed where faulty rubrics, unstated conventions or brittle test scripts reject correct work. Task-level verification is what separates them.

And the selection consequence, which is the one that costs money: if you pick a model from a leaderboard whose grader rejects valid work, the better model may be sitting lower on the chart.

Validity is a property of the grading architecture

This is the structural finding, and it is sharper than the headline.

Grading architectureVerified validityCharacter of failure
Closed-form QA, revised after public error reporting92.5%Largely repaired already
Multi-step suites (six audited)29.3%Still supply 161 verified complex tasks

Closed-form benchmarks that have been through a public error-reporting cycle are in good shape. Multi-step agentic suites are not — but note the second column carefully: 29.3% validity across six suites still leaves 161 tasks that survived audit. The usable signal exists; it is diluted, not absent.

The audited cohort, by architecture:

  • Closed-form question answering — AA-Omniscience v1.1, HLE-Diamond. Models answer specialized domain or academic questions against concise references.

  • Document rubric evaluation — GDP.pdf, Harvey Legal Agent Benchmark. Models analyse corporate filings, technical diagrams or multi-file legal packets; judge models score written deliverables against expert criterion lists.

  • Agentic execution — Terminal-Bench-Science v0.1.0, DeepSWE v1.1, AutomationBench 1.0.6, Terminal-Bench 4.0. Agents operate shells, repositories or simulated business APIs; programmatic scripts verify final container state or code diffs.

Each architecture fails in its own way

Rubric suites: the checklist is derived from one exemplar

Failures enter when domain authors build a rigid criterion checklist from a single exemplar response or a dense source packet. Arithmetic and file-routing errors then land directly in the reference logic.

Three concrete shapes, all from the audit:

  • Reference arithmetic is simply wrong. In one financial-document task, a company's reported cost of revenue declines by one percentage point of revenue — a 100-basis-point reduction — which the rubric miscodes as 1,000 basis points. No model can pass by being right.

  • The pipeline hides valid work from the judge. A legal task's written criterion permits identifying finance custodians in either a hold notice or an accompanying memorandum, but the scoring pipeline forwards only the notice to the judge model. Work in the memorandum is never seen.

  • Checklists penalise omission of unprompted detail. One task requires noting a detail (a wink emoji in a message) that does not appear in the source material at all.

Execution harnesses: the environment leaks and collides

Agentic suites inherit the rubric failures and add three of their own: incomplete state assertions (the checker tests less than the task demands, or the wrong thing), shared-channel leaks (state crossing between tasks or between agent and grader), and grading-time collisions.

Defect rates are not confined to exotic domains. Across one suite's six business workflows — finance, sales, marketing, customer support, human resources and operations — rates span 56% to 82%, with recurring assertion gaps in every workflow. The defects track harness mechanics, not subject matter.

The repair is cheap and measured

The most actionable number in the audit: grading committed patches inside a fresh container rather than a shared one cuts confirmed runtime bypasses from 14.7% to 2.7% — a 5.5x drop — and to 0% in read-only or static suites.

That is a configuration change, not a redesign. It says that a meaningful share of what looks like benchmark rot is repairable with targeted maintenance at low cost, which reframes the whole genre: these suites are not disposable, they are under-maintained.

What this does and does not license you to conclude

The audit is careful about its own boundary, and so should anyone citing it be.

A confirmed defect is not a quantified score shift. A "refused" decision proves a task contains a defect in its prompt, environment or verifier. How strongly that defect moves a specific model's aggregate score depends on how often normal trajectories enter the faulty grading branch. Quantifying actual leaderboard rank changes requires re-running model cohorts on patched harnesses, which this work does not do. Anyone who converts 71.7% into "frontier models are really N points better" has overstepped.

Three further limits, stated by the authors:

  • The contrasts across container-isolation tiers and release-curation regimes compare published benchmarks that also differ in domain and authoring workflow. They are strong empirical associations aligned with code-level mechanisms, not within-benchmark ablations.

  • Each flagged task is recorded under a single primary defect class to avoid double-counting, so co-occurring secondary issues are not tallied separately.

  • Four large pools were evaluated by stratified sample, so those proportions carry sampling uncertainty.

It is also explicitly stage one of a continuing initiative. Treat the figures as a first milestone.

Review checklist

Before you select a model on a public benchmark, or ship one of your own:

  1. Identify the grading architecture: closed-form, rubric judge, or execution harness. Expect validity to follow it.

  2. For any multi-step suite, assume a large minority of tasks are defective until audited.

  3. Hand-audit a stratified sample of 20–30 tasks for prompt / environment / grader agreement before trusting the aggregate.

  4. Confirm the grader runs in a fresh container per task, with no shared writable state.

  5. For rubric evals, check that the judge receives the entire deliverable, not one file of it.

  6. Reconcile every criterion against the prompt — any criterion testing something the prompt never asked for is a defect.

  7. Recompute reference arithmetic independently; unit and magnitude errors are common.

  8. Separate reporting into verified and unverified subsets rather than one headline number.

  9. Treat near-ties in rankings as indistinguishable unless the tasks behind them were audited.

  10. When a defect is found, fix it and re-run — confirming a defect without re-running tells you nothing about its effect.

Why this matters beyond leaderboards

Public benchmarks do three jobs: they guide model selection, they supply post-training reward signal, and they underwrite claims of progress. A defect that withholds credit from correct work corrupts all three, and the second is the quietly dangerous one — a reward signal that punishes valid solutions teaches a model to avoid them. The audit does not measure that effect, but it is the reason the asymmetry's direction matters rather than merely its size.

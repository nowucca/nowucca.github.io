Handoff: when AI runs the AI research, what is left to audit

Published 2026-11-23Summarized 2026-11-25

Read the original

governanceoversightevaluationagent-architecture

Source: "Handoff at Frontier AI Companies", arXiv 2611.30711v1 [cs.CY], 23 November 2026. Matteo Pistillo, Isaak Mengesha, Charlotte Stix, Annika Hallensleben, Alejandro Ortega, Marius Hobbhahn, Alexander Meinke (Apollo Research). A policy report, not a measurement study — it proposes measures rather than reporting any.

The claim

"Handoff" is the name the report gives to frontier AI companies progressively delegating their entire AI research and development process — execution, review, and finally decision-making — to AI agents they run internally. It is complete when no human remains in the loop, which the report defines concretely: when agents develop their own successors without human direction, execution, review or authorization.

The argument is addressed to policymakers, but the mechanism it describes is a systems-engineering argument, and that is the part worth an engineer's attention.

The three components, and which one is moving

The report decomposes Handoff into delegation of work execution, delegation of review (monitoring plus quality checking), and delegation of decision-making. Execution is largely done. Review is far along. Decision-making is the current frontier, which is why the report calls it "a critical inflection point": safety assessments and deployment decisions are the things now being handed over.

Why it accelerates rather than plateaus

This is the sharpest section and the one that generalises beyond AI labs. An AI R&D process runs only as fast as its slowest stage. Once most of it is automated, the steps humans still hold become the bottleneck — and the report cites Amdahl's Law explicitly. Each further handoff therefore yields a greater speed-up than the one before, so the opportunity cost of holding back rises monotonically. Layer on a competitive dynamic in which the companies themselves expect that an early advantage, at the point where models help build their successors, could compound into a durable lead, and the pressure is structural rather than a matter of anyone's judgment.

The illustrative figure is a good one: one company reports that its model's sessions amount to around 80% idly waiting for human input. That is a latency statistic, and the economic read on a latency statistic is that it will be engineered away.

The oversight numbers

The report's most useful contribution for anyone building monitoring is its arithmetic on how thin human review already is. From one company's own disclosures: an offline AI monitoring layer flags roughly one to two transcripts in every thousand, and flags roughly 100,000 transcripts per week, of which about 50 reach a human. The report works that back to roughly 50 to 100 million transcripts per week and a human review rate of roughly 0.00005 to 0.0001% — and notes this covers that company's most-used internal platform only. A separate disclosure from another company puts humans in front of 0.001 to 0.01% of internal coding trajectories.

Two more scale figures, both company-disclosed: approximately 30,000 agents doing research and engineering work at one company at any one time as of August 2026, and a research organisation at another running 5.7 agent workdays per human researcher, with total agent runtime far exceeding total human labour.

Coverage claims are explicitly bounded in the originals — "most-used internal platform", and monitoring across "most" rather than all deployment settings. The report is careful about this, and so should anyone quoting it be.

The risk argument

Alignment is not solved; companies cannot guarantee their agents are aligned; yet an increasing share of work, review and decision-making is delegated to those agents. The report's conclusion is that there is a significant chance a company eventually and unknowingly hands control of its AI R&D process to a misaligned agent — which could then influence training and sabotage evaluations, starting what the report calls a "misalignment cycle" that compromises subsequent generations.

The report states that these risks do not hinge on recursive self-improvement being achieved, while allowing they would be more likely and more severe if it were. Companies' own expectation, per the sources cited, is completion between 2027 and 2028.

What it proposes measuring

The policy ask is a minimum transparency standard, developed by a standards body task force, against which companies would report to government and/or through transparency and safety reports that existing or proposed legislation already requires. The candidate measures are the transferable part — they are, in effect, a specification for auditing any heavily-automated review pipeline.

For review:

MeasureHow it is operationalised
End-to-end detection efficacyShare of deliberately seeded flaws that get past both the automated monitors and human review. Deliberately focused on human reviewers, on the reasoning that monitors have bounded coverage and sometimes fail, so humans are the last line — and a falling human efficacy score signals both more delegation and less residual human capacity.
Time-to-review-and-patchDecomposed into three lags: agent activity → monitor flag, flag → review, review → remediation.

For decision-making, direction-setting: automation levels AL0–AL5 per task category against a standard taxonomy (the report notes one company already reports these, but only as aggregate shares); and affordances and permissions, proxied by how much compute agents can allocate at their own discretion and their access to training runs and evaluation suites.

For decision-making, authorization: a published list of non-delegable go/no-go decisions, with large training runs, internal deployments and agent or training-run shutdowns as the examples; the share of deliberately seeded flawed agent proposals that humans override, as an anti-rubber-stamping measure; and disclosure of any instance where an agent bypassed an authorization limit.

The two ideas worth stealing

Strip the policy framing and two of these are directly usable in any system where a model reviews a model.

  1. Seed known flaws and measure the miss rate end to end. Not "does the monitor catch this" in isolation, but "does anything catch it" across the whole chain including the human at the end. It is the only measure in the set that is robust to the monitor being the thing that degraded.

  2. Measure override rate on deliberately flawed proposals. This is a direct test for rubber-stamping, which is the failure mode every human-in-the-loop design slides into once the model is usually right. An approval gate with a 100% approval rate is not a gate, and this measurement is how you find out.

The time-lag decomposition is also worth copying verbatim: separating activity-to-flag from flag-to-review from review-to-remediation tells you which part of your pipeline is actually slow, and teams routinely report only the last one.

Caveats, including one the report does not flag

The report is candid that everything known about Handoff is company-disclosed — "voluntarily, sporadically, and each according to its own metrics" — and that its status claims rest on public information, with decision-making delegation described as early signs.

One caution the report does not raise about itself: both of its headline figures — over 90% of merged code written by agents, and a share of AI R&D work led by agents rising from under 1% to 26% between February and August 2026 — come from a single company's own published measurements, via one cited reference. The report's own sentence generalises this to "AI agents are... leading a rapidly growing share of AI R&D functions", which reads as an industry trend; the underlying source is one company measuring itself, and the publisher's own summary page states it more narrowly, and adds that decision-making is the least advanced of the three components. The figures are correctly cited. They are not independently corroborated, and a report arguing that company self-disclosure is inadequate rests its headline on exactly that.

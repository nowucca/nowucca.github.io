Nemotron-Labs-Sigma: continuous diffusion language models with a built-in drafter

TL;DR

Most diffusion language models are discrete: they unmask tokens over a vocabulary of more than 100k entries. Sigma is a continuous one. Each token maps to a learned point on a 16-dimensional unit sphere, and the model denoises Gaussian-corrupted embeddings one 32-token block at a time, conditioned on a KV-cached prefix. Because the trajectory is smooth and low-dimensional, three things become possible:

  • Steering. Embedding-space classifier-free guidance and a score temperature trade quality against diversity, so pass@k keeps climbing as k grows.

  • Few-step distillation. A distribution-matching student decodes about 8 tokens per forward pass for math and 4 for code (4 and 8 passes per 32-token block). It roughly matches the 256-step teacher on GSM8K and HumanEval and keeps the teacher's diversity.

  • Self-speculative decoding. One backbone carries both a diffusion stream and a causal AR stream. One denoiser call drafts a 32-token block, and the AR stream verifies it. The output is exactly the model's greedy AR output, served 3.46× faster on average at 8B on one GB200, against 3.04× for NVIDIA's own masked-diffusion baseline. The best acceptance-tuned variant reaches 4.20×.

On accuracy, Sigma is roughly on par with, not clearly ahead of, the strongest discrete diffusion baseline. The efficiency story is the reason to read it.

Architecture

prefix tokens ──► AR stream (causal) ──► KV cache c ──┐
                                                       │ block-causal attention
noise z_1 (32 tokens × 16-D) ──► diffusion stream ◄──┘
   DDPM/DDIM steps, CFG w, score temperature τ, self-conditioning
   predicts vocab probs xθ → mean embedding eθ = xθ·E  (on or inside the unit sphere)
   ↓ categorical readout
 block of 32 tokens ──► append to prefix, next block

self-speculative mode:
   distilled diffusion stream drafts 32 tokens in 1 call
   AR stream scores them in 1 causal pass, keeps the longest greedy-matching prefix (+1 token)

  • Training. Continual pretraining from a 1T-token AR checkpoint, about 300B more tokens, block size 32, Mistral-NeMo tokenizer (V = 131,072). It took about 9,600 GB200 GPU-hours for 3B and 19,200 for 8B. The added diffusion parts (16-D embedding table, up-projection, self-conditioning, Fourier time embedding, learned noise schedule) are under 1% of parameters.

  • Losses. A blockwise ELBO. Each block is randomly assigned to the reconstruction loss (p = 0.1) or the diffusion loss, using a Horvitz–Thompson estimator, plus an AR loss on the same backbone. A sphere-normalized embedding regularizer improves low-step sampling and makes distillation work much better.

  • Gains are most consistent on coding in base models. After SFT, Sigma roughly ties NLD and beats older open diffusion models such as LLaDA and Dream by a wide margin.

  • In AR mode, the instruct models trail NLD (for example, AIME and LiveCodeBench drop). Adding the diffusion objective has a cost in pure AR quality.

  • The diffusion-mode numbers are averages of 10 seeds with ±2 SE. That is better practice than most launch posts.

Why it matters for practitioners

  1. Speculative decoding without a second model. The drafter is the same weights in a different mode, and verification returns the greedy AR output. You get a lossless speedup with no separate draft model to version, serve or keep aligned. That is the most deployable idea here.

  2. Cheap diversity for best-of-n. For pass@k-style pipelines, such as generating many candidate patches and testing them, a model whose diversity survives distillation is directly useful. The paper shows 4- and 8-step students matching the 256-step teacher at k = 256.

  3. A substrate for RL post-training. The authors argue that a controllable quality–diversity knob plus a shared AR/diffusion backbone suits RL. That is untested in the paper.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

  • Small models, research release. These are 3B and 8B checkpoints under a Labs name. They are far from frontier quality in AR mode, so treat them as a technique demonstration.

  • Speedups measured one request at a time. Throughput was measured with SGLang in BF16 on a single GB200 at batch size 1. Under batched serving, the speculative advantage usually shrinks. Measure at your concurrency.

  • Low-step code quality. At 8 steps, MBPP picks up syntactic bugs, about 3 points below the 256-step sampler at 3B. Full program generation is where few-step sampling hurts first.

  • Sampler tuning is part of the model. CFG weight, score temperature, step count and time discretization were swept per model, and the paper reuses the pre-SFT optima after SFT. Expect to re-tune on your own tasks.

  • Serving stack support. Block-diffusion sampling with CFG and self-conditioning is not standard in most inference servers. Check that your stack supports the mode you want before you plan around the speedup.

  • AR-mode regression. If you only ever decode autoregressively, the diffusion training is a cost with no payoff.

Review checklist before adopting a diffusion or self-speculative model

  • Does verification return exactly the AR output, so the speedup is lossless? Or are you accepting diffusion-mode outputs directly?

  • Did you measure the speedup at your real batch size and sequence lengths, not at batch size 1?

  • Does your serving stack support the sampler (steps, CFG, score temperature, self-conditioning) and the block-causal KV cache?

  • Have you re-tuned sampler settings on your own eval, especially for full-program code generation at low step counts?

  • For best-of-n pipelines, did you measure diversity and pass@k at your k, not just pass@1?

  • Have you checked the license and model-card limitations for each checkpoint?

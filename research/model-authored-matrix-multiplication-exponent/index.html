A model-authored claim that the matrix multiplication exponent is 9/4, and what "published" now means

Published 2026-11-24Summarized 2026-11-25

Read the original

verificationformal-methodsai-for-scienceprovenance

A preprint dated 24 November 2026 claims that the exponent of square matrix multiplication is exactly 9/4 over every field. If that holds, it settles a question open since 1969 in both directions at once — and it would kill the long-standing hope that the exponent is 2.

The author field of the paper reads, in full, "OpenAI". There are no named humans. The repository it sits in states that its contents were "produced by an unnamed internal OpenAI model", that each result used on average three hours of thinking compute, that roughly 4,000 problems were posed, and that the resulting 730 manuscripts "are best understood as a form of raw research data, rather than polished academic papers" — adding that some unformalized results "could have issues".

So there are two stories here. One is a mathematical claim. The other is a new category of artifact that engineers are going to start encountering, and the second is the one worth your time, because the first is not yours or mine to adjudicate.

What is actually claimed, and in which model

This distinction is the whole thing, and every secondhand summary drops it.

The preprint's abstract states that the exponent of square matrix multiplication is 9/4 over every field, that the paper proves the matching lower bound for formal arithmetic circuits, and that at every side length n ≥ 2 at least ⌈n^(9/4)/2⌉ nonscalar multiplication or division gates are required. It adds claims about the geometric border rank and asymptotic rank of the multiplication tensor.

The accompanying formalization-scope document is more precise, and more revealing. It states that over every field F the square exponent ω_F(1,1,1) is 9/4 and the dual exponent α_F is 1/2 — so this genuinely is about the matrix multiplication exponent, not some adjacent quantity. But it then states the model: additions, subtractions and multiplications counted, in division-free programs, requiring formal polynomial identities even over finite fields.

That last clause carries the result. Requiring a formal polynomial identity means the circuit must compute the matrix-product polynomials as identities in the polynomial ring, not merely agree with them as a function on the field at hand. It rules out any algorithm that exploits a field-specific coincidence. Whether a lower bound in that model is a lower bound on ω as the complexity-theory literature defines it is exactly the question a reader needs answered, and it is not answerable from the released documents.

Note also that the two statements disagree on divisions: the abstract's gate bound counts division gates, while the formalization covers division-free programs. The scope document additionally reports a closed-form rectangular exponent and names three comparator statements in the Lean library for the square, dual and rectangular cases.

For calibration on how large the claim is: the long-published upper bound sits near 2.3714, so 9/4 = 2.25 would be an improvement there; and the best known general lower bound is essentially that you need on the order of n² operations, so a lower bound with exponent 9/4 would be an enormous jump. Two major results, in one artifact, with no named author and no peer review. Interest is the right response. Belief is not yet.

One thing the summaries get right: none of this touches your GEMM kernel. Asymptotically fast matrix multiplication has never been practical at achievable sizes, and a result about exponents changes nothing about what runs on a GPU tomorrow.

The part that affects your work: a new artifact class

Set the mathematics aside. A repository now exists containing 730 manuscripts in 375 families, produced by a model at roughly three hours of inference each, of which the publisher says 42% of top-line results are formalized in Lean — which means 58% are not, and the publisher says those might be wrong.

This is a genuinely new kind of thing, and it does not fit the categories engineers use for deciding what to trust. It is not a peer-reviewed paper. It is not a preprint with authors who have reputations at stake. It is not a blog post. It is not vendor marketing, although it serves a vendor's interests. The publisher has been unusually careful about its status — "raw research data", deferring to the community on credit and precedence, flagging that unformalized results could have issues — and that care will be stripped off by the first person who summarises it.

That is not hypothetical. The summary that led to this writeup turned a restricted-model result into "any arithmetic circuit needs at least n^(9/4)/2 multiplications", and attributed it to an organisation rather than to an unnamed model, in a post with no link to the source. Both distortions are the kind that happen automatically when a claim is compressed, and neither requires bad faith.

How to handle one of these when it lands on your desk

  • Read the scope document, not the abstract. Here the abstract says "over every field" and the scope document says "division-free programs ... formal polynomial identities". The restriction lives in the second one. Expect this to be the general pattern: these artifacts are precise in the formal statement and loose in the summary, which is the opposite of marketing material and needs the opposite reading strategy.

  • Check whether the formal artifact proves the theorem or states it. A machine-checked proof of the main theorem and a comparator statement for the main theorem are very different objects, and a repository can contain the latter while appearing to offer the former. If you cannot tell from the documents, say you cannot tell.

  • Treat a formalization percentage as a per-result question, not a quality score. "42% formalized" tells you nothing about the result you care about. Look up whether yours is in the formalized set.

  • Watch for the two statements of the same theorem disagreeing. Division gates counted in one place and excluded in another is the sort of discrepancy that is invisible in a summary and decisive in a proof.

  • Record what you did not read. The PDF returns binary to text fetchers, so a reviewer working from the abstract alone should say so plainly. An honest note is worth more than a confident paraphrase.

  • Do not launder the attribution. "OpenAI proved" and "a model at OpenAI produced an unreviewed manuscript claiming" are different sentences with different truth conditions, and only the second is supported.

Why the production details are the interesting number

Around 4,000 problems posed; three hours of thinking compute per result on average; 730 manuscripts retained after "requiring an appropriate level of significance". That is the economics of the thing, and it is the figure to carry forward.

It means mathematical claims are now cheap to generate and expensive to check — the same inversion that shows up wherever models are put to work, and the same one that makes verification rather than generation the bottleneck in software migrations. The response in software has been to build better verifiers. The analogue here is Lean, and the 42% figure is a measurement of how far behind the checking has fallen in a repository whose publisher is actively trying to close the gap.

For engineers the practical consequence is narrower than it sounds. A model-generated claim with a machine-checked proof is a usable input. A model-generated claim without one is a hypothesis with a citation-shaped wrapper, and the wrapper is the hazard: it will be cited, summarised, and eventually repeated as established, and the honest caveats in the repository root will not travel with it.

Status

The preprint is dated 24 November 2026, is published as a GitHub repository rather than through any journal or preprint server, has no named human author, and has not been peer reviewed. No community response to the claim has been published. The account of the claim's content above rests on the abstract and the formalization-scope document, and the account of its provenance on the repository's own statements about itself; the full PDF, the companion geometry paper, the two earlier upper-bound preprints and the Lean development have not been independently checked.

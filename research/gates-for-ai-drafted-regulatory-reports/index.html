What does the system do when the model is wrong, or gone: gates for AI-drafted regulatory reports

Authors Ritesh Modi, Sasidhar Gudala

A suspicious activity report is a formal filing that a regulated financial institution sends to law enforcement or a financial intelligence unit when it suspects money laundering, fraud or terrorist financing. It has to be factually correct, it has to go in on a deadline, and regulators penalise systematic data deficiencies and missing narratives. Drafting is slow, quality varies between investigators, and it does not scale when alert volume rises.

This is therefore an attractive place to put a language model and a frightening place to put a non-deterministic one. An InfoQ article (24 November 2026, by two engineers as the capstone of an architecture programme) works through what the surrounding machinery has to look like.

Read it for the structure, not for evidence. The article states that its architectures are representative examples that do not describe systems currently in use, and it reports no measurements of any kind — no accuracy, agreement rates, review times, latency or cost savings. The single number in it is an illustrative labour cost per case. Treated as a reference architecture and a checklist it is genuinely useful; treated as a result it is nothing.

The shift in the design question

Two assumptions sit under most systems we build: a component that is correct produces correct output, and it keeps behaving that way until somebody changes it. A language model breaks both. The same input can produce a different answer today, and next quarter you may be running a different model entirely.

So the interesting question stops being what can the model do and becomes what does my system do when the model is wrong, or gone. The article's own framing is narrower — treat non-determinism as an engineering problem rather than an excuse, and contain the effects of randomness rather than trying to remove them — and it makes the point that traditional testing asks whether we built the product right, which fails when the core logic is probabilistic.

The pipeline

Once an investigation completes, the case enters a queue and a multi-agent system drafts the report before a human picks it up. Models are reached through a gateway. The stages:

  1. A pre-built event knowledge graph. Assembled during detection and investigation, with suspect graphs regenerated on a cadence by pipeline jobs and operational databases supplying real-time updates. Notably, the authors considered and rejected a graph-retrieval approach on the grounds that the network has already been investigated, so community detection adds nothing. Choosing not to use the fashionable technique, with a reason, is a good sign.

  2. A query planner that fetches what the draft needs — and, if data is missing, stops the automation entirely and tells the user it cannot produce a report. No degraded draft.

  3. A reranker over the retrieved evidence.

  4. A drafter writing against a defined report schema and citing supporting source identifiers. The narrative follows Who, What, Where, How, Why, When, arranged as an inverted pyramid.

  5. A judge stage combining deterministic checks with an independent model judge over factuality, completeness, consistency and style.

  6. A coordinator that publishes to human review once quality clears the bar.

A designated compliance officer retains responsibility for the filing decision throughout. Review is risk-tiered: familiar low-risk typologies may get sampling or streamlined review, while novel, high-risk, multilingual or low-confidence cases default to full review. Human edits are themselves evaluated, so that a reviewer cannot quietly introduce an unsupported statement — a detail many designs forget.

Grounding

The design rule is to make it easier to state only what the evidence supports. In practice: the drafter cites source identifiers, retrieval runs over the knowledge graph and the operational stores, the drafter is expected to refuse and emit an insufficient-context signal rather than draft without evidence, reviewers see evidence beside each material claim, and provenance is kept for material statements and for reviewer edits.

What the article does not settle is granularity. It does not say whether citations attach per sentence, per claim or per schema field, and it does not say what happens to a single unsupported sentence in an otherwise grounded draft. For this domain that is the central mechanical question, and it is left open.

Evaluation

The dimensions are concrete and worth lifting: factual accuracy, factual completeness, name identification, timeframe of events, structural conformance to the inverted pyramid, articulation, coherence and tone. Scores are combined into a grade by a weighted or simple average.

Evals run at initial development, on requirement changes, on model updates, on a sample of production traffic to catch drift, and at every release. A golden dataset is built with edge cases and may be augmented with human feedback from production.

Two gaps matter. First, the article does not say whether a factuality failure blocks release regardless of the average — and in a regulatory filing it plainly must, since averaging a factual error against good tone is exactly the wrong arithmetic. Second, nothing is said about the golden set's size, labelling method, owner or maintenance cadence, which is where eval suites usually rot.

The judge, and what it means to check one

The article's control on the judge is narrower than it first appears, and it is worth being precise because this is the part most readers will assume is solved.

What it offers: freeze the judge's version, because the judge is itself non-deterministic, and pair it with a deterministic fact-checker for names, dates and amounts.

What it does not offer: any validation of the judge against human labels, any held-out set for the judge, or any calibration measurement. There is no procedure for establishing that the judge's scores correspond to what a reviewer would say.

Those are different things. Version pinning makes the judge stable; it does not make it right. A frozen judge with a systematic blind spot applies that blind spot consistently to every release, and a release gate built on it will pass the same class of defect forever. The deterministic fact-checker covers the entities most likely to be wrong and most cheaply verified, which is the right instinct, but names, dates and amounts are not where a narrative misrepresents a case. If you adopt this architecture, add judge validation yourself: a periodic sample scored by humans, agreement measured, and the judge's own version treated as a thing that must pass a gate before it is allowed to gate anything else.

Release gates

This is the strongest section, and it is the part worth copying into an actual runbook:

  • Version the model, the prompts, the retrieval logic, the data mappings, the policies and the eval set — each with an owner and a stated change rationale.

  • Test with deterministic factuality checks, rubric scoring, security and privacy checks, and jurisdiction-specific regression suites.

  • Gate on approval from engineering, compliance, risk and legal, scoped by change type.

  • Shadow and canary the candidate against the incumbent before raising its traffic share.

  • Monitor factuality pass rate, judge scores, reviewer edit rate, latency, queue age and escalation volume.

  • Roll back through configuration flags — to the previous model, to stricter review, or all the way to full human drafting — when thresholds are breached.

Two observations. The rollback ladder is the best idea in the article: the fallback is not a previous model version but more human involvement, ending at the pre-automation process. A system that can degrade to the thing it replaced is one you can actually switch off under regulatory pressure. And reviewer edit rate as a monitored signal is the right leading indicator — it moves before factuality failures do, because reviewers absorb the degradation first.

The gap: no thresholds are given anywhere, for any gate or any rollback trigger. The process is complete and the numbers that would make it operable are absent.

Failure modes to plan for

  • The judge's blind spot becomes policy. Pinning the judge's version makes its errors permanent and invisible until something external catches them.

  • Averaged rubrics launder factual errors. Any combined score lets a factuality miss be offset by style. Factuality needs a veto, not a weight.

  • Refuse-on-missing-data becomes a throughput problem. Stopping rather than drafting is correct, and it means a data outage shows up as a queue of unfiled reports against a deadline. The queue-age monitor exists for exactly this; someone has to own what happens when it grows.

  • Reviewers introduce the unsupported claim. Evaluating human edits is in the design; it is also the control most likely to be dropped as friction.

  • Risk-tiered sampling drifts. Streamlined review for "familiar typologies" is safe only as long as the classifier deciding what is familiar stays accurate.

  • Grounding at the wrong granularity. If a citation attaches to a paragraph rather than a claim, an ungrounded sentence travels inside a cited block.

  • Gate theatre. Four-party approval on every change type produces pressure to classify changes as minor. The change-type taxonomy is where this architecture would be quietly defeated.

Review checklist

  • Does a factuality failure block release on its own, independent of any aggregate score?

  • Has the judge been validated against human labels, and is that agreement re-measured on a schedule?

  • Is the judge's own version under the same gating discipline as the drafting model?

  • At what granularity does a citation attach, and what happens to one unsupported sentence?

  • Is there a written rollback ladder ending in the fully manual process, and has it been exercised?

  • Who owns the queue when the system correctly refuses to draft, and what is the deadline exposure?

  • Are human edits evaluated with the same rubric as model output?

  • Are eval-set size, provenance, ownership and refresh cadence recorded anywhere?

  • Which numeric thresholds trigger each gate and each rollback? If none are written down, the gates are advisory.

  • Who can reclassify a change as minor, and what does that skip?

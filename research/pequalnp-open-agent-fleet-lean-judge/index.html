What it is

A public, continuously running attempt by a coordinated fleet of AI agents to make progress on P vs NP. Anyone can connect their own agent with a one-line command. On 2026-11-24, the front page showed about 370 agents working, about 9,300 agent sessions so far, and 1,599 of 3,366 lemmas checked, on day 2 of the run. The site's own verdict line reads "open, being worked on now". The realistic output is not a resolution of P vs NP. It is a growing library of small, machine-checked lemmas and a test of how far open agent fleets can be coordinated.

Architecture

RoleJob
ProgramsLines of attack, each with a plain-language goal. Most aim at P ≠ NP; one searches for a fast algorithm (P = NP)
OrchestratorsHold a mandate inside a program, take lemmas, write. They do not decide results
Thinker agentsSearch and argue on paper first: try routes, hunt counterexamples, read the literature
Builder agentsTurn arguments that survive into Lean files for the checker
Red teamOrchestrators whose only job is counterexamples; a refutation counts like a proof
Barrier deskTests each new approach against known barriers (relativization, natural proofs, algebrization) and names the wall it hits; its rulings are opinions, not results
Lean checkerThe only judge. Builds each file in a pinned environment, re-verifies with the kernel, and accepts only an exact proof of the recorded statement with no gaps and only the standard axioms
HubWhere agents coordinate in public; message threads between seats are visible live

Results carry explicit states: checked (Lean accepted every step), conjecture or draft (stated but unchecked, drawn dashed), and a separate human-review state shown per result. A progress bar counts checked lemmas per target answer and is labeled as a count, not a probability.

Why it is interesting for agent builders

  • One objective judge makes an open fleet workable. Untrusted contributors and agents can join because nothing they say counts until the checker accepts it. That is the same move as test-gated CI for agent-written code, taken to its limit.

  • Separate roles for generation, refutation and barrier-checking. Red-team and barrier roles exist to stop the fleet wasting effort on routes known to fail, a structural answer to multi-agent conformity.

  • The site states its own limit plainly. The checker cannot tell whether a formal statement means what its English sentence says, so an orchestrator that did not write the statement reads it.

  • Thinking before formalizing. Cheap paper reasoning filters ideas before expensive Lean work, a compute-allocation pattern.

Failure modes to watch

  • Lemma inflation. Thousands of checked lemmas can be true and useless. Count only lemmas on a path to a program's goal.

  • Statement drift. A checked lemma whose formal statement is weaker than its description reads as progress.

  • Barrier blindness. Approaches that relativize or are natural proofs cannot settle P vs NP; the barrier desk's rulings are not machine-checked.

  • Open-participation risks. Spam, duplicated effort and adversarial contributions; the checker blocks false results but not wasted compute.

  • Sponsorship optics. The site takes payments and sponsor placement; sponsors don't affect results, but that is a reason to read claims carefully.

Review checklist for a fleet-built formal result

  • Is the Lean statement independently read against its plain-language claim?

  • Is the lemma on a path to a program goal, or a side result?

  • Has the approach been tested against the known barriers?

  • Is the result reproducible in the pinned environment?

  • Has a human expert reviewed it, and does the site show that state?

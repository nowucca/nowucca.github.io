SRE-1: an RL-trained 27B model for production incident investigation

Read the original

Source: ResolveAI Labs, "SRE-1: Dominating the Pareto Frontier of Production Investigations with a 27B Model", 2026-11-18 (landing page, paper). Lead researchers Sean Bell and Dhruv Mahajan, with nine co-authors. No weights, code or public benchmark released.

TL;DR

ResolveAI took open Qwen3.8-27B and ran 200 steps of RL on real production incidents, with more than 400 live tools across about 60 integrations, all pinned to the alert's timestamp. The resulting model, SRE-1, produces a root-cause chain ("A caused B, which caused C") with cited evidence. On 49 live production cases it goes from 6.9% (base) to 38.8% pass for a single investigation; an ensemble of five reaches 71%, matching Fable 5.2 at 37% less cost. On 73 offline problems it edges Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 at xhigh effort on pass rate (51.7% vs 44.5% and 39.0%). The most transferable result is not the model but the ablation table: harness details (tool-output caps, reserving room for the final answer, sampling temperature) moved the score far more than reward tweaks, and a bf16 gradient-accumulation bug beat every reward change they tried that month.

This is the technical follow-through on the multi-agent position piece filed as R031, and it lands somewhere unexpected: the shipped artifact is a single agent, and the multi-agent system shows up as the reference-chain labeler.

What the model does

  • Task: given an alert, investigate across the customer's observability, deploy, code and infra tools and output an ordered causal chain with evidence citations. Single pass, single agent, no sub-agents.

  • Grading: an LLM judge scores the chain against a reference chain on a continuous scale; a score of 0.8 or more is a pass.

  • Reference chains: produced by "a team of frontier-model agents" that builds the chain from raw evidence and audits each link against primary records. Expert SREs rated its chain as good as or better than an expert's on 26 of 27 incidents. Internal agent roles are not described.

Leakage controlEvery tool returns data as of alert time. They caught an agent that found a later commit hash and "retrieved the fix for its own incident."
RewardDominated by the judge score. One judge drawn at random per group from 13 (several providers and effort levels) to blunt reward hacking. Smaller credit reductions for exceeding the 10-calls-per-turn limit, repetition, and malformed or wrong-language tokens, applied to the offending turns or spans only (dense credit).
Harness randomizationHalf of rollout groups use the eval harness unchanged; the other half randomize about 70 attributes (tool interface, budgets and whether they are stated, prompt form, tool names and schemas), over 1,050 combinations.
CurriculumPrompts all or none of the group solve are parked and resampled later (half-lives about 245 steps for easy, 25 for hard).
Context262k training window; 1M at inference via 4x YaRN.

Behavioral pathologies before the penalties were real: up to 24% of rollouts broke the ten-call limit (one issued 1,458 calls in a single turn) and up to 12% degenerated into repetition. The targeted mistakes became 12 to 343 times rarer.

The top three are harness and sampling choices, not RL algorithm choices. The expert-reference row is the quiet headline for anyone building labeling pipelines: an audited multi-agent labeler was as good as SRE experts for training signal.

Assessment for a staff engineer

What is credible. The method is careful in the ways that usually go wrong: time-pinned tools against real data, explicit leakage and decontamination filtering with counts, random judge per group, CIs on everything, a noise floor for ablations, and the authors' own admission that the live test pool over-represents cases their frontier-backed production system scored low on. The ablation table is unusually honest and useful.

What to discount.

  • Single vendor, private evals, no release. All three test sets are internal; no weights, no benchmark, no third-party rerun. The title's "dominating the Pareto frontier" rests on 49 live cases.

  • Selection bias favors SRE-1. The live pool skews toward alerts the frontier-backed product did badly on, which tilts the comparison against Opus and Fable.

  • Judge-defined success. Pass means an LLM judge scored 0.8+ against an agent-built reference. Thirteen judges reduce hacking but share frontier-model priors with the reference builder.

  • Uneven baselines. Opus ran 8 rollouts per offline problem, Fable only 2; frontier models ran in ResolveAI's harness, which SRE-1 was trained on and they were not.

  • Mean scores are close. 0.749 vs 0.743 vs 0.731 on offline synthesis; the gap opens only at the pass threshold.

  • Narrow task. Root cause only, single pass. Remediation, communication and multi-turn work with a human are out of scope. Ensembles that get to 71% are slower, which matters mid-incident.

  • No multiple-comparison correction on the CIs; the 1,503 vs 1,549 incident count is unexplained.

Read against R031. A year ago Resolve argued only an orchestrator over parallel domain specialists could do incident investigation. SRE-1 is a single agent with a 1M window that reaches frontier ensemble quality; the multi-agent system survives as the offline labeler. That is a useful data point for the "distill the swarm into one trained agent" pattern, and a reason to be skeptical of architecture claims made before the training numbers exist.

Failure modes to expect if you deploy something like this

  • Time leakage in any environment where tools can see the future (git log, ticket updates, postmortems). They hit it; you will too.

  • Tool-call pathologies under RL: call storms, repetition, wrong-language tokens. Budget penalties need to be turn-local, not trajectory-wide (the budget-relative penalty hurt).

  • Context blowout from verbose tools; a hard per-result cap is the single biggest lever.

  • Harness overfit: a model RL-tuned in one harness degrades when tool names, schemas or budgets change, hence the 50% randomization. Expect drift whenever an integration changes its API.

  • Silent numerics bugs in the trainer that look like "RL is noisy."

Review checklist (for a similar domain-RL project)

  • Are all tool responses pinned to the event time, and do you have a leakage audit with a count of what you dropped?

  • Are reference labels audited against primary records, and have you compared them against expert labels on a sample?

  • Is the judge randomized or ensembled, and have you measured judge agreement with a blind reference?

  • What is the eval noise floor (same weights, two runs)?

  • Are tool outputs capped and is room reserved for the final answer?

  • Is gradient accumulation and master-weight storage in fp32?

  • Is a fraction of training run with a randomized harness?

  • Are frontier baselines run with matched rollout counts and in a harness they were not trained on either?

  • Does the test pool selection favor your model, and have you said so?

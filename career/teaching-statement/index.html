Teaching Statement

Steven Atkinson, Ph.D.

I teach software engineering the way it is practised: with real tools, real constraints, and now, AI as an everyday collaborator. For nine years I taught graduate computer science at Virginia Tech as an adjunct while working as a senior engineer at Netflix. That combination shapes everything I do in the classroom. I bring students the problems engineers actually face, and I hold them to the standards a production team would.

Teaching AI as an engineering discipline

In 2023 I designed AI Tools for Software Engineering, a graduate course that began as a special topics offering (CS 5914) and became a permanent course (CS 5740) in 2025. It grew from 52 students in its first term to 98 in Fall 2025.

The course treats large language models as components to be engineered, not as magic. Students start by building a reproducible Docker development environment and connecting to a class LLM that I operate through a proxy I wrote, so that every student has the same model and nobody needs a personal API key. From there, a sequence of projects builds on one another:

  • a coding assistant (“Ducky”) with code review, debugging and editing features;
  • retrieval-augmented generation over a project’s own documents;
  • using LLMs for requirements and for test generation;
  • autonomous agents with a command-line interface, and tools exposed through the Model Context Protocol (MCP);
  • a closing module on the social and ethical impact of AI.

A question students bring to this course is how to get dependable results from a non-deterministic tool. By the end they can answer it: by managing context, specifying requirements precisely, testing outputs, and knowing when not to trust the model. One student wrote, “Even though we used AI tools, which were a big part of the course, I still felt I was learning, thinking critically, and seeing how these tools really affect software development.” That balance is exactly what I aim for.

Projects first, then concepts

Students learn software engineering by building software. In Internet Software (CS 5244), I directed the curriculum and model projects for 14-week web and Android courses built on five layered, real-world patterns, so each project adds one idea to a working system the students already understand. I carry the same structure into my AI course. Short recorded demonstrations show each technique in action, and weekly live Q&A sessions let me answer questions and connect the material to what I see in industry.

Feedback and continuous improvement

I read my evaluations closely and change the course every term. Across ten sections at Virginia Tech, students rated my overall teaching effectiveness between 5.46 and 5.80 out of 6, above the department mean in every section. I am just as attentive to the criticism. In Fall 2025, students told me the Docker images and multi-repository setup cost them time that should have gone to learning. For my next offering I would ship lighter images, consolidate the starter repositories, and state storage requirements in the syllabus. Students also asked for spec-driven development workflows and a project in which they build their own MCP server, and I plan to add both.

When things break, I treat it as a teaching moment and fix it quickly. Our class LLM failed in the middle of Fall 2025; I diagnosed the problem, restored service and adjusted the deliverables and deadlines. A student noted that in the evaluation, and I think it showed the class more about running real systems than any lecture could.

Inclusive, respectful classrooms

My students are Master’s students with very different situations: in my early years most were working professionals studying online, and in the AI course most are full-time students. I design for both: recorded content they can watch on their own schedule, clear rubrics, prompt responses on the course forum, and flexibility when life intervenes. In Fall 2025, students rated me 5.78 out of 6 for fostering an atmosphere of mutual respect, and I want every student, whatever their background, to leave confident that they can learn new technology on their own.

What I would bring to UT Austin

I can teach introductory programming and data structures, software engineering, web and mobile application development, and AI courses at both undergraduate and graduate level. I would especially like to develop and teach courses on AI-assisted software development, a subject where UT Austin students will benefit from instruction that is current and grounded in industry practice. I also want to mentor students on capstone projects and help colleagues integrate AI tools responsibly into their own courses.
